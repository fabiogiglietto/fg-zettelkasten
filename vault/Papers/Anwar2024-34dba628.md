# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed articles (2016–2023) that examine how users engage with Facebook's Reactions feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), introduced in 2016 as an expansion of the Like button. Organizing the corpus into three thematic clusters — introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues — the authors argue that Reactions function as paralinguistic digital affordances that convey emotional nuance at low cognitive cost, and that their distributions vary systematically by content type. A recurring finding across studies is that negatively valenced content, especially anger-eliciting political and populist messaging, drives disproportionately high engagement, while lifestyle and entertainment content skews toward positive reactions.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research across politics, health, business, and social-issue domains.
- A thematic taxonomy organizing a fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into coherent categories.
- Identification of cross-study regularities, most notably the amplifying role of negative emotion (particularly anger) in political engagement.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: fragmented data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, and underexplored event-based content.
- Agenda-setting suggestions, including LLM-based categorization of reaction patterns and applications to misinformation and digital activism.

## Methods

The authors follow Briner and Denyer's (2012) five-step systematic review protocol: formulating research questions, locating studies, selecting and evaluating them, analyzing findings, and reporting. Searches in Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines (July–September 2023) using "Facebook" + "Reaction" combinations yielded 64 peer-reviewed articles and conference papers. Each article was thematically coded via Armstrong's (2010) comparative naturalistic method by research question, results, and conclusions, then grouped into three macro-categories with finer sub-topics (political news, populism, healthcare, business, emotion detection, etc.).

## Findings

- Of the 64 articles: 6 concern the introduction of Reactions, 19 politics and far-right groups, and 39 other social issues.
- In electoral contexts (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament), anger-based appeals generated substantially more engagement and sharing than positive appeals.
- Populist and far-right leaders disproportionately elicit *Angry* and *Haha* reactions relative to mainstream politicians; anger-triggering communication amplifies sharing.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news produce a bivalent logic: *Love* and *Anger* rarely co-occur, while *Anger* clusters with *Sad* and *Wow*.
- Reactions have been used computationally to predict emotion classes, sentiment, and controversy (via entropy of reaction distributions), though reaction-to-emotion mappings remain ambiguous — *Haha* and *Wow* can signal derision or disbelief in political contexts.
- Reactions serve as useful sentiment labels for low-resource languages (Sinhala, Bangla).
- Lifestyle/entertainment posts skew positive; sociopolitical posts draw a wider and more negative emotional spectrum.
- Cultural framing matters, as evidenced by divergent reaction patterns across pro-China, China-critical, and neutral outlets in Hong Kong.

## Connections

This review consolidates the empirical basis for treating Reactions as low-cost affective signals in studies of political communication and coordinated amplification, connecting directly to work on Italian electoral and hyperpartisan dynamics such as [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], where reaction-based engagement metrics inform detection of problematic information flows. Its emphasis on anger-driven engagement with populist and far-right content resonates with [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] on affective political discourse on Facebook, and with broader platform-level analyses of engagement asymmetries such as [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]. The methodological reflections on fragmented data sources also speak to ongoing debates about Facebook data access reflected in [[F2020-6278a4aa]].
