# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed articles published between 2016 and 2023 examining user engagement with Facebook's "Reactions" feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), introduced in February 2016 as an expansion of the "Like" button. The authors frame Reactions as paralinguistic digital affordances (PDA) — lightweight affective cues that convey nuanced emotional meaning at low cognitive cost. Organizing the fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into three thematic clusters (introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues), they conduct a meta-analysis of cross-study patterns. The central finding is that emotionally charged, especially negative, content drives higher engagement — with anger reactions consistently amplifying political and populist communication — while lifestyle and entertainment content skews positive.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research (2016–2023) across politics, social issues, health, and business domains.
- A thematic taxonomy that organizes a fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into coherent categories.
- Identification of cross-study regularities, notably the role of negative emotion in driving engagement with political and populist content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: no unified data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, underexplored event-based/tabloid topics.
- Suggested future directions, including LLMs for categorizing reaction patterns and applications to misinformation, fact-checking, and digital activism.

## Methods

A five-step systematic review process (Briner and Denyer, 2012): formulating research questions, finding studies, selecting and evaluating them, analyzing findings, and reporting. Monthly database searches (Scopus, Google Scholar, others) from July–September 2023 combined "Facebook" and "Reaction" with Boolean operators. Inclusion was restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings (2016–2023), yielding 64 articles. A comparative naturalistic method (Armstrong, 2010) was used to summarize and thematically code each article by research question, results, and conclusions, grouping them into three major categories with finer sub-topics (political news, populism, healthcare, business, emotion detection).

## Findings

- Of 64 articles: 6 on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics and far-right groups, 39 on other social issues.
- In political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament elections), negative appeals — especially anger — generated significantly more engagement and sharing than positive content.
- Populist and far-right leaders elicit disproportionately more "angry" and "haha" reactions than mainstream politicians; anger-triggering communication boosts sharing.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news display a bivalent emotional logic: "love" and "anger" rarely co-occur, while "anger" co-occurs with "sad" and "wow."
- Reactions have been used computationally to predict emotion classes, controversy (via entropy of reaction distributions), and sentiment, though reaction-to-emotion mappings remain ambiguous ("Wow" as disbelief, "Haha" as derision).
- Cross-linguistic studies (Sinhala, Bangla) show Reactions' utility as sentiment labels for low-resource languages.
- Lifestyle/entertainment posts skew positive; sociopolitical posts attract a wider, more negative spectrum. Cultural context shapes patterns (e.g., Hong Kong media audiences).

## Connections

As a review of platform-mediated affective engagement, this note connects to work on the amplification of negative and moral-emotional content in political communication, such as [[Brady2026-ln]], and to broader debates on how platform affordances shape the diffusion and valence of political and junk news, relevant to [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Allcott2017-yz]]. Its focus on populist and far-right engagement via Facebook signals also situates it alongside platform-engagement studies like [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]].
