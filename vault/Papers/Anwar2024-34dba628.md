# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This paper is a systematic literature review synthesizing 64 peer-reviewed studies (2016–2023) on Facebook's Reactions feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry) since its 2016 rollout. Framing Reactions as paralinguistic digital affordances that convey affective nuance at low cognitive cost, the authors organize the fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into three thematic clusters—the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues—and extract cross-study regularities. The central argument is that emotionally charged content, especially negatively valenced political and populist messaging, systematically drives higher engagement, while lifestyle and entertainment content skews positive; Reactions thus offer a versatile but methodologically inconsistent proxy for sentiment, controversy, and audience emotional response.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research spanning politics, health, business, and social issues.
- A thematic taxonomy organizing a heterogeneous literature into coherent categories.
- Identification of cross-study regularities, notably the outsized role of anger in engagement with populist and far-right content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: inconsistent data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, underexplored event-based/tabloid content.
- Research agenda proposing LLM-based approaches to categorizing reaction patterns and applications to misinformation and digital activism.

## Methods

Five-step systematic review procedure (Briner and Denyer, 2012): question formulation, study retrieval, selection/evaluation, analysis, and reporting. Searches were run in Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines from July–September 2023 using Boolean combinations of "Facebook" and "Reaction." Inclusion was restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings from 2016–2023, yielding 64 items. Each article was coded via a comparative naturalistic reading (Armstrong, 2010) by research question, results, and conclusions, then grouped into three top-level categories with finer sub-topics.

## Findings

- Of 64 articles: 6 on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics/far-right groups, 39 on other social issues.
- In political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament), anger-laden appeals generated significantly more engagement and sharing than positive content.
- Populist and far-right leaders disproportionately elicit Angry and Haha reactions relative to mainstream politicians.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news show a bivalent emotional logic: Love and Angry rarely co-occur, while Angry co-occurs with Sad and Wow.
- Reactions can predict emotion class, controversy (via entropy of reaction distributions), and sentiment—though reaction-to-emotion mappings are sometimes ambiguous (e.g., Wow as disbelief, Haha as mockery).
- Reactions serve as effective sentiment labels in low-resource languages (Sinhala, Bangla).
- Lifestyle/entertainment posts skew positive; sociopolitical posts elicit a broader, more negative spectrum.
- Cultural context shapes reaction patterns (e.g., China-critical vs. China-supporting Hong Kong audiences).

## Connections

This review consolidates the empirical basis for using Facebook Reactions as engagement and affect signals, which underpins a substantial body of work on coordinated behavior, junk news, and political communication on Facebook — most directly the CooRnet/Giglietto line of research including [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], and [[F2020-6278a4aa]], where reaction distributions inform measures of problematic information and audience response. It also connects to studies of platform-mediated political engagement and populist affect such as [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], and provides methodological grounding for computational uses of reaction data explored in works like [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]].
