# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed articles published between 2016 and 2023 that examine user engagement with Facebook's Reactions feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry). Anwar and Giglietto organize the fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into three thematic clusters — the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues — and derive cross-study regularities. The central argument is that Reactions function as paralinguistic digital affordances that provide nuanced, low-cost emotional signals, and that sociopolitical content (especially from populist and far-right actors) systematically elicits broader, more negative emotional profiles, with anger consistently driving higher engagement and sharing.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research spanning 2016–2023 across politics, health, business, and social issues.
- A thematic taxonomy that organizes a scattered interdisciplinary literature into coherent categories.
- Identification of cross-study regularities, notably the role of negative emotion in driving engagement with political and populist content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: absence of unified data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, and underexploration of event-based topics.
- Agenda-setting suggestions, including using LLMs to categorize reaction patterns and applying Reactions research to misinformation and digital activism studies.

## Methods

The authors follow the five-step systematic review procedure of Briner and Denyer (2012). Monthly searches in Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines from July to September 2023 combined "Facebook" and "Reaction" with Boolean operators. Inclusion was restricted to peer-reviewed articles and conference proceedings from 2016–2023, yielding 64 studies. Each was coded thematically using Armstrong's (2010) comparative naturalistic method, then grouped into (A) Introduction of Reactions, (B) Politics and Far-right Groups, and (C) Other Social Issues, with finer sub-topics.

## Findings

- Of 64 articles, 6 addressed the introduction of Reactions, 19 covered politics and far-right groups, and 39 examined other social issues.
- In political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament elections), negative emotional appeals — especially anger — generated significantly more engagement and sharing than positive content.
- Populist and far-right leaders receive disproportionately more Angry and Haha reactions than mainstream politicians; anger-triggering posts are shared more.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news follow a bivalent emotional logic: Love and Anger rarely co-occur, while Anger tends to co-occur with Sad and Wow.
- Reactions have been used to predict emotion classes, controversy (via entropy of reaction distributions), and sentiment, though reaction-to-emotion mappings can be ambiguous — Haha and Wow are context-dependent.
- Cross-linguistic studies (Sinhala, Bangla) demonstrate Reactions' value as labels for sentiment analysis in low-resource languages.
- Lifestyle/entertainment content skews positive; sociopolitical content draws a wider, more negative spectrum.
- Cultural context matters, as evidenced by comparisons of China-critical, China-supporting, and neutral media audiences in Hong Kong.

## Connections

This review consolidates a body of work in which Giglietto and collaborators have been central, and it connects directly to their studies of engagement, junk news, and coordinated behavior on Facebook, such as [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], and the earlier work on Facebook reactions and news in [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]. Its findings on anger-driven engagement with populist and far-right content resonate with broader research on platform-mediated political communication and hyperpartisan news ecosystems represented in this register.
