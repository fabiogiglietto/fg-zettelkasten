# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed studies (2016–2023) that use Facebook's "Reactions" feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry) as either an object of inquiry or a measurement instrument. Organizing the fragmented literature into three thematic clusters — the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues — the authors argue that Reactions function as paralinguistic digital affordances that convey nuanced affect at low cognitive cost, and that their distributions systematically differ across content types. The central empirical regularity is that sociopolitical and populist content elicits broader and more negative emotional profiles, with anger in particular driving heightened engagement and sharing.

## Key Contributions

- First systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research across politics, health, business, and psychological domains for 2016–2023.
- A thematic taxonomy that imposes order on a highly interdisciplinary and methodologically heterogeneous body of work.
- Identification of a cross-study regularity: negative affect, especially anger, disproportionately drives engagement with political and populist content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps — inconsistent data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, and underexploration of event-driven or tabloid content.
- A research agenda flagging LLM-based classification of reaction patterns and applications to misinformation, fact-checking, and digital activism.

## Methods

The authors follow Briner and Denyer's (2012) five-step systematic review protocol: formulating research questions, retrieving studies via Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines (July–September 2023, using combinations of "Facebook" and "Reaction" with Boolean operators), applying inclusion criteria (peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings, 2016–2023), and thematically coding the resulting 64 articles. Coding uses Armstrong's (2010) comparative naturalistic approach, grouping articles by research question, findings, and conclusions, then organizing them into three top-level categories with finer sub-topics (political news, populism, healthcare, business, emotion detection).

## Findings

- Of 64 articles: 6 on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics/far-right groups, 39 on other social issues.
- Negative appeals — especially anger — outperform positive content in political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament elections) in engagement and sharing.
- Populist and far-right leaders receive disproportionately more Angry and Haha reactions than mainstream politicians.
- Junk/hyperpartisan news shows a bivalent structure: Love and Anger rarely co-occur, while Anger clusters with Sad and Wow.
- Reactions have been used to predict emotion classes, sentiment, and controversy (via entropy over reaction distributions), though reaction-to-emotion mappings are ambiguous.
- In low-resource languages (Sinhala, Bangla), Reactions serve as effective sentiment labels.
- Lifestyle/entertainment content skews positive; sociopolitical content elicits a wider, more negative spectrum.
- Cultural context matters — e.g., divergent reaction patterns across China-critical, China-supporting, and neutral Hong Kong media audiences.

## Connections

This review directly intersects with the strand of work using Facebook engagement metrics to study political information flows and problematic content, especially the CooRnet-adjacent research on coordinated link sharing and junk news dynamics on Facebook — see [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], and [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]]. Its emphasis on negative affect and populist mobilization also connects to broader analyses of platform-mediated political communication and hyperpartisan discourse, such as [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Larsson2026-ro]], while the methodological discussion of Reactions as measurement instruments speaks to work on the constraints and repurposing of platform data traces like [[Rieder2025-ju]].
