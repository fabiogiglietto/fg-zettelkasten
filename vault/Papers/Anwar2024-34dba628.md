# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *FRONTIERS IN SOCIOLOGY*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed articles (2016–2023) on Facebook Reactions (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), organizing them into three thematic clusters: the introduction of Reactions as a platform feature, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues (health, business, psychological). The authors argue that Reactions function as paralinguistic digital affordances that convey nuanced emotional meaning while retaining the low-effort character of the Like button, and that they have become a valuable proxy for measuring sentiment, controversy, and audience response. A central cross-study regularity is that negatively valenced emotional content — particularly anger — consistently drives higher engagement in political communication, especially for populist and far-right actors, while lifestyle and entertainment content elicits predominantly positive reactions.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research from 2016 to 2023 across political, social, health, and business domains.
- A thematic taxonomy that organizes a fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into coherent categories.
- Cross-study identification of the "anger drives engagement" pattern in political and populist content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: absence of unified data sources, weak cross-cultural comparability, and underexploration of event-based topics.
- Research agenda pointing to LLM-based reaction categorization and applications in misinformation, fact-checking, and digital activism.

## Methods

The authors follow Briner and Denyer's (2012) five-step systematic review protocol: formulating research questions, retrieving studies, screening, thematic analysis, and reporting. Database searches (Scopus, Google Scholar) were run monthly from July to September 2023 using combinations of "Facebook" and "Reaction" with Boolean operators, restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings from 2016–2023. Articles were coded using Armstrong's (2010) comparative naturalistic method by research question, results, and conclusions, then grouped into three top-level categories with finer sub-topics (political news, populism, healthcare, business, emotion detection).

## Findings

- Of 64 articles: 6 on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics/far-right, 39 on other social issues.
- Negative emotional appeals — especially anger — drive significantly more engagement and sharing in political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament elections).
- Populist and far-right leaders disproportionately elicit "angry" and "haha" reactions relative to mainstream politicians.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news show a bivalent affective structure: "love" and "anger" rarely co-occur; "anger" clusters with "sad" and "wow".
- Reactions have been used to predict emotion classes, measure controversy via entropy of reaction distributions, and label sentiment — though reaction-to-emotion mappings can be ambiguous (e.g., "Wow" as disbelief, "Haha" as mockery).
- Reactions provide useful sentiment labels for low-resource languages (Sinhala, Bangla).
- Lifestyle/entertainment posts skew positive; sociopolitical posts draw a broader, more negative emotional spectrum.
- Cultural context matters: reaction patterns differ across China-critical, China-supporting, and neutral audiences in Hong Kong.

## Connections

This review sits at the intersection of computational political communication and platform-affordance research, and it maps directly onto a body of work by one of its co-authors on Facebook engagement metrics, junk news, and coordinated behavior — see [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]], which develop related empirical approaches to reactions, engagement, and problematic information flows on Facebook. Its findings about affective asymmetries and negative-emotion-driven engagement in political content resonate with broader debates on platform-shaped political discourse addressed in [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Allcott2025-jb]], while its call for LLM-based categorization of reaction patterns connects to methodological work such as [[Le-Mens2025-qz]].
