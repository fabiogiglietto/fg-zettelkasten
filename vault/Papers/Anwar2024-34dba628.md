# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed studies (2016–2023) on Facebook's "Reactions" feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), introduced in 2016 as an emotional expansion of the "Like" button. Anwar and Giglietto organize the fragmented literature into three thematic clusters — the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues (health, business, psychological/emotional) — and conduct a cross-study meta-analysis. Their central argument is that Reactions operate as *paralinguistic digital affordances*: low-effort cues that nonetheless carry rich affective signal, with distinct valence patterns across content types. Sociopolitical content, especially from populist and far-right actors, consistently attracts broader and more negatively skewed reaction profiles, and emotionally charged posts (particularly anger-inducing ones) drive higher engagement regardless of valence.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research across the 2016–2023 window.
- A thematic taxonomy organizing an interdisciplinary and fragmented literature into coherent categories.
- Identification of cross-study regularities, especially the pivotal role of negative emotion (anger) in driving engagement with populist and far-right content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: inconsistent data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, and underexploration of event-based/tabloid content.
- Roadmap for future work using LLMs to classify reaction patterns and to link Reactions research to misinformation, fact-checking, and digital activism.

## Methods

Five-step systematic review protocol following Briner and Denyer (2012): formulating research questions, searching, selecting and evaluating studies, analyzing, and reporting. Searches were conducted in Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines (July–September 2023) using "Facebook" and "Reaction" combined with topical keywords and Boolean operators. Inclusion was restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings (2016–2023), yielding 64 items. Articles were coded thematically using Armstrong's (2010) comparative naturalistic method and grouped into three major categories with finer sub-topics.

## Findings

- Distribution of the corpus: 6 articles on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics and far-right groups, 39 on other social issues.
- In political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament elections), negative emotional appeals — especially anger — outperformed positive content in engagement and sharing.
- Populist and far-right leaders receive disproportionately more "Angry" and "Haha" reactions than mainstream politicians; anger boosts sharing.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news content exhibits a bivalent emotional logic: "Love" and "Anger" rarely co-occur; "Anger" clusters with "Sad" and "Wow".
- Reactions have been productively used to predict emotion classes, measure controversy via entropy of reaction distributions, and label sentiment — including in low-resource languages (Sinhala, Bangla).
- "Love" and "Haha" typically read as positive, "Sad" and "Angry" as negative, but "Haha" and "Wow" can flip in political contexts (e.g., "Wow" as disbelief, "Haha" as mockery).
- Lifestyle/entertainment posts skew positive; sociopolitical posts elicit a broader and more negative spectrum.
- Cultural context matters: Hong Kong audiences of China-critical, China-supporting, and neutral outlets show distinct reaction profiles.

## Connections

This review sits at the intersection of political communication, affective engagement, and platform studies, and provides a useful backdrop for empirical work using Reactions as engagement or sentiment signal. It resonates directly with [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] on Facebook-based political content circulation and junk news, and with populism-and-emotion work such as [[Gattermann2025-yx]] and [[Knupfer2025-vt]]. The finding that anger drives sharing of populist and far-right messaging also connects to broader engagement-and-affect research including [[Brady2026-ln]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]].
