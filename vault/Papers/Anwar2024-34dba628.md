# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed studies published between 2016 and 2023 on Facebook's Reactions feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), introduced in 2016 as an expansion of the Like button. The authors argue that Reactions constitute paralinguistic digital affordances that convey nuanced affect at low cognitive cost, and that their distribution across posts offers a valuable window into user emotion, controversy, and engagement dynamics. Organizing the literature into three clusters — introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues — the review finds that emotionally charged content drives higher engagement regardless of valence, that political and populist content disproportionately triggers negative reactions (notably Anger), and that lifestyle/entertainment content skews positive.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research spanning 2016–2023.
- A thematic taxonomy organizing a fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into coherent categories.
- Identification of cross-study regularities, particularly the role of negative emotion in driving engagement with populist and far-right content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: absence of unified data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, and underexplored event-based/tabloid topics.
- Suggested future directions, including LLM-based reaction categorization and applications in misinformation and digital activism research.

## Methods

The authors follow Briner and Denyer's (2012) five-step systematic review process: formulating research questions, finding studies, selecting and evaluating them, analyzing findings, and reporting. Searches were conducted in Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines from July to September 2023, using combinations of "Facebook" and "Reaction" with Boolean operators. After applying inclusion criteria (peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings, 2016–2023), 64 articles were retained and analyzed using Armstrong's (2010) comparative naturalistic method, then grouped into three major categories with finer sub-topics.

## Findings

- Of 64 articles, 6 addressed the introduction of Reactions, 19 politics and far-right groups, and 39 other social issues.
- In political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament), negative appeals — especially anger — generated significantly more engagement and sharing than positive content.
- Populist and far-right leaders elicit disproportionately more Angry and Haha reactions than mainstream politicians.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news show a bivalent emotional logic: Love and Anger rarely co-occur, while Anger co-occurs with Sad and Wow.
- Reactions have been used to predict emotion classes, measure controversy via entropy of reaction distributions, and label sentiment, though reaction-to-emotion mappings remain ambiguous (e.g., Wow as disbelief, Haha as mockery).
- Reactions serve as effective sentiment labels in low-resource languages (Sinhala, Bangla).
- Lifestyle/entertainment posts skew positive; sociopolitical posts elicit broader and more negative emotional profiles.
- Cultural context shapes reaction patterns (e.g., China-critical vs. China-supporting media audiences in Hong Kong).

## Connections

This review consolidates a strand of work on emotional engagement and platform affordances that intersects with studies of coordinated and problematic information sharing on Facebook, such as [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], and [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], which similarly leverage Facebook engagement metrics to study political communication and junk news. Its emphasis on Reactions as computational signals for sentiment, controversy, and emotion also connects to broader efforts to measure political affect and mobilization on social platforms, including [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]]. The methodological reflection on fragmented data sources resonates with contemporary concerns about platform data access raised in [[Rieder2025-ju]].
