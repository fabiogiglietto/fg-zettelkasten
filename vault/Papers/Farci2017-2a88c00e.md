# Networked intimacy. Intimacy and friendship among Italian Facebook users

> [!note] Corrected
> - Correction (2016-07-19): [10.1080/1369118x.2016.1211636](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118x.2016.1211636)

> Farci, M., Rossi, L., Artieri, G. B., & Giglietto, F. (2017). Networked intimacy. Intimacy and friendship among Italian Facebook users. *Information, Communication & Society*. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2016.1203970
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2016.1203970)

## Summary

This paper advances the concept of **"networked intimacy"** to describe how intimacy on Facebook is achieved not through solitary private self-disclosure but through public, collaborative practices that depend on the cooperation of one's friendship network. Drawing on a qualitative study of Italian Facebook users, the authors argue that intimate relationships are negotiated in semi-public symbolic spaces where meaning is encoded for particular audiences and co-constructed with friends. The paper explicitly positions itself against narcissistic readings of social media intimacy (Turkle, Lasch, Bauman), reframing public disclosure as a relational, community-oriented resource for maintaining friendship and achieving social inclusion. In doing so, it connects sociological theories of the "pure relationship" and friendship with platform studies, affordance theory, and research on networked publics.

## Key Contributions

- Proposes **"networked intimacy"** as a theoretical framework synthesizing and extending prior work on Facebook self-disclosure.
- Identifies and operationalizes **five collaborative disclosure strategies**, newly adding Tagging and Liking to previously discussed categories.
- Broadens the geographic scope of SNS intimacy research beyond predominantly Anglo-American samples, with empirical evidence from the Italian context.
- Reframes public intimacy as relational and community-building rather than narcissistic self-promotion.
- Bridges sociological theories of pure relationships and friendship (Giddens, Jamieson, Pahl) with contemporary affordance-based platform studies.

## Methods

A qualitative study based on 118 semi-structured interviews with Italian Facebook users (aged 13–54), conducted in 2013 as part of a national PRIN-funded multi-year project. Recruitment used guided snowball sampling — drawing subsequent participants from a prior interviewee's Facebook contacts — stratified by age and gender quotas aligned with Italian Facebook penetration data. Interviews averaged 45 minutes and took place in participants' homes while they browsed their own profiles. Analysis followed Strauss and Corbin's Grounded Theory, moving through open, axial, and selective coding (~400 initial codes down to broader categories) using Dedoose.

## Findings

- Participants' chief concern was managing visibility and self-presentation while avoiding undesired disclosure consequences.
- **Showing rather than telling** — preferring images and visual cues legible only to intended audiences.
- **Sharing implicit content** — quotes, lyrics, and cryptic posts to encode messages for select friends (related to but distinct from social steganography).
- **Tagging** — publicly marking exclusive relational events and directing content to specific individuals while keeping the post public.
- **Expectation of mutual understanding** — assuming close friends will recognize targeted content without explicit cues, a tacit relational competence.
- **Liking** — a low-cost signal of relational proximity and "thinking of you" that reinforces intimacy with minimal effort.
- Together these practices create ephemeral, semi-public symbolic spaces where intimate audiences are continually reconstituted.
- Notably, participants did not spontaneously link the word "intimacy" to Facebook, but described practices fitting the concept once prompted.

## Connections

This paper's account of audience-targeted encoding and collaborative visibility management resonates with work on how users imagine and manage their networked audiences under platform conditions, connecting to [[Marwick2025-ov]] on context and the collapse of distinct audiences. Its focus on how affordances like tagging and liking shape relational signalling links to research on platform legibility and the algorithmic imaginary in [[Bucher2017-qh]] and [[Cotter2019-js]]. The strongest ties are conceptual rather than methodological, as most other papers under this topic address authenticity and performance in different platform or creator contexts.
