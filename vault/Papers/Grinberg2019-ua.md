# Fake news on Twitter during the 2016 U.S. presidential election

> Grinberg, N., Joseph, K., Friedland, L., Swire-Thompson, B., & Lazer, D. (2019). Fake news on Twitter during the 2016 U.S. presidential election. *Science*, *363*, 374–378. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aau2706
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aau2706)

## Summary

This study measures the spread and consumption of fake news on Twitter during the 2016 U.S. presidential election by linking Twitter accounts to registered voter records. By characterizing individual-level exposure and sharing, the paper pushes back against inflated impressions of fake news prevalence, arguing instead that both exposure to and dissemination of fake news were highly concentrated among a small subset of users. Fake news represented only a modest fraction of overall political news consumption on the platform, situating the phenomenon within a broader, more nuanced debate about the reach and impact of misinformation online.

## Key Contributions

- Provides empirical, individual-level evidence on the concentration of fake news exposure and dissemination during a major election.
- Demonstrates a methodology of matching social media accounts to voter records, enabling demographically and politically grounded analysis of information consumption.
- Reframes the "fake news epidemic" narrative by quantifying its actual share of the news diet.

## Methods

- Analysis of Twitter data spanning the 2016 U.S. presidential election cycle.
- Matched Twitter accounts to specific registered voters to characterize who was exposed to and who shared fake news.
- Quantified fake news consumption relative to factual news consumption at the individual level.

## Findings

- Fake news accounted for nearly 6% of all news consumption on the platform.
- Just 1% of users were exposed to roughly 80% of fake news.
- Only 0.1% of users were responsible for sharing the majority of fake news (exact figure truncated in source).
- Exposure and sharing were thus driven by a "supersharer" and "superconsumer" minority rather than the general user base.

## Connections

This paper is a foundational empirical anchor for the study of misinformation exposure, closely aligned with work quantifying the limited overall reach of fake news such as [[Allen2020-nj]] and [[Guess2020-rr]], and with individual-level exposure studies like [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Budak2024-ef]]. Its concentration findings speak directly to research on superspreaders and elite dissemination, complementing diffusion studies like [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and Grinberg-adjacent recalibration debates around [[Guess2019-ym]] and [[Nyhan2023-gb]].
