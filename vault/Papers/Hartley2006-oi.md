# The public sphere on the beach

> Hartley, J., & Green, J. (2006). The public sphere on the beach. *European Journal of Cultural Studies*, *9*, 341–362. https://doi.org/10.1177/1367549406066077
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/1367549406066077)

## Summary

This article develops and operationalizes the concept of a **cultural public sphere** — a domain where the politics of private life, culture, and identity become visible to, and interact with, the mainstream political public sphere. Using the December 2005 Cronulla Beach "race riots" in Sydney as a case study, Hartley and Green argue that cultural conflicts rooted in ethnic identity are typically expressed through private pursuits such as leisure, music, and the beach, and only occasionally erupt into the political public sphere via mainstream media. They contend that mainstream news effectively *stages* extreme displays of conflict so that the ordinary public — the "missing middle" — can work through cultural-political tensions that rational public debate normally excludes. Adding this "cultural" dimension, they argue, makes the public sphere a more accurate guide to actual politics than the formal, rational Habermasian version.

## Key Contributions

- Refines the concept of the **cultural public sphere** (building on McGuigan) by distinguishing it from both "the" political public sphere and counterpublic spheres, and by specifying an internal/external communication relationship between them.
- Extends counterpublic theory to encompass right-wing and racist identity politics, challenging the field's tendency to associate counterpublics solely with progressive activism.
- Applies Warner's notion of "discourse publics" to show how non-canonical media (SMS, talk radio, websites) call publics together outside state and mainstream institutional control.
- Theorizes mainstream media's role in *staging* cultural conflict as a mechanism enabling the general public to process identity-based tensions excluded from rational political debate.
- Recasts "the beach" in Australian cultural studies from a cultural motif into a site of the public sphere and political contestation.

## Methods

Conceptual and theoretical synthesis drawing on public sphere theory (Habermas, Sennett), counterpublic theory (Felski, Warner, Cunningham, Gitlin), and the cultural public sphere (McGuigan, Hartley). The empirical work is a qualitative discourse analysis of media coverage — Australian and international newspapers, talk radio "shock jocks," television, white-supremacist and activist websites, and Wikipedia entries — organized as a case study of the Cronulla riots, contrasted with the Melbourne beach celebration of Princess Mary of Denmark. The authors stress that their media quotations are illustrative of discursive positions and the formation of a "discourse public," not a representative social-science sample.

## Findings

- Informal media (SMS, email, supremacist websites) operated "under the radar" to mobilize both Anglo-Celtic rioters and Lebanese-Australian retaliators — and were also used for counter-campaigns promoting diversity.
- Talk radio "shock jocks" functioned as a key site where an exclusionary, singular Australian identity was rehearsed and amplified, provoking and legitimizing the sentiments feeding the riot.
- The mainstream press shifted roles rapidly — from championing outraged locals ("We want our beach back") to condemning "Our Disgrace" and reframing events as a law-and-order matter.
- Commentators and the state struggled to classify events, labelling perpetrators "criminals" or "troublemakers" rather than political actors, revealing the political public sphere's difficulty managing identity conflicts.
- The beach functions as a mythic site of "Australian Ordinariness," whiteness, and national identity — long treated as cultural, not political — which is why its politicization was so shocking.
- The events illustrate a "conservative redefinition" of the Australian imagined community excluding Muslim- and Lebanese-Australians, contextualized by 9/11, the Tampa affair, and prior gang-rape cases.
