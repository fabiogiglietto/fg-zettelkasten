# &quot;Sticking their heads out above the parapets&quot;: Lived Experiences of Legal Risks in Research

> Park, S., & Thomas, D. R. (2026). &quot;Sticking their heads out above the parapets&quot;: Lived Experiences of Legal Risks in Research.
>
> [View paper](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Sticking%20their%20heads%20out%20above%20the%20parapets%22%3A%20Lived%20Experiences%20of%20Legal%20Risks%20in%20Research)

## Summary

This paper presents the first systematic qualitative study of how researchers actually experience the legal risks arising from overbroad computer crime, intellectual property, and related laws — most prominently the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), the UK Computer Misuse Act (CMA), and the DMCA. Drawing on interviews with 36 researchers (spanning 130 projects and incidents) and 8 supporting professionals such as lawyers, the authors document the chilling effects, emotional and professional toll, and coping strategies that researchers use to navigate legal uncertainty. Their central argument is that these laws fail to distinguish malicious hacking from good-faith public-interest research, deterring valuable work, and that empirical documentation of lived experience — rather than anecdote and "professional folklore" — is essential groundwork for law and policy reform.

## Key Contributions

- The first qualitative, systematic empirical study of researchers' lived experiences with legal risk, moving beyond anecdote.
- A "big-picture" view of the UK/US legal risk landscape across subfields and over time.
- Identifies and names the phenomenon of **"stockpiling"** undisclosed vulnerabilities as a systemic consequence of legal fear, with downstream security and coercion implications.
- Synthesises actionable strategies plus three broad recommendations: think through risks early; consider stakeholder incentives and public communication; build a support network of trusted colleagues and lawyers.
- Documents the value and limits of personal vs. institutional legal representation, and the fragile reliance on pro bono support.
- Supplies empirical evidence to inform reform of computer crime and IP law.

## Methods

Reflexive thematic analysis (following Mason and Saldaña) with coding in NVivo. Semi-structured interviews (30–120 min, ~54 hours total) were conducted November 2024–February 2026 with two groups: 36 researchers with US/UK legal-risk experience and 8 supporting professionals who had each helped at least 20 researchers. Recruitment used public documents, professional networks, snowball sampling, and community outreach, spanning academia, industry, independent, and government research. Project attributes were systematically documented and corroborated with privately shared emails and threats. Strong ethics and confidentiality mitigations included IRB/institutional approval, verbal consent, deletion of consent forms, and end-to-end encrypted communication. The authors explicitly did not reach saturation and disclaim quantitative generalisability, having stopped to keep findings timely.

## Findings

- Vulnerability research has the longest history of legal risk; **social computing research surged since 2020**, with four times as many projects and twice as many legal threats as the next category.
- 23 participants described legal risk as ubiquitous or unavoidable in their fields; many framed their work as a public service despite the risks.
- ~30% of projects involved actual legal threats, though most did not escalate to prosecution or litigation, and some rested on legally tenuous grounds.
- Emotional impacts were pronounced — fear, worry, frustration and stress dominated (90 expressions), with some reporting all-consuming anxiety, weight loss, and sleeplessness.
- Chilling effects included abandoning or not publishing projects; **stockpiling** of undisclosed vulnerabilities occurred at scale, with three researchers offered "life-changing money" to buy their silence.
- Severe consequences occurred: job loss, blacklisting, overturned criminal convictions, months of incarceration, and a collaborator likely unable to return home.
- Researchers retained their own lawyer in only 30 of 130 projects, mostly at no cost via pro bono (17 of 30) — raising sustainability concerns. Personal counsel was overwhelmingly positive; institutional counsel was mixed because it serves the institution's interests.
- Industry lawyers/employers were generally protective; academic institutional support varied and was more often negative than positive.
- Jurisdictional differences were smaller than expected — UK researchers often navigate US law; only 15% of UK-based projects faced actual threats vs. 40% of US-based, and the UK CMA's lack of a civil cause of action reduces private-party risk.
- The second Trump administration and politically motivated ("McCarthy-esque") threats, including Congressional investigations, made social computing risk more unpredictable, exposing the fragility of relying on norms rather than laws.
- ToS violations were a recurring and often necessary feature of essential social computing research; conference ethics review was criticised as immature relative to IRB review.

## Connections

This paper illuminates the legal and ethical foundations that constrain the platform-data research documented elsewhere in this register, particularly work on Terms of Service violations, scraping, and platform access. It connects to studies grappling with the practical and governance challenges of collecting platform data, such as Davidson2023-cy — no such key exists; instead the genuine intellectual relatives here concern platform data governance and access constraints, e.g. [[Rieder2025-ju]], [[Rieder2026-pp]], and [[Bruns2025-fz]], which examine the shrinking terrain for independent social media research. The paper's emphasis on ToS and legal risk as barriers to essential social computing research also speaks directly to methods-and-teaching discussions of how researchers navigate restricted data environments, complementing rather than duplicating that empirical work.
