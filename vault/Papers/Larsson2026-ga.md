# From content to likes – how political parties’ campaign posts trigger user engagement across Facebook, Instagram and Twitter

> Larsson, A. O., & Skogerbø, E. (2026). From content to likes – how political parties’ campaign posts trigger user engagement across Facebook, Instagram and Twitter. *Journal of Information Technology & Politics*, 1–15. https://doi.org/10.1080/19331681.2026.2767282
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/19331681.2026.2767282)

## Summary

This study examines how the content of political campaign posts drives low-threshold user engagement—measured as likes—across Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter during Norway's 2021 parliamentary election. Through a manual content analysis of 2,197 posts from nine parliamentary parties and their leaders, Larsson and Skogerbø test whether three content types—calls to action, populist styles, and policy topics—influence the number of likes a post receives. Their central argument is that engagement is strongly platform-specific: the same content performs very differently depending on each platform's affordances and audience composition, challenging any view of "social media" as a single undifferentiated channel. The paper advances the case for cross-platform comparative analysis and offers empirical evidence from a multiparty European democracy as a counterpoint to US-centric scholarship.

## Key Contributions

- A rare cross-platform comparative analysis that assesses multiple content types (populist styles, calls to action, policy issues) together rather than studying one in isolation.
- Empirical evidence from Norway, a multiparty European democracy, contrasting with the US-dominated literature.
- Historically valuable documentation of 2021 Twitter campaign dynamics, given the platform's subsequent decline as a Norwegian campaign venue after its transformation into X.
- Methodological demonstration of treating "likes" as a functionally equivalent engagement metric to enable cross-platform comparison.
- Practical guidance that campaign strategies must be tailored per platform and that policy content rarely drives engagement.

## Methods

Standardized manual content analysis of 2,197 posts (Facebook: 1,106; Instagram: 576; Twitter: 515) from the nine parliamentary parties and their leaders. Data were collected via the CrowdTangle API (Facebook, Instagram) and the Twitter academic research API, with retweets and deleted posts removed. Three content concepts were coded dichotomously and combined into indices: calls to action (informing, mobilizing, interacting), populist styles (people-centrism, anti-elitism, exclusion of out-groups, following Reinemann et al. 2017), and 37 topics reduced to 22 issue categories. The number of likes served as the dependent variable, analyzed through three separate negative binomial regressions (one per platform), controlling for actor type, populist party status (Progress Party), and vote share. Intercoder reliability (Brennan and Prediger's kappa) ranged from .781 to 1 on a 5.4% subsample.

## Findings

- **Platform-specific engagement** was the headline result—content effects differed markedly across the three platforms.
- **Twitter:** both informing and mobilizing calls to action positively affected likes, consistent with its elite/engaged-user profile; no populist style was significant; only education/research and cultural policy had (negative) effects.
- **Facebook:** exclusion of out-groups positively affected likes, reinforcing Facebook's reputation as a populism-privileging platform; the informing index negatively affected likes; several topics reduced likes while domestic policy increased them.
- **Instagram:** the interacting index negatively affected likes; economic inequality and foreign policy raised likes while several other topics lowered them; the populist (Progress Party) control was non-significant, unlike on Facebook.
- **Policy topics** generally had little influence, and where significant were more often negative than positive.
- **Party leaders** consistently received more likes than party accounts across all three platforms.
- Hypotheses were only partly supported: calls-to-action effects on Twitter and exclusion of out-groups on Facebook held, but interacting and other populism hypotheses did not.
