# Social media is now parasocial media

> danah boyd (2026). Social media is now parasocial media. *Social Media + Society*, *12*. https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261437487
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261437487)

## Summary

In this anniversary essay for *Social Media + Society*, danah boyd argues that the platforms once called "social media" have changed so fundamentally over two decades that the label has become a misnomer. Where early-2000s platforms supported reciprocal sociality, identity work, and community-building, contemporary platforms reward scrolling over posting, algorithmically curated and professionally produced content over peer-to-peer exchange, and one-sided attention to influencers over mutual friendship. boyd proposes that scholars deprecate the term "social media" in favor of **"parasocial media"** to better capture this shift and its consequences for governance, inequality, and digital sociality. Writing as both a longtime participant in coining social-media-era vocabulary and a critic revisiting those choices, she positions the piece as a corrective terminological intervention.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces and argues for **"parasocial media"** as a replacement term for "social media" in the contemporary platform era.
- Provides a first-person historical account of how "social media" emerged and consolidated in the mid-2000s, tracing labels from "social computing" and "social software" through "Web 2.0," "social network sites," and "social media."
- Connects parasocial interaction theory (Horton & Wohl, 1956) to current debates on platform political economy, influencer culture, and enshittification.
- Calls on the field to revise its analytic vocabulary and reframe governance, inequality, and sociality debates accordingly.

## Methods

A reflective, historically grounded essay drawing on the author's two decades of participant-observation, blogging, and consulting in the social media industry. boyd combines conceptual and terminological genealogy with a synthesis of secondary literature across platform studies, influencer studies, parasocial interaction theory, and the political economy of platforms.

## Findings

- Posting behavior on major platforms has declined while scrolling dominates user activity.
- Feeds are increasingly populated by professionalized creator and advertiser content rather than peer updates from friends.
- Doctorow's three-stage "enshittification" framework captures the shift from user-serving to investor-serving platforms, with current platforms in the third stage.
- Algorithmic incentives transformed "creators" into "influencers" producing assetizable content, while ordinary users face privacy, reputational, and social risks that make posting less attractive.
- Parasocial engagement, though emotionally intense, fails to generate reciprocal social fabric and can coexist with loneliness and toxicity.

## Connections

boyd's argument that intimacy on platforms has become one-sided and mediated by influencers speaks directly to work on parasociality and creator relations, most obviously [[Baym2026-tr]]. Her enshittification-inflected political economy of platform consolidation and algorithmic incentives connects to platform-infrastructure and governance critiques such as [[Helmond2026-ll]] and [[Bechmann2026-dr]], while her concern with the decline of reciprocal sociality and the rise of toxicity and manipulation links to [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]].
