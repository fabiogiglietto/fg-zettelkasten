# The link has died; Long live the link! Cross-platform controversy mapping with the use of URLs

> Lompe, M. (2026). The link has died; Long live the link! Cross-platform controversy mapping with the use of URLs. *Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW)*, *35*, 20. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10606-026-09557-1
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10606-026-09557-1)

## Summary

Lompe argues for a methodological revival of URL (link) analysis as a tool for cross-platform controversy mapping, challenging the widespread assumption that link studies became obsolete after platformization shifted research toward hashtags, keywords, and platform-native metrics. Drawing on controversy mapping as an empirical application of Actor-Network Theory and on CSCW work on socio-technical systems, the paper treats links not as neutral carriers of information but as non-human mediators whose meaning is shaped by platform affordances. Using the decade-long Polish controversy over a shipping canal through the Vistula Spit, the author analyzes a large Facebook and Twitter corpus to show that URLs remain reliable controversy indicators — but in platform-specific ways: markers of political alignment and polarization on Twitter, and evidential and mobilizing resources for collective action on Facebook.

## Key Contributions

- Demonstrates that URLs function as platform-specific indicators of engagement with a controversy — polarization on Twitter versus mobilization on Facebook.
- Explains these differences through platform affordances, which shape not just the circulation but the *meaning* and *function* of links.
- Reframes links as active mediators (non-human actors) rather than residual artifacts of the pre-platform web.
- Argues for a methodological return to link analysis, bridging early digital methods with contemporary platformization research and CSCW work on socio-technical systems.
- Offers link-tracing as a practical, trace-based method for detecting contested issues, influential sources, and the spread of potentially misleading content across platforms.

## Methods

Mixed-methods controversy mapping centered on a single digital object — URLs shared in posts about the Vistula Spit Canal controversy (2016–2023). Twitter data were collected via university API access through 4CAT; Facebook data via CrowdTangle, using identical phrase-search keywords. A nested two-query design distinguished a "core collection" (bare object-phrase names) from an "extended collection" (phrases paired with discourse terms, slogans, and hashtags from both camps). The resulting dataset (21,756 Facebook posts; 133,814 tweets) was analyzed quantitatively for the frequency and distribution of digital objects. URL-sharing networks were built in Gephi, spatialized with ForceAtlas2, clustered via modularity-based community detection, and manually coded into seven link categories (news, local news, economic news, conservative, far-right, suspicious, and informational sites) using external ratings such as Media Bias/Fact Check.

## Findings

- Linking was prominent on both platforms but served different roles. On Twitter, external linking was common (48.7% core, 61.8% extended) and highly self-referential — up to ~52% of extended-collection links pointed back to Twitter — traveling with retweets and mentions in an economy of amplification.
- On Facebook, over 90% of posts contained links (93% core, 94% extended), a near-universal default even after the 2018 feed change deprioritized external-publisher content; link posts drew more comments than link-free posts.
- Twitter link clusters were dense and polarized, concentrating around conservative, far-right, and suspicious (including Russian-affiliated) sites set against a news cluster, bridged mainly by self-referential platform-internal links — links as markers of alignment rather than evidence.
- Facebook link clusters were more dispersed and outward-oriented, connecting local news, environmental and grassroots protest accounts, and far-right sites — links functioning evidentially and as mobilizing resources.
- The same mainstream news resources were mobilized by different political camps across platforms (e.g., state broadcaster TVP circulating within Twitter's conservative/nationalist segment, while mainstream portals were shared by center-left accounts on Facebook).

## Connections

This paper's cross-platform, URL-based tracing of circulation and source-sharing resonates with other link- and domain-sharing network studies of coordinated or contested content, such as [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], and [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]. Its concern with how platform affordances shape the meaning and circulation of shared links connects to broader news-sharing research on exposure and amplification, including [[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Messing2014-jc]], and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]]. The Actor-Network / controversy-mapping framing and attention to suspicious and far-right sources also link it to work on misleading-content diffusion like [[Vosoughi2018-at]].
