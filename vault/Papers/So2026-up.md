# The social and cultural life of artificial intelligence (AI) slop: A research framework

> So, R. J., & Long, H. (2026). The social and cultural life of artificial intelligence (AI) slop: A research framework. *Big Data & Society*, *13*. https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517261506941
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517261506941)

## Summary

This paper proposes **SLOP** as an analytical category — deliberately distinct from the vernacular pejorative "AI slop" — that names a *condition* rather than a class of objects. The condition is one in which AI-generated content has achieved sufficient scale and surface competence that the signals people once used to evaluate quality and credibility no longer reliably function. So and Long argue that the dominant institutional response — detection tools that treat synthetic content as "pollution" to be filtered — is inadequate, because the value or harm of any artifact emerges from its social context (who makes it, who engages with it, and why) rather than from innate properties. They characterize SLOP along three dimensions — production, reception, and effects — and translate the concept into a falsifiable, three-part research agenda. The core diagnosis is that what has broken down is not an abundance of content but the *evaluative capacity* by which human judgment distinguished competence from its imitation.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces SLOP as a distinct analytical category naming a **relational social condition**, separating serious analysis from the vernacular dismissal "slop."
- Systematically shows what the detection, spam, and misinformation frameworks each capture and each miss about AI-generated content.
- Articulates a three-dimensional framework — **production, reception, effects/governance** — in which consequences vary by an artifact's position and context.
- Reframes governance failure as a **loss of evaluative capacity** rather than a failure to write better rules; institutions must invest in judgment itself.
- Offers a concrete, falsifiable research agenda designed to run *without* platform cooperation, specifying how each prediction could fail.

## Methods

This is a conceptual and theoretical framework-building paper reporting no original empirical data. Its method is critical review and synthesis of three existing framings — AI-content detection (zero-shot methods, watermarking, population-level estimation), spam scholarship, and misinformation/disinformation studies — grounded in platform studies (Gillespie, Srnicek, Seaver), cultural sociology (Bourdieu's field theory, Lamont's sociology of evaluation and "heterarchy"), book and print history (Johns, Wellmon), and Bok's philosophy of deception versus concealment. From this the authors propose a three-part agenda: (1) ethnographies and interviews with content-farm producers in Kenya, India, Vietnam, and the Philippines; (2) computational and interview-based reception studies, including analysis of LinkedIn's "mark as AI slop" button and other in-the-wild judgment signals; and (3) study of peer-review gatekeeping via editor/reviewer interviews plus archival records.

## Findings

- Detection tools perform poorly: >60% average false-positive rates on non-native English writers; another study found <40% mean accuracy, dropping to 22% on lightly edited text.
- Detection is structurally positioned to lose an "arms race," since distributional signatures become harder to identify as models improve.
- AI content is already prevalent and unevenly distributed: ~20% of YouTube videos, >80% of LinkedIn long-form posts, 9% of new US online newspaper articles, ~25% of certain TikTok hashtag results vs. <5% on Instagram, and 6.5–16.9% of reviews at major AI conferences.
- Production is often profit-motivated and automated, yet underwritten by low-wage human labor in content mills and subsidized by platform bonus programs.
- Institutional labeling largely fails: only about half of AI content on TikTok carried appropriate labels; Amazon KDP's caps and disclosure rules are imperfect proxies that cannot validate the distinction they demand.
- Users often cannot distinguish synthetic content and sometimes prefer it — AI-synthesized faces were rated more trustworthy than real ones, and accusations of AI authorship do not track actual AI-distinguishing prose features.

## Connections

This paper's central move — distinguishing SLOP from falsehood and deception — makes it a deliberate counterpoint to the misinformation-exposure literature, where work on the limited reach and partisan concentration of exposure ([[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2021-ym]]), the question of real-world effects ([[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Allen2024-av]]), and the diffusion of false content ([[Vosoughi2018-at]]) share its concern with how synthetic or low-quality content circulates without resolving the evaluative breakdown So and Long foreground. Within the generative-AI imaginaries topic it speaks directly to cultural and platform-studies accounts of AI's informational condition such as [[Manovich2026-ih]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]], and its reframing of concealment over deception usefully contrasts with intervention-oriented work on recalibrating judgment like [[Pennycook2021-jq]].
