# Resilience to Online Disinformation: A Framework for Cross-National Comparative Research

> Humprecht, E., Esser, F., & Aelst, P. V. (2020). Resilience to Online Disinformation: A Framework for Cross-National Comparative Research. *The International Journal of Press/Politics*, 1940161219900126. https://doi.org/10.1177/1940161219900126
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/1940161219900126)

## Summary

This paper reframes online disinformation from a purely content-level problem into a question of national **resilience** — the capacity of a country's political, media, and economic environment to resist the spread and impact of misleading content. Working in the tradition of comparative political communication and media systems research, Humprecht, Esser, and Van Aelst argue that some countries are systematically more resistant to disinformation than others, and that this variation can be traced to identifiable structural conditions. Rather than presenting new empirical data, the paper's central output is a theoretical framework designed to enable systematic cross-national comparison. Disinformation itself is defined as misleading content produced to generate profit, pursue political ends, or maliciously deceive.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces **national resilience to online disinformation** as a distinct object of comparative study.
- Provides a theoretical framework organizing the structural conditions that shape cross-national differences in resilience.
- Establishes a conceptual foundation for systematic cross-national comparative research on disinformation.
- Reframes disinformation as a phenomenon whose impact is conditioned by national context, not merely by content.

## Methods

The paper is conceptual rather than empirical. Its method is the development of a theoretical framework that identifies the political, media-system, and economic conditions relevant to resilience, coupled with a comparative cross-national research design intended to array countries along these conditions. No empirical findings are reported; the framework is offered as scaffolding for future comparative work.

## Findings

- No empirical results are presented — the contribution is theoretical.
- The core argument is that resilience varies across countries and that this variation is explainable through structural conditions rather than being random or purely content-driven.
- A comparative, cross-national lens is presented as necessary for understanding why disinformation gains traction in some contexts but not others.

## Connections

This framework is foundational to later comparative work on disinformation resilience; see [[Humprecht2025-ml]] for continuation of this research program. The media-systems and structural-conditions framing connects to comparative research on partisan media ecosystems such as [[Heft2021-ky]] and [[Benkler2018-lw]], as well as to broader theorizing on why disinformation spreads and how audiences resist it in [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] and [[Tsfati2020-uo]]. It also complements individual-level exposure studies like [[Guess2019-ym]] and [[Grinberg2019-ua]] by supplying the country-level context those studies often hold fixed.
