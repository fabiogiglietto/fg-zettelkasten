# If Likes Were Votes: An Empirical Study on the 2011 Italian Administrative Elections

> Giglietto, F. (2012). If Likes Were Votes: An Empirical Study on the 2011 Italian Administrative Elections. *Elsevier BV*. https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.1982736
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.1982736)

## Summary

This paper asks whether Facebook popularity, measured through page Likes, can forecast electoral outcomes, using the 2011 Italian administrative elections as an early empirical test case. Giglietto collected Like counts for mayoral candidates across Italy's 29 provincial capitals and built two models — one predicting vote shares and one predicting the winning candidate. The results are deliberately mixed: Facebook Like share did not meaningfully correlate with vote share, yet the most popular candidate on Facebook won or placed second in 82% of contests. Rather than resolving the contradictory findings that already characterized the social-media-forecasting literature, the study confirms them while adding a new cultural and political context (Italy's multiparty system) and introducing refined metrics for evaluating prediction gaps.

## Key Contributions

- A new case study extending social-media election forecasting to the previously unexamined Italian administrative/multiparty context.
- Two forecasting models: a vote-share prediction model and a winner-prediction precision index.
- Refined evaluation metrics — Candidate Prediction Gap (CPG), Municipality Prediction Gap (MPG), Party Prediction Gap (PPG), and a 0–10 prediction precision index.
- Documentation of systematic partisan bias (overestimation of extreme parties, underestimation of the center-right), pointing toward poll-weighting-style corrections.

## Methods

- Sampled 229 mayoral candidates across the 29 provincial capitals; identified 104 Facebook pages (~44.5% of candidates) via Facebook's internal search.
- Collected Like counts from 25 April to 15 May 2011 by periodically querying the Facebook Graph API (179,003 total Likes on monitored pages at the final checkpoint).
- Defined CPG (vote share minus Like share per candidate) and ABS[MPG] (absolute average of candidate CPGs within a municipality).
- Built a precision index (0–10) rewarding correct prediction of first- and second-place candidates.
- Categorized candidates into seven political areas (extreme right to extreme left) to compute PPG; grouped municipalities by share of candidates on Facebook and by electorate size. Methodology adapted from Olson and Bunnett (2010).

## Findings

- No significant correlation between Facebook popularity share and vote share.
- Average absolute CPG was 15.77% (range 0–84.18%); average ABS[MPG] was 18.99%.
- Prediction error fell as more candidates had Facebook pages (ABS[MPG] 29.43% → 11.88% across tiers) and in larger cities (9.52% for electorates over 200,000 vs. ~21% for smaller).
- All PPG values were negative — predicted shares typically exceeded actual votes; extreme forces showed larger margins and the center-right was markedly underestimated.
- Average precision index score was 4.71/10; the most popular Facebook candidate won 39% of races and came second in 43%.

## Connections

This is an early contribution in Giglietto's longer trajectory on social media, elections, and digital traces, and connects to his later work on Italian political communication and platform data such as [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], [[Giglietto2015-320951c4]], and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]]. Its concern with partisan asymmetries in online participation — where more polarized supporters inflate certain parties' visibility — anticipates themes in engagement- and exposure-based platform studies like [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]].
