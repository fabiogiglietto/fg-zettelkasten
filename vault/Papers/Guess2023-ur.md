# How do social media feed algorithms affect attitudes and behavior in an election campaign?

> Guess, A. M., Malhotra, N., Pan, J., Barberá, P., Allcott, H., Brown, T., Crespo-Tenorio, A., Dimmery, D., Freelon, D., Gentzkow, M., González-Bailón, S., Kennedy, E., Kim, Y. J., & Tucker, J. A. (2023). How do social media feed algorithms affect attitudes and behavior in an election campaign?. *Science*, *381*, 398–404. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abp9364
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abp9364)

## Summary

This paper reports one of the landmark experimental studies from the 2020 Facebook and Instagram Election Study, testing whether the platforms' feed-ranking algorithms causally shape political attitudes and behavior. Consenting users were randomly assigned to receive a reverse-chronological feed in place of the default algorithmic ranking for the duration of the 2020 US election campaign. The intervention had large effects on how users engaged with the platforms and on the content they saw — but no detectable downstream effect on political attitudes or behaviors. The paper thus challenges the widespread assumption that algorithmic curation is a strong driver of polarization or misinformation-driven attitude change, at least over a several-month campaign window.

## Key Contributions

- Provides rare **causal experimental** evidence (not correlational) on the real-world political effects of social media ranking algorithms during a high-stakes election.
- Decouples two often-conflated effects: algorithms strongly shape **engagement and content exposure**, but have **limited downstream effect** on political attitudes and behavior.
- Injects causally identified findings into public and policy debates about the democratic impact of algorithmic feeds.

## Methods

- Large-scale randomized controlled field experiment on consenting Facebook and Instagram users during the 2020 US election.
- Treatment: replacing the default algorithmic feed with a **reverse-chronologically ordered feed**.
- Outcomes measured across three layers: platform usage (time spent, activity), content exposure (political content, untrustworthy content), and downstream political attitudes and behaviors.

## Findings

- Removing algorithmic ranking **substantially decreased** time spent on and activity within the platforms.
- The chronological feed **increased** the volume of political content users saw on both platforms.
- The chronological feed **increased** exposure to untrustworthy content — a counterintuitive result, since the algorithm suppressed rather than amplified such content in aggregate.
- Despite these exposure and engagement shifts, there were **no measurable changes** in political attitudes or behavior over the study period.

## Connections

This is a foundational paper in the empirical literature on platform effects during elections and sits alongside other work from the same experimental program and adjacent studies of exposure and misinformation, including [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]], [[Guess2023-ai]], and [[Eady2023-xg]]. Its finding that increased exposure to untrustworthy content need not shift attitudes complements the "minimal effects" and low-prevalence framing in work such as [[Budak2024-ef]], [[Allen2024-av]], and [[Guess2020-rr]]. It also speaks to the broader debate over echo chambers and algorithmic filtering represented by [[Flaxman2016-lm]] and [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]].
