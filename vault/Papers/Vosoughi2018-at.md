# The spread of true and false news online

> Vosoughi, S., Roy, D., & Aral, S. (2018). The spread of true and false news online. *Science*, *359*, 1146–1151. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aap9559
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aap9559)

## Summary

This landmark study examines how true and false news stories propagate on Twitter over an eleven-year period (2006–2017). Drawing on roughly 126,000 rumor cascades spread by about 3 million people, and classifying stories as true or false using independent fact-checking organizations, the authors quantify differences in the diffusion dynamics of verified and unverified information. Their central finding is stark: false news travels farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth across every category examined. Crucially, they attribute this asymmetry to human behavior rather than automated bots, and point to the novelty of false stories and the emotional reactions they provoke as candidate explanations.

## Key Contributions

- Large-scale empirical evidence quantifying the differential spread of true versus false news online.
- A data-driven foundation for understanding misinformation dynamics on social platforms.
- Evidence that the amplification of false news is a human, not primarily automated, phenomenon.
- Grounding for debates on the political, economic, and social risks of false news.

## Methods

- Analyzed a dataset of rumor cascades on Twitter spanning 2006 to 2017.
- Examined approximately 126,000 rumors spread by ~3 million people.
- Labeled stories as true or false using six independent fact-checking organizations.
- Compared diffusion metrics — reach, speed, depth, and cascade size — between true and false news, and assessed the role of bots versus human accounts.

## Findings

- False news reached more people overall than the truth.
- The top 1% of false news cascades reached between 1,000 and 100,000 people, whereas true news rarely reached more than 1,000.
- False news diffused faster and more broadly than true news across categories.
- Human users, not bots, were primarily responsible for the greater spread of false news.
- Novelty and emotional response (e.g., surprise, disgust) plausibly explain why false stories spread more readily.

## Connections

This is a foundational reference for the empirical study of online misinformation diffusion, sitting alongside work on echo chambers and rumor dynamics such as [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] and studies of exposure to fake news on Twitter like [[Grinberg2019-ua]]. Its emphasis on human sharing behavior and the emotional/novelty drivers of false content complements accuracy-nudge and cognitive interventions in [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and the reflexive-control framing of coordinated rumoring in [[Starbird2019-qv]], while its scale-and-reach findings inform later reassessments of misinformation prevalence such as [[Budak2024-ef]] and [[Allen2020-nj]].
