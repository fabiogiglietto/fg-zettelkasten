# Network propaganda: Manipulation, disinformation, and radicalization in American politics

> Benkler, Y. R., Farris, R., & Roberts, H. (2018). Network propaganda: Manipulation, disinformation, and radicalization in American politics. *Aquila Digital Community (University of Southern Mississippi)*.
>
> [View paper](https://aquila.usm.edu/katrinagulfcoast_photos/42)

## Summary

*Network Propaganda* mounts a structural, evidence-driven challenge to the conventional post-2016 narrative that blames social media, Russian influence operations, and "fake news" entrepreneurs for the "post-truth" condition of American politics. Benkler, Farris, and Roberts argue that these novel technologies and foreign actors, while present, were less decisive than commonly claimed. Instead, they locate the core dynamics of disinformation in the *structure* of the American political media ecosystem — and specifically in a fundamental **asymmetry** between an insular right-wing media sphere and the more porous, professionally-normed mainstream and left-leaning outlets. Combining large-scale empirical mapping with targeted case studies, the book reframes disinformation and radicalization as products of media-ecosystem architecture rather than of platform technology or manipulation alone.

## Key Contributions

- Offers an evidence-based rebuttal to the dominant explanation that social media, Russian propaganda, and fake-news entrepreneurs drove the 2016 election's information environment.
- Introduces a structural, ecosystem-level framework for understanding propaganda, disinformation, and radicalization in American politics.
- Advances the concept of **asymmetric polarization** — showing the two sides of the U.S. media landscape are not mirror images.

## Methods

- Large-scale empirical analysis of media coverage and online news-sharing patterns.
- Network analysis of the structure and interconnections among media outlets and their audiences, used to map the ecosystem.
- Detailed case studies of specific disinformation and propaganda episodes to trace propagation pathways.

## Findings

- The American media ecosystem is characterized by **structural asymmetry**, not symmetric polarization; the right-wing sphere is more insular and self-reinforcing.
- This asymmetric architecture, rather than technology per se, shapes how disinformation originates and spreads.
- Technological manipulation and foreign interference were less decisive than internal media-ecosystem dynamics in producing the disinformation environment.

## Connections

This is a foundational structural counterpoint to platform- and algorithm-centric accounts of misinformation, connecting to work on selective exposure and echo chambers such as [[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Flaxman2016-lm]], and [[Barbera2015-fw]], and to the ecosystem-oriented study of hyperpartisan and radical-right media in [[Heft2021-ky]]. Its emphasis on structural rather than individual drivers complements affective-polarization research like [[Iyengar2019-jj]] and partisan-motivated sharing in [[Osmundsen2021-et]], while its skepticism of the "fake news" panic dialogues with corrective work in [[Lazer2018-mm]] and [[Tsfati2020-uo]].
