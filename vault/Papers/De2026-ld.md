# The discursive flexibility of changecraft: Platform change discourse in Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X

> De, A., & Cotter, K. (2026). The discursive flexibility of changecraft: Platform change discourse in Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X. *Platforms & Society*, *3*. https://doi.org/10.1177/29768624251408212
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/29768624251408212)

## Summary

This paper introduces **changecraft** as a conceptual vocabulary for how major social media platforms discursively construct, justify, and normalize ongoing transformation in their features, policies, and governance. Analyzing 301 public-facing communications from Meta, YouTube, X, and TikTok (April 2022–January 2025), De and Cotter argue that platforms exhibit "discursive flexibility": they narrate change to appear neutral, responsive, and innovative while quietly consolidating power and deepening user dependence. Crucially, the authors depart from prior accounts centered on concealment, showing instead that platforms actively *stage* and manage change discursively — rendering infrastructural shifts aesthetically visible, reframing ideological pivots as continuity, and using incremental "patchworking" to cumulatively reorient platform logics.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces **changecraft** as a novel analytic concept for studying platform transformation as a discursive practice.
- Provides a shared vocabulary — infrastructural visibility, ideological recalibration, and patchworking — for platform studies scholars.
- Shifts analytic focus from *concealment* to *strategic discursive control* of change.
- Offers a comparative empirical mapping of change discourse across four major platforms, identifying recurring rationalization tropes: **creator benevolence, democratization, and connectivity**.
- Bridges platform and infrastructure studies by showing how platforms invert the typical invisibility of infrastructure, making change aesthetically visible as a governance tactic.

## Methods

Qualitative content analysis of 301 platform-authored documents — Meta (n=88), TikTok (n=70), X (n=70), YouTube (n=58) — drawn from official newsrooms and blogs (April 2022–January 2025). The authors conducted iterative inductive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke) in ATLAS.ti, beginning with five randomly sampled documents per platform and refining categories across rounds, supplemented by selective discourse-analytic techniques (Wood and Kroger) attending to framing, positioning, and silences. Data were reduced to platform updates, company communications, user-directed messages, and management statements, then developed via memoing and team discussion into themes of material change, ideological change, and rationalization.

## Findings

- **Material changes** are foregrounded via visible redesigns, screenshots, and feature demos (Meta AI Backgrounds, YouTube Shorts on TV) that emphasize *how* rather than *why*, creating an aesthetic of progress.
- These visible updates often mask deeper monetization and control shifts (Meta Reels ranking/payouts; YouTube likeness-management framed as creator protection).
- **Ideological changes** are presented as recalibration, not rupture, via phrases like "updating our approach" and "original intention" (Meta's fact-checking pivot; X's post-acquisition transparency messaging).
- **Creator benevolence** framing casts platforms as supportive mentors (TikTok Creator Studio, YouTube Hype, Instagram Best Practices).
- **Democratization rhetoric** ("for fans, by fans"; collaborative teen supervision) simulates participatory governance without ceding decision-making power.
- **Connectivity discourse** ties feature changes to community-building while embedding monetization, surveillance, and engagement maximization.

## Connections

This paper contributes to critical scholarship on how platforms narrate their own governance and legitimacy, connecting to work extending Helmond's platformization and infrastructuralization arguments [[Helmond2026-ll]]. Its attention to the rhetorical and normative labor platforms perform resonates with critiques of platform accountability and self-presentation [[Marwick2026-ss]] and with anniversary-essay reflections on the shifting relationship between platforms and their publics [[Baym2026-tr]].
