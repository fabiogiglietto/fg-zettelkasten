# Divide and conquer? The impact of media narratives on digital election interference on political and perceived polarisation

> Karlsson, E. (2026). Divide and conquer? The impact of media narratives on digital election interference on political and perceived polarisation. *New Media & Society*, *28*, 2812–2837. https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448251410509
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448251410509)

## Summary

This paper asks whether news coverage of digital election interference — independent of any actual persuasive effect of the interference itself — reshapes political polarisation. Using a pre-registered UK survey experiment (N=3779) fielded in March 2024, participants read fabricated news stories about coordinated fake-account interference in the 2019 general election, with the beneficiary party and interference source systematically varied. While exposure produced no aggregate polarising effect, respondents who saw the *opposing* party portrayed as the beneficiary reported significantly higher perceived societal division and stronger affective polarisation toward ideological opponents. The paper argues that media discourse about interference can itself be a distinct causal pathway to democratic harm, potentially advancing the very polarising goals such campaigns pursue.

## Key Contributions

- Provides causal experimental evidence on political polarisation as an outcome of interference discourse, extending literatures that have focused on trust and satisfaction with democracy.
- Empirically distinguishes perceived, affective, and ideological polarisation and shows they respond differently to the same stimulus.
- Identifies partisan-alignment asymmetry: effects concentrate among respondents whose out-party is portrayed as beneficiary.
- Offers a UK-based counterweight to US-dominated disinformation-effects research, suggesting ideological (left–right) identities matter more than partisan ones in fluid multi-party systems.
- Contributes to normative debates about responsible journalistic coverage of election interference.

## Methods

Online survey experiment (Qualtrics via Prolific) with 10 conditions (9 treatments + control, ~378 per cell) fully crossing interference source (foreign/domestic/unspecified) with beneficiary party (Labour/Conservative/unspecified). Sample pre-balanced for Labour and Conservative partisans, with manipulation checks for attention. Outcomes: perceived polarisation (division and extremism items), affective polarisation (feeling thermometers for party and ideology in/out-groups), and ideological polarisation (party support strength and left–right distance from centre). Analysis used linear regression with robust SEs comparing (i) binary treatment vs. control and (ii) partisan match vs. no-match, with Benjamini–Hochberg corrections.

## Findings

- No significant aggregate effect of treatment exposure on perceived, affective, or ideological polarisation.
- "No-match" condition (opposing party portrayed as beneficiary) raised perceived societal division (~0.026, p=.01, q=.09); no effect on perceived extremism.
- No-match condition also raised affective polarisation toward ideological opponents (~0.026, p=.016, q=.094); no equivalent partisan out-group effect.
- Exploratory evidence that respondents seeing their preferred party benefit reported *lower* affective polarisation, hinting at motivated reasoning or dissonance reduction.
- Ideological polarisation (party support strength, left–right self-placement) was unaffected across all specifications.

## Connections

This paper contributes to work on how coverage and meta-discourse about disinformation — rather than exposure to it — shapes democratic attitudes, resonating with [[Bailard2024-pj]] and broader arguments about alarmist framing effects. Its focus on affective and perceived polarisation as downstream consequences of information-disorder narratives connects to [[Hameleers2026-mc]] and [[Lyons2026-ca]], while the asymmetric, partisan-conditional response echoes motivated-reasoning findings in [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] and [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]. The UK-focused, non-US design also complements the largely US-centric polarisation literature represented in this cluster.
