# Exposure to the Russian Internet Research Agency foreign influence campaign on Twitter in the 2016 US election and its relationship to attitudes and voting behavior

> Eady, G., Paskhalis, T., Zilinsky, J., Bonneau, R., Nagler, J., & Tucker, J. A. (2023). Exposure to the Russian Internet Research Agency foreign influence campaign on Twitter in the 2016 US election and its relationship to attitudes and voting behavior. *Nature Communications*, *14*, 62. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-35576-9
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-35576-9)

## Summary

This paper examines whether exposure to the Russian Internet Research Agency (IRA) foreign influence campaign on Twitter shifted American political attitudes or voting behavior during the 2016 US election. By linking longitudinal survey responses to respondents' actual Twitter feeds, the authors directly measure individual-level exposure to IRA-generated content — a task previously constrained by data scarcity. Their central finding is deflationary: exposure was heavily concentrated among a small number of already-partisan users (especially Republicans), and there is no measurable relationship between that exposure and changes in attitudes, polarization, or vote choice. The paper thus serves as an evidence-based counterweight to widespread assumptions about the persuasive power of foreign influence operations.

## Key Contributions

- Provides the first direct, individual-level empirical link between measured exposure to a foreign influence campaign and political behavior, merging survey data with actual Twitter feeds.
- Offers an empirical counterweight to widespread assumptions about the persuasive impact of the IRA campaign on the 2016 election.
- Introduces a methodological template for studying influence operations by combining survey and behavioral (social media) data.

## Methods

The authors collected longitudinal survey data from US respondents and linked each respondent to their real-world Twitter feed, allowing direct measurement of actual exposure to IRA-generated content rather than relying on self-report or aggregate estimates. They then conducted quantitative analyses relating measured exposure to attitudinal and voting-behavior outcomes.

## Findings

- Exposure to IRA content was highly concentrated: a small fraction of users accounted for the vast majority of exposure.
- Exposure tracked pre-existing partisanship, concentrated among strong partisans and especially Republicans.
- No measurable association was found between IRA exposure and shifts in attitudes, polarization, or vote choice.

## Connections

This paper sits within a broader literature that empirically deflates alarmist claims about disinformation reach and persuasive effects, resonating with work on the concentrated, partisan consumption of low-quality and fake news such as [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], and [[Guess2020-rr]], as well as measured skepticism about misinformation's causal impact in [[Budak2024-ef]] and [[Allen2024-av]]. Its focus on the mechanics and audience of foreign information operations connects it to accounts of coordinated campaigns like [[Starbird2019-qv]] and studies of IRA activity and reach such as [[Freelon2024-sc]].
