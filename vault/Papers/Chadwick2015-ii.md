# Politics in the age of hybrid media

> Chadwick, A., Dennis, J., & Smith, A. P. (2015). Politics in the age of hybrid media. *The Routledge Companion to Social Media and Politics*, 7–22. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315716299-2
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315716299-2)

## Summary

This chapter advances the **hybrid media system** as the central analytic lens for understanding contemporary political communication in Western democracies. Its core argument is that older and newer media do not simply displace one another; instead they interact, adapt, and coevolve in a "chaotic transition" that produces a more polycentric environment. Building on the ontology of hybridity, the authors organize their framework around three concepts — power, systems, and media logics — and apply it across news and journalism, election campaigning, and engagement/mobilization. Surveying a growing body of cross-national empirical work, they conclude that today's arrangements are, on balance, more inclusive than the twentieth-century broadcast/newspaper duopoly, while insisting that participation is not the same as power.

## Key Contributions

- Consolidates and extends the hybrid media system framework as a lens spanning journalism, campaigning, and mobilization.
- Pluralizes the classic singular "media logic" into **hybrid media logics** — bundles of technologies, genres, norms, behaviors, and organizational forms — suited to a post-broadcast, polycentric environment.
- Introduces the concept of **political information cycles** (as opposed to traditional news cycles) and foregrounds **temporal power** — the mastery of timing and real-time intervention — as an under-researched object of study.
- Reframes power as relational and empirically observable through actor–technology interactions rather than assumed from structural snapshots.
- Moves debate beyond utopian/dystopian and displacement/absorption dichotomies toward a "not only, but also" account of coevolution.

## Methods

The chapter is a conceptual and theoretical synthesis, drawing hybridity from across disciplines (hybrid regimes, hybrid genres, hybrid organizations, actor-network theory) and reworking established concepts of power (Castells, Dahl), systems (Easton, McNair), and media logic (Altheide and Snow, Dahlgren) through assemblage theory. The authors advocate a mixed-methods repertoire — conceptual work, historical and documentary analysis, real-time "live" online research, and insider ethnography — and review emerging empirical studies from Germany, Norway, the US, UK, Spain, Portugal, Italy, and Sweden. Illustrative case episodes include Obama's 2008 campaign, the Jeremiah Wright/Sarah Palin coverage, the Snowden/Guardian webchat, and Kony2012.

## Findings

- Obama's 2008 campaign integrated online, broadcast, and physical-space activism (raising $500m of $750m online yet spending only 4% of the ad budget online), showing the persistent power of older media.
- Twitter around televised debates follows a hybrid logic that both meshes with and deviates from broadcast events, with celebrities acting as "bridging elites."
- Norwegian journalists rarely use candidates' social media as sources; larger parties still generate news through traditional media events.
- News production is reshaped by blurring roles, post-digital norms of transparency and risk-taking, the "liminal press," and new organizational forms — yet mass media logic still prevails and television remains dominant even among heavy social media users.
- Hybrid mobilization movements (38 Degrees, Avaaz, MoveOn, GetUp!) strategically switch between media logics and time campaigns to mainstream media prominence.
- Dual screening intensifies around media events, involves real-time frame contestation, and predicts online political participation.
- Hybridity can amplify NGO messages but also cause loss of control over meaning, as with Kony2012's spread beyond its intended audience.

## Connections

This chapter provides the theoretical backbone — hybrid media logics, temporal power, and political information cycles — for much of the empirical work in the register on election campaigning and platform politics. It resonates with structural accounts of connective action and networked publics such as [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Bennett2018-ys], and its concern with elite adaptation and the limits of participatory power connects to research on partisan asymmetry and platform dynamics like [[Tornberg2025-ir]] and [[Osmundsen2021-et]] (elite-driven sharing). The insistence that participation does not equal power also frames debates over algorithmic curation and exposure examined in [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] and [[Guess2023-ai]].
