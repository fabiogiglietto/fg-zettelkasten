# Digital & Computational Research Methods

## The Line of Argument

This body of work maps a methodological toolkit built around one central tension: the tools and infrastructures for studying digital media are simultaneously becoming more powerful and more precarious. Two arcs run through the collection — one tracing what generative AI and computational techniques now make *possible* for social science methodology, and another documenting how platform infrastructures are making the underlying data increasingly *inaccessible or distorted*. Read together, the notes filed here sketch a course syllabus in miniature: how to compute with digital trace data, and how to be honest about its limits.

### Generative AI as a methods layer

A cluster of papers treats LLMs and vision-language models not as objects of study but as instruments to be integrated into research pipelines, with careful attention to validation. [[Ober2026-vd]] combines topic modeling with LLM prompt engineering in a human-in-the-loop workflow for qualitative interview coding, arguing that transparency-preserving clustering plus LLM-assisted labeling can scale interpretive analysis without abandoning it to opacity. [[Arminio2025-tw]] pushes a parallel logic into the visual domain, showing that vision-LLMs generating connotative textual descriptions of images outperform CNN-based pipelines for the kind of socially-meaningful image clustering communication scholars need — directly relevant to the multimodal turn documented in [[Gardam2025-er]] and [[Bruns2025-fz]], both of which argue that text-first methods (word counts, hairball network graphs) misrepresent image- and interaction-heavy platforms. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] and [[Meher2025-qb]] extend this integration to text classification and clustering of political content — fine-tuning open-weight or proprietary models for domain-specific tasks (Italian political URLs, terrorism event types) — while explicitly wrestling with what "validation" even means once the model itself does clustering, labeling, and coding rather than merely assisting a human coder. Across this cluster a shared conviction emerges: LLMs are useful precisely to the extent that human oversight, task-specific validation, and interpretability (TF-IDF summaries, expert coders, prompt-robustness checks) are built back in.

[[Alizadeh2026-es]] takes this logic to its logical extreme, asking whether AI coding agents can themselves reproduce published social science findings — with high but conditional success, undercut by sycophantic behavior under confirmatory prompting. This paper functions as a kind of meta-commentary on the whole cluster: if agents can debug, run, and interpret others' pipelines with near-human competence, the boundary between "tool" and "methodological collaborator" is dissolving, but so is the guarantee that reproduction implies independent verification.

### Measurement validity under the hood

A second thread interrogates the infrastructural layer beneath any of these pipelines: what exactly is being measured when we scrape, embed, or sample. [[Fan2025-ut]] shows that pretrained text embeddings encode confounding artifacts of source and language that bias similarity-based analyses, and offers linear concept erasure as a cheap corrective — a caution relevant to any embedding-based clustering step used elsewhere in this topic (e.g. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], [[Arminio2025-tw]]). [[Ulloa2024-jm]] performs an analogous service for web-tracking research, demonstrating that ex-situ scraping — the default method in computational communication research — introduces large, systematically-biased content discrepancies unrelated to the temporal-delay problem researchers have worried about, undermining any downstream classification of news content. [[Fan2026-af]] reframes digital trace data itself, arguing that communication research's habitual aggregation of timestamped behavioral data into cross-sectional variables discards exactly the temporal, sequential information that could reveal media-use dynamics, and surveys six computational approaches (sequence analysis, HMMs, process mining, embedding models) for recovering it.

### The post-API condition and its research consequences

The largest thread documents the collapse of open platform data access and its methodological fallout, forming an evolving diagnostic literature that this hub effectively organizes chronologically. [[Freelon2024-sc]] anchors the arc with a periodized history of the "post-API age," and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] extend the survey to the current landscape of tools and access regimes, the latter naming the "price, proficiency, or permission" triad that structures who can do election research at all. [[Bruns2026-pn]] deepens this critique by examining the "clean room" model (principally Meta's Content Library) as a governance mechanism that nominally satisfies DSA transparency mandates while structurally excluding qualitative, cross-platform, and under-resourced research. [[Rieder2025-ju]] and [[Zheng2026-bi]] supply empirical audits of specific platform APIs — YouTube's search endpoint shown to be "forgetful by design," and TubeStats/TokStats built as compensatory infrastructure providing defensible random samples where the platforms themselves offer none. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review closes the loop empirically, showing across fifteen years of publishing that platform API access directly shapes the empirical footprint of social media research itself: usage rose with API generosity and has plateaued or declined since 2022 as access has tightened.

### Why this matters as a teaching toolkit

Taken together, these seventeen papers do not just describe methods — they enact a pedagogy. They move from building tools (embedding pipelines, fine-tuned LLMs, clustering methods, sampling infrastructure) to auditing tools (API behavior, scraping validity, embedding confounds) to reflecting on the political economy that governs whether any of this infrastructure remains usable. A course or supervision practice modeled on this note would need to teach not just how to run a topic model or fine-tune an LLM, but how to interrogate the API, sample, or embedding space that a computational method silently assumes is neutral — treating methodological literacy and infrastructural critique as inseparable parts of the same skillset.
