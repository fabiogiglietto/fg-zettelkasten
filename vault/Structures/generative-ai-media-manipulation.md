# Generative AI & Media Manipulation

## The anchor case: industrializing visual persuasion

The centre of gravity for this topic is [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]], which supplies the empirical anchor the rest of the literature circles around: a corpus of over 2,300 images from 223 coordinated Facebook gambling groups showing an exponential, structurally-broken jump in posting volume that coincides almost exactly with ChatGPT's public release. The paper's real contribution is not just documenting scale but showing *how* generative AI is absorbed into an existing rhetoric of persuasion — aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement, cultural localization — rather than replacing it. GenAI here behaves as an amplifier and recombiner of established visual drivers, producing a hyper-real, dreamlike aesthetic that slips through regulatory gaps between Meta's tightly-policed paid ads and its loosely-governed organic content. This finding — that the danger lies in amplification infrastructure as much as in content itself — becomes a recurring thesis across the topic, echoed in [[Schroeder2026-im]]'s argument that malicious "AI swarms" threaten democracy less through raw persuasive power than through their capacity to fabricate consensus and colonize platform architectures at scale, and in [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s account of "synthetic realities" as layered systems (content, identity, interaction, institutions) rather than isolated fake artifacts.

## Coordinated operations: from troll farms to agentic swarms

A second thread traces the organizational evolution of influence operations. Poliakoff2026-9b6a992d's OSINT reconstruction of the St Petersburg Internet Research Agency grounds the field historically, showing that even the most mythologized "troll factory" was a mundane, labor-market-integrated PR/media operation — young, inexperienced staff, standard hierarchy, driven initially by the Crimea annexation rather than the 2016 US election. This human-labor baseline throws into relief what [[Schroeder2026-im]] calls a "qualitative leap": the fusion of LLMs with multi-agent architectures that removes the cost, cadence, and coordination constraints that limited IRA-style operations, enabling persistent, adaptive, cross-platform "swarms" capable of infiltrating communities and even poisoning future training data ("LLM grooming"). [[Beacken2026-zb]] complicates any single narrative of AI-driven propaganda by comparing six democratically weakened states, arguing that GenAI adoption for propaganda is structurally contingent rather than a uniform technological given — a useful corrective against the deterministic tone of much swarm and synthetic-reality literature.

## The mechanics and limits of AI persuasion

A distinct but connected cluster interrogates *why* AI-generated content persuades, using large controlled experiments rather than case studies. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] isolates information density — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims — as the dominant mechanism behind conversational AI's persuasiveness, more powerful than model scale or personalization, but shows this comes at the cost of factual accuracy: the same levers that persuade also mislead. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] extends this logic against elite human opponents, showing frontier AI out-persuading professional canvassers and championship debaters, again traceable to fact-density/throughput rather than rapport or perceived humanness — and showing that constraining AI to human writing speed collapses its advantage. Together these two papers reframe the "manipulation" fear articulated qualitatively in [[Schroeder2026-im]] and [[Emilio2026-ik]] into a measurable, mechanistic claim: scale of plausible-sounding claims, not novel psychological techniques, is what generative AI weaponizes.

## Deepfakes, fraud, and the scam economy

Adjacent to influence operations proper is a strand on AI-enabled financial deception. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] maps the global "scam ecosystem" of deepfake financial fraud, arguing regulatory responsibility should shift from victims to platform/telecom/financial gatekeepers, a framing that resonates with [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]'s point about amplification infrastructure and regulatory gaps. [[Swartz2026-zb]] widens the lens further, arguing that social media itself has entered a "scam age" in which fraud is not peripheral but constitutive of platform economics — AI tools like deepfakes are simply the newest vector for a much older logic of network scams that blur believer, shill, and victim. [[Baym2026-tr]] situates this same drift within a broader political-economic diagnosis, revisiting a decade of platform critique (wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction) and noting how generative AI intensifies each: user data appropriated for model training, non-consensual synthetic imagery, and a general "crisis of authenticity" that corrodes trust in ordinary social media content.

## Epistemology after generative AI

Several papers step back to ask what "truth" and "fact" even mean once content is synthetically generated rather than retrieved. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] proposes "emergent facts" as a fourth epistemic category alongside evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based facts, arguing GenAI outputs are plausible-but-unverified probabilistic recombinations that demand relational rather than binary assessment. [[Emilio2026-ik]] arrives at a structurally similar diagnosis from a risk-taxonomy angle, naming the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic content proliferates, societies may rationally discount *all* digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself and enabling strategic plausible deniability. Both push the field's object of concern from artifact-level authenticity detection toward systemic "epistemic security," a reframing that also underlies [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s empirical audit of Grokipedia: an AI-generated encyclopedia that is derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically shifts sourcing toward unreliable and blacklisted domains on ideologically charged topics, illustrating in miniature how generative rewriting can quietly launder institutional facticity.

## Audience reception and sensemaking

A final cluster turns from production to reception, asking how ordinary audiences actually process AI-generated deception. [[Choi2026-bz]] finds a "modality-congruent carryover effect," where difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes confidence and vulnerability toward subsequent fakes in the same format. [[Wack2026-bt]], working in a Kenyan electoral context, shows that deepfake impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated through surrounding comments — a "collective sensemaking" argument that complicates purely individual-cognition models of deepfake harm and has particular force in low-resource content-moderation environments. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] offers a useful counterweight to synthetic-media alarmism: in controlled comparison, AI-generated images and decontextualized real video are not uniformly more persuasive than text, and effects are strongly issue-dependent, suggesting that low-tech recontextualization of authentic footage can rival high-tech fabrication. [[Mattis2026-gu]] closes the reception loop from the institutional side, showing that disclosing GenAI use in journalism uniformly depresses perceived trustworthiness, with the penalty steepest for fact-checking tasks — a finding in tension with fact-density-driven persuasion research and a caution to newsroom transparency policy.

## Platform-specific vectors

Rounding out the topic are two platform ethnographies that anchor these dynamics in TikTok's distinctive affordances. [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an under-examined, platform-specific propaganda technique in Ukraine-war disinformation, while [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds Canadian immigration discourse on TikTok skewing pro-immigration overall, even as a minority of anti-immigration content already deploys generative AI for racialized "speculative worldbuilding" — a small but telling instance of the gambling-image playbook (aspirational fantasy, hyper-real fabrication) migrating into political content.

Read together, the eighteen papers trace an arc from a single documented case of AI-supercharged visual persuasion ([[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]) outward into organizational history, mechanistic persuasion science, financial fraud, epistemology, audience psychology, and platform ethnography — converging on a shared diagnosis: generative AI's disinformation threat lies less in any single deceptive artifact than in its capacity to industrialize scale, blur institutional and infrastructural accountability, and erode the very conditions under which audiences and platforms can distinguish evidence from fabrication.
