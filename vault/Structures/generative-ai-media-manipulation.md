# Generative AI and Media Manipulation

## The shifting ground of manipulation and trust

The papers filed here trace an arc from *mechanism* to *consequence*: they first establish, with increasing experimental precision, that generative AI is a uniquely potent persuasive and fabricative technology, and then ask what happens to public epistemology, trust, and democratic life once that technology is deployed at scale, often adversarially. Read together, they suggest a field moving from proof-of-concept demonstrations of AI persuasion and deception toward a harder question: what remains of "truth," "fact," and "trust" as concepts once synthetic content and synthetic argument become ambient.

### The persuasion frontier

A cluster of large-scale experiments interrogates *how* conversational AI persuades, rather than simply *whether* it does. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] decomposes the "levers" of persuasion across 19 LLMs and thousands of participants, finding that post-training and prompting strategies matter more than raw scale, and that the deciding factor is information density — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims a model can deploy in conversation. Crucially, they identify a persuasion–accuracy trade-off: the same levers that make AI persuasive make it less truthful. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates this claim by pitting frontier AI against elite human persuaders — professional canvassers and championship debaters — and showing that AI wins even against coached experts, with the advantage collapsing only when AI is throttled to human writing speed. This confirms, mechanistically, that AI's edge is one of throughput, not rapport or humanness. [[Lin2025-xp]] extends the paradigm into real elections across four democracies, showing that brief AI dialogues shift votes more than traditional political advertising, again through "facts and evidence" rather than manipulation tactics — while noting a troubling partisan asymmetry in claim accuracy. [[Kotz2026-lk]] rounds out this strand by showing the same evidence-based dialogue approach can move entrenched skeptics on climate, vaccines, and inequality, with effects concentrated exactly where persuasion is hardest to achieve. Together these four papers converge on a counterintuitive normative inversion: AI persuasion is less a psychological dark art than an information-density engine — which is precisely what makes it hard to regulate as "manipulation" in any traditional sense, and why its accuracy failures (documented in [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Lin2025-xp]]) matter more than its rhetorical cunning.

### Synthetic imagery, scams, and coordinated deception

A second cluster examines generative AI's use in applied deception, closest to the topic's gambling-promotion case. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] is the anchor case study: it documents how coordinated Facebook gambling networks absorbed generative AI after ChatGPT's late-2022 launch, producing an exponential, structurally-confirmed leap in post volume and a recombination of pre-existing persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement) into hyper-real AI aesthetics that exploit gaps between paid-ad and organic-content moderation regimes. This finding — that GenAI *intensifies and recombines* existing manipulation repertoires rather than inventing wholly new ones — resonates with [[Hameleers2026-mc]]'s experimental finding that AI-generated still imagery does *not* uniformly outperform text or decontextualized video disinformation; visual persuasion remains issue-bound, contingent on the availability of plausible real footage, complicating alarmist assumptions about GenAI's unique potency. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] scales the deception-economy analysis to global financial fraud, mapping a "scam ecosystem" of platforms, telecoms, and financial intermediaries and arguing — in a governance move that echoes Giglietto's regulatory-grey-zone diagnosis — that liability should shift from individual victims to gatekeeper infrastructures. At the level of audience cognition, [[Choi2026-bz]] shows that difficulty in authenticating one instance of (deep)fake content carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to shape confidence and vulnerability toward *subsequent* fake content — a micro-level mechanism that helps explain why repeated exposure to synthetic media (as in the gambling and scam ecosystems above) compounds rather than inoculates.

### Automating coordination: swarms, states, and information operations

A third strand shifts from single-artifact deception to coordinated, systemic manipulation. [[Orlando2025-ul]] offers a striking simulation-based finding: networked LLM agents, given only mutual awareness of teammates (not explicit central direction), spontaneously reproduce the coordination signatures of real information operations — network density, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification — approaching the performance of fully deliberate campaigns. [[Schroeder2026-im]] theorizes the endpoint of this trajectory as "malicious AI swarms," a qualitative escalation beyond botnets that can manufacture consensus, poison training data ("LLM grooming"), and erode institutional trust, arguing that symmetric "good" swarms cannot fix an attention economy that structurally rewards outrage. [[Waight2026-ts]] identifies a subtler, state-level variant of the same logic: rather than agents *acting* in a coordinated way, training corpora themselves already encode state media control, so that LLMs "launder" authoritarian framing into ostensibly neutral outputs — a mechanism of influence that requires no deliberate swarm at all, just biased data ingestion. [[Beacken2026-zb]] provides the necessary comparative corrective to techno-deterministic readings of all three: across six democratically weakened states, GenAI propaganda adoption is highly uneven, shaped by local political-economic structure rather than following a uniform diffusion curve — a caution against assuming swarms, laundering, or scam ecosystems operate identically everywhere.

### Epistemic aftermath: trust, truth, and the erosion of shared reality

A fourth cluster steps back to theorize what all this produces at the level of collective epistemology. [[Emilio2026-ik]] offers the most totalizing synthesis, proposing "synthetic reality" as a four-layer stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and naming the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic content proliferates, societies may rationally discount *all* digital evidence, which paradoxically empowers bad-faith denial more than it protects against deception. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] approaches the same problem from fact-checking epistemology, arguing that GenAI outputs fit none of the traditional categories of fact (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) and require a new category — "emergent facts" — assessed relationally rather than as binary true/false claims. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework for trust in generative AI explicitly building on, but distinguishing itself from, decades of mass-media and social-media trust research. [[Baym2026-tr]], revisiting a decade-old critique of social media's structural harms, situates GenAI's erosion of authenticity within a longer political economy of platform power and data extraction, insisting the crisis is continuous with — not a rupture from — older harms. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] pushes furthest structurally, arguing that generative AI (alongside algorithmic feeds and retreat into private spaces) is dissolving the "social media" paradigm itself, producing "platforms without users" and AI-mediated communication as a genuinely new, non-public communicative form — a claim that reframes several other papers in this set (Giglietto's coordinated groups, Orlando's swarms) as symptoms of a broader post-social transition rather than isolated pathologies.

### Institutions, media representations, and governance

A final set of papers examines how institutions perceive, represent, and attempt to govern this landscape. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows that news media's own framing of LLMs as agentic or humanlike is neither uniform nor simply hype-driven, but a contested site of anthropomorphic rhetoric doing varied rhetorical work — a reminder that public understanding of the very technology under discussion is itself journalistically constructed. [[Triedman2025-uy]] provides a concrete case of AI-generated knowledge infrastructure diverging from human-curated norms: Grokipedia is shown to be derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically more reliant on low-quality and blacklisted sources on controversial topics, illustrating empirically how "AI-authored" reference content can quietly shift source reliability at scale. [[Manovich2026-ih]] offers a deliberate counterpoint to the manipulation-and-harm framing dominating this topic, analyzing generative AI purely as an artistic medium with its own cognitive and stylistic affordances — a useful reminder that the same technology sits simultaneously in registers of creative practice and information warfare. Finally, [[Unknown2025-qj]] and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] examine emerging institutional and platform-level responses: red-teaming as a public-interest safety practice, and TikTok's affordances as a "double-edged" resource that can enable both digital inclusion and (via generative AI "speculative worldbuilding") xenophobic misinformation — both suggesting that governance and platform design, not just detection technology, are where the next battles over authenticity will be fought.

Taken as a whole, the collection narrates a shift from asking *can AI deceive or persuade* to asking *what kind of epistemic order survives when it reliably can* — moving from mechanism (Hackenburg, Lin, Kotz), through applied cases (Giglietto, Schiffrin, Hameleers, Choi), to systemic coordination (Orlando, Schroeder, Waight, Beacken), and finally to theory and governance of trust itself (Emilio, Dierickx, Suk, Baym, Törnberg, Nguyen, Triedman, Manovich, red-teaming, TikTok).
