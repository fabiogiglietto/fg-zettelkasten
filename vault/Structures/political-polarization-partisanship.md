# Political Polarization & Partisan Engagement

## From networked publics to algorithmic clusters

The historical throughline of this literature begins with the question of exposure: do social media structurally insulate partisans from each other, or does insularity emerge from choice? [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the field's foundational empirical benchmark, showing that Facebook's News Feed modestly suppresses cross-cutting content but that individual clicking behavior does so more, a finding that has anchored two decades of "it's us, not the algorithm" argumentation. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] extend this pre-recommender-era picture into hybrid media systems, showing how Twitter functioned as a second screen to television and as a site of dissenting counter-hashtags, respectively — evidence that partisan communicative insularity long predates algorithmic curation. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] then bridges this older Twitter-Facebook axis directly to the populist moment, demonstrating that insularity is not uniform but concentrated among populist-aligned sources (M5S, the League) whose supporters alternate between amplifying favorable coverage and contesting unfavorable coverage — a strategic, rather than merely structural, form of partisan engagement that anticipates the Bolsonaro case below.

[[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] argues that this whole "networked publics" paradigm is now historically bounded: TikTok-style platforms replace explicit, people-centric friend graphs with implicit, item-centric "clustered publics," a morphological shift that recasts questions of insularity as questions of algorithmic classification rather than homophilous choice. This reframing sets up the topic's central empirical battleground — whether recommender systems themselves manufacture partisan sorting. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] supplies the most decisive causal evidence to date: switching users onto X's algorithmic feed shifts political attitudes rightward and, crucially, does so asymmetrically — turning the algorithm off does not reverse the effect, because the algorithm has already changed who users choose to follow. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complements this from an audit perspective, showing that apparent right-leaning algorithmic bias on post-Musk Twitter is largely explained by behavioral proxies (agitating content, proximity to Musk) rather than ideology per se, while documenting a broader collapse of the platform's town-square function around a single centralizing node. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] pushes the mechanism further inward, demonstrating that X's embedding space inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear representation of Left-Right ideology even without being designed to, raising both scientific and regulatory stakes. [[Brown2026-br]] offers a more skeptical counterweight on YouTube, formally distinguishing echo chambers, rabbit holes, and radicalization pathways and finding strong evidence only for content-level rabbit holes, not for algorithmically-induced ideological echo chambers or radicalization — a useful corrective against over-generalizing from the X findings. [[Lai2024-to]] provides a methodological bridge across this cluster, showing that video-level (not just channel- or user-level) ideology can be recovered from Reddit sharing patterns, extending ideal-point methods into content itself.

## Echo chambers reconsidered: measurement, curation, and sedimentation

A second thread interrogates whether "echo chamber" is even a coherent, stable construct. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 studies concludes that much apparent disagreement in this literature is an artifact of incompatible conceptualizations and operationalizations rather than a real empirical dispute — a meta-level diagnosis that resonates with [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s parallel finding that depolarization *recommendations* are almost never actually derived from the polarization *evidence* scholars themselves produce, resting instead on unexamined deliberative-democratic ideals. Both papers converge on a call for reflexivity about how normative commitments and methodological choices shape conclusions, a concern sharpened empirically by [[Schemer2026-mh]], whose specification-curve analysis of 504 measurement combinations shows that the strength (though not direction) of partisan-media-polarization relationships is highly sensitive to how slant and polarization are operationalized — audience-based measures inflate effects relative to content-based ones, and conservative-leaning associations are systematically stronger than liberal ones.

Substantively, [[Dehghan2026-sy]] offers the sharpest challenge to naive cross-cutting-exposure optimism: even discursively resonant political subreddits show minimal user, source, and linking overlap, suggesting polarization is "sedimented" into platform architecture, moderation, and discourse jointly rather than caused by any single factor — directly undercutting the idea that spaces like r/politics function as Habermasian town squares. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates the whole domain-level tradition (including Bakshy et al.'s and Eady et al.'s methods below) by showing that story-level partisan audiences diverge sharply from their parent domain's average, especially for "moderate" outlets, meaning source-level echo-chamber measures systematically mistake heterogeneity for moderation. [[Mosleh2024-op]] extends the critique geographically, showing that engagement with partisan and low-quality news diverges substantially across seven platforms, undermining Twitter/Facebook-centric generalizations that dominate this literature — a point [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] generalizes still further into a call for globally equitable polarization research beyond the US-centric default. [[Oswald2025-km]] adds a demand-side caveat relevant to all of the above: because a small minority of highly active users produce most visible political content, both citizens and researchers risk mistaking this "tip of the iceberg" for public opinion itself — a caution that should temper any inference drawn from posting- or engagement-based polarization metrics. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] gesture toward adjacent but underspecified questions — whether alternative platforms independently drive discursive polarization, and how unsupervised methods might surface latent framing structures — that remain largely programmatic within this cluster.

## Affective engagement, emotion, and the Bolsonaro case

The topic's empirical center of gravity is the study of affective polarization as it plays out in partisan engagement data, and here [[Marino2026-he]] is the anchor text. Its three-year longitudinal analysis of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook communities overturns the assumption — implicit in much cross-sectional echo-chamber work above — that hyperpartisan communities are stable and impermeable; instead, affective engagement toward in-group and out-group actors is highly unstable, event-driven, and marked by a decisive 2023 inflection (Lula's inauguration, the January coup attempt) toward declining emotional intensity and rising internal debate. This longitudinal, Global-South-centered approach directly answers [[Falkenberg2026-ka]]'s call and extends [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]'s insight that populist communities strategically modulate sharing versus commenting according to valence, now showing that even this strategic modulation itself decays over time.

Complementary Scandinavian and Norwegian populist case studies extend the emotion-engagement link with more granular platform mechanics. [[Larsson2026-ro]] traces a decade of Progress Party Facebook posts and finds negative sentiment — intensified under a new, more combative leader — reliably drives shares and comments while positive content drives likes, suggesting these engagement types follow distinct affective logics. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] generalizes this across Norway, Sweden, and Denmark: populist parties disproportionately elicit "Angry" reactions, and anger (not love) predicts sharing, a pattern [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review confirms as a cross-study regularity — sociopolitical content skews toward a broader, more negative emotional spectrum than lifestyle content, with anger-driven engagement especially pronounced for far-right and populist actors. Together these studies establish emotion, and specifically anger, as a load-bearing mechanism connecting content, platform affordances, and amplification — the affective analogue to the algorithmic amplification findings above.

Normatively and diagnostically, [[Esau2025-tf]] theorizes "destructive polarization" as a target concept and proposes deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening as an antidote, echoing the Habermasian ideals that [[van-Eck2026-xg]] found underlying most depolarization prescriptions. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a subtler psychological layer beneath this affective landscape: a "hostile misinformation effect" in which partisans across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by misinformation, amplified by political interest, identity strength, and — notably — right-wing orientation, with downstream costs to media trust and perceived electoral integrity. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows a structurally similar asymmetry at the level of media narrative: exposure to stories about election interference does not raise polarization on average, but does so specifically when the *opposing* party is portrayed as benefiting, again locating the polarizing mechanism in partisan identity-matching rather than information content per se.

## Elite radicalization and asymmetric partisanship

A distinct but connected strand asks whether polarization is elite-driven and asymmetric rather than a mass, bottom-up phenomenon. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" shows how the sudden rise of Critical Race Theory as a Republican talking point tracks a subgroup of more ideologically extreme, digitally networked legislators who are rewarded with disproportionate right-wing media attention and engagement — a mechanism [[Bennett2025-xs]] generalizes into a broader theory of "digital surrogate organizations" that pull conservative parties toward illiberalism, explicitly arguing that individual-level disinformation research (the dominant paradigm funded post-2016) has occluded this institutional, organizational story. [[Eady2025-vm]]'s common-scale ideology measurement across politicians, media, and the public supports this asymmetry empirically: ideologically extreme legislators share disproportionately more and more extreme news, and reduced electoral competition predicts more polarized elite sharing — evidence that elite behavior, not just algorithmic exposure, drives the polarized information environment. [[Tai2026-qk]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]], though thinner on detail, extend this elite focus to institutional checks on politicians' misinformation sharing and to how far-right electoral performance shapes voter disinformation concerns, while [[Iris2026-pg]]'s cross-national content analysis documents a parallel media-side phenomenon: radical-right parties receive systematically disproportionate news visibility relative to their actual electoral strength across most studied European countries, a structural amplification independent of any platform algorithm.

Partisan asymmetry recurs sharply in the platform-governance literature. [[Renault2025-uh]] finds Republican posts flagged 2.3 times more often than Democratic posts under X's crowdsourced Community Notes — an asymmetry the authors argue cannot be dismissed as fact-checker bias, directly contesting Musk's and Zuckerberg's stated rationale for abandoning professional fact-checking. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] extends this into a structural critique: because Community Notes requires cross-partisan agreement to surface a note, the system is designed to undermoderate precisely the most polarizing content, with acute implications for electoral integrity as the system is exported across 13 countries. [[Ventura2026-yc]] shows a related dynamic playing out at the level of state action rather than platform design: Brazil's 2024 ban on X was disproportionately circumvented by conservative users while liberal users went silent, producing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" in the platform's news environment — a striking demonstration that democratic content-moderation interventions can themselves become instruments of partisan realignment, echoing the persistence mechanism [[Gauthier2026-iq]] documents for algorithmic feed changes. [[Kim2026-wg]] rounds out this asymmetry literature with evidence of geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising in 2016, showing that heterogeneous, targeted treatment effects (rather than average effects) are necessary to detect real electoral harms — a methodological lesson later echoed in this cluster's emphasis on subgroup and specification-level analysis.

## Disinformation as narrative, identity, and boundary work

Running parallel to the structural and algorithmic threads is a cultural-narrative reframing of disinformation itself. [[Marwick2026-qd]] argues that successful disinformation should be understood as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *story* rather than discrete false claims — a framing that explains why fact-checking and domain-level moderation persistently fail, since narratives migrate across genres via catchphrases and visual shorthand. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] extends this narrative-identity logic to conspiracy culture, showing that "Great Reset" communities across six platforms are held together less by shared ideological content than by symbolic boundary work — reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor and converting personal grievance into collective, affectively bonded opposition to a "mainstream other." [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes a cognate insight computationally, introducing "narrative polarisation" (via Greimas' actantial model and LLM annotation) to show that YouTube videos on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict are highly polarized in how they assign roles to actors, even though viewer comments substantially converge — suggesting narrative and audience polarization can decouple. [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s evidence-frame framework makes a structurally similar point about election-integrity rumors: misleadingness arises from the interaction of (often accurate) evidence with partisan frames supplied via quote-tweet "call and response," not from fabricated facts per se. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows the same dynamic at the level of metadiscourse, finding that both left- and right-wing hyperpartisan outlets deploy "fake news" as a delegitimizing rhetorical weapon rather than a descriptive term, actively renegotiating Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance.

Several papers extend this narrative-and-source-centered approach to specific empirical or methodological niches: [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that misinformation *source* (influencer versus regular user) independently drives toxic audience response via credibility legitimation and parasocial "community enmeshment," particularly for sociopolitical content on low-pseudonymity platforms; [[Lieu2025-nl]] finds that climate misinformation content category (attacking solutions) matters more than logical-fallacy type for perceived veracity and ideological polarization; [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] documents a UK press analogue in which right- and left-leaning newspapers selectively cite scientists aligned with their editorial line on COVID-19 restrictions, with segregation reproduced even in academic co-authorship and Twitter-sharing networks. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] contribute computational-methodological advances — multimodal and unsupervised frame extraction — designed to detect these narrative and framing dynamics at scale without relying on predefined categories, positioning themselves as infrastructure for the narrative-turn scholarship above.

## AI, persuasion, and the future information environment

A final emerging cluster asks how large language models reshape the partisan information ecosystem this topic has traced from Facebook to Bolsonaro networks to Bluesky. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer political alignment even from nonpolitical discourse, exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary — a capability with clear implications for the microtargeting concerns raised by [[Kim2026-wg]] and the profiling risks raised by [[Bouchaud2026-lr]]. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] demonstrates that frontier AI systems out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the advantage traced to sheer information throughput — raising the stakes on [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]]'s finding that this persuasive power is nevertheless politically contingent, collapsing by roughly a quarter when users merely perceive the model as biased against their party. Read together, these three papers suggest that LLM-mediated political communication will be governed by the same partisan-identity dynamics — trust, perceived bias, motivated reasoning — that structure human media consumption, rather than escaping them.

Finally, two papers offer forward-looking correctives to the algorithmic-amplification story with which this note began. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment shows that engagement-based feeds amplify intergroup, moralized, emotional content and distort perceptions of social norms around political dialogue — but that a purpose-built "diversified extremity" algorithm can reduce this distortion without sacrificing platform enjoyment, offering the clearest experimental proof-of-concept in this collection that algorithmic design, not just algorithmic critique, can be a lever against affective polarization. [[Smith2025-kc]]'s concept of the "attention broker" (tertius amplificans) supplies a general network-theoretic mechanism — amplification-with-attribution accelerating tie formation — that helps explain *why* algorithmic and elite amplification (Musk's centrality in [[Efstratiou2025-gs]], populist leaders' engagement advantage in [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]) so reliably compounds into the structural polarization this whole literature is trying to measure, explain, and — per [[van-Eck2026-xg]] and [[Esau2025-tf]] — normatively address.
