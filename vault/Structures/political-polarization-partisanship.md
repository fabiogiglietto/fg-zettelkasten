# Political Polarization & Partisan Media Dynamics

## Foundations: Insularity, Selective Exposure, and Their Limits

The line of inquiry organized here begins from a foundational empirical puzzle: does social media actually sort citizens into ideologically closed communities? [[Bakshy2015-rn]] set the terms early by showing that Facebook's algorithm modestly dampens cross-cutting exposure but that individual clicking behavior does so more, complicating simple "filter bubble" narratives. That ambivalence recurs throughout the corpus. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] operationalizes "insularity" directly in the Italian 2018 campaign, finding that populist-aligned news sources (M5S, the League) are more insular than mainstream ones, and that insularity itself predicts a distinctive Facebook signature—amplification via shares versus contestation via comments. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] extends this logic to Reddit, showing that even discursively similar subreddits share almost no users, cross-posts, or links, arguing polarization is "sedimented" as a structural precondition rather than emergent from discourse alone. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates the measurement further: domain-level partisan scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, meaning "curation bubbles" are built from unbundled and rebundled stories rather than from source loyalty per se. [[Zhu2026-tn]] shows this insularity is context-dependent cross-nationally—alternative-mainstream audience overlap is generally low but rises sharply where far-right parties have institutionalized political access, producing Sweden's isolated alternative-media enclave as an instructive outlier. Meta-level reviews temper the whole picture: [[Hartmann2025-px]] argues that disagreement over whether echo chambers even exist stems largely from inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization, while [[Oswald2025-km]] and [[Schulz2026-ts]] independently argue that visible online discourse—dominated by a small, more extreme, more vocal minority—systematically manufactures an appearance of polarization that outstrips actual population-level attitudes. [[Mosleh2024-op]] adds that these dynamics are not uniform even across platforms, undercutting Twitter/Facebook-centric generalizations, a critique echoed methodologically by [[Gaisbauer2025-by]], which calls for multi-level (story/outlet/content) cartographies of news circulation rather than single-dimension partisan-leaning scores.

## National Laboratories: Italy, Germany, Brazil, and the Cross-European Frame

A second strand grounds these general dynamics in specific electoral contexts that recur across the corpus. Italy anchors an early hybrid-media perspective: [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] documents how Twitter functioned as a second screen to televised talk shows in the 2013 election, with narrow, TV-driven participation rather than agenda-setting from below—an argument later sharpened by [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] on the 2018 election and complemented by discourse-level studies of Italian political language and rhetoric, including [[Paci2025-ag]] on LLMs' struggles with implicit meaning in Italian political speech and [[Mosca2026-yh]], which reframes "fake news" accusations in the Italian press as strategic weapons of negative campaigning rather than symptoms of misinformation. Germany appears through both methodological and substantive lenses: [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs for analyzing visual self-presentation in the 2021 federal campaign, while [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] situates Germany alongside the Netherlands and Poland to document a "hostile misinformation effect" around the 2024 EU elections. Brazil emerges as the corpus's richest single-country cluster, tracing an arc from elite narrative contestation ([[Rodarte2026-dk]] on the Manaus crisis) through platform governance shocks ([[Ventura2026-yc]] on the X ban's rightward "sorting ratchet"; [[Ventura2025-sw]] on WhatsApp deactivation) to the consequences of disinformation for democratic norms ([[Rossini2026-jn]] on intolerance; [[Rossini2026-mj]] on trust in the 2022 election results) and finally to longitudinal affective dynamics within a hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro network ([[Marino2026-he]]), which finds that even seemingly closed communities are volatile and event-sensitive rather than stable echo chambers. The cross-European frame is completed by comparative work on the 2024 EP elections: [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate media visibility for the Radical Right across five countries, [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance to citizens' disinformation concerns, and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] issues a programmatic call for polarization research to move beyond its Western, especially U.S., skew toward genuinely global comparison—a call this very set of national case studies partially answers.

## The Affective Turn: Emotions, Reactions, and Hostility

Running alongside issue- and ideology-based polarization is an affective register concerned with how emotion structures engagement and hostility. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] shows that "Angry" reactions on Facebook—disproportionately elicited by populist parties across Norway, Sweden, and Denmark—drive sharing more than "Love" reactions do, a pattern corroborated at the level of individual leadership by [[Larsson2026-ro]], which traces a decade of Norway's Progress Party communication toward increasing negativity under a new leader, and synthesized more broadly by the systematic review [[Anwar2024-34dba628]], which finds sociopolitical content elicits a wider, more negative emotional spectrum than lifestyle content. [[Marino2026-he]] extends this affective lens longitudinally, showing that emotional intensity toward in- and out-group actors in Bolsonarist networks is highly unstable and tied to political shocks rather than fixed ideological identity. At the level of perception rather than expression, [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a parallel "hostile misinformation effect"—partisans believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by falsehoods—moderated by political interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation, with downstream costs to media trust and perceived electoral integrity. Normatively, [[Esau2025-tf]] proposes "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as theoretical antidotes to what it terms "destructive polarization," bridging affective-polarization findings with deliberative-democratic theory—an ambition that resonates with [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s critical finding that most scholarly depolarization recommendations are not actually derived from the evidence they cite, but from unstated deliberative-democracy ideals.

## Platforms as Architects: Algorithms, Feeds, and Governance

A substantial cluster interrogates the platform infrastructures that mediate all of the above. Field experiments provide the strongest causal evidence: [[Gauthier2026-iq]] shows X's algorithmic feed shifts users rightward via a persistent "follow" mechanism that survives algorithm deactivation, while [[Brady2026-ln]] demonstrates on Bluesky that engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup, moralized, emotional content and distorts perceived social norms—effects a purpose-built "diversified extremity" algorithm can partially reverse. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] both push back against "black box" framings of Facebook's News Feed, showing instead that visibility for partisan versus quality journalistic content fluctuates in step with documented governance interventions (e.g., 2020 "break the glass" measures), positioning the platform as an active, historically contingent curator. [[Votta2025-xz]] extends this governance lens to political advertising, arguing that ad-delivery algorithms opaquely shape campaign reach and cost. Compliance and circumvention of platform governance itself becomes a polarizing force in [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s account of Brazil's X ban, and content-moderation design is directly implicated in [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]], both examining X's Community Notes: the former shows Republicans are flagged 2.3 times more often than Democrats even under a bridging algorithm, while the latter argues that bridging-based systems structurally undermoderate exactly the polarizing content most relevant to electoral integrity. Cross-platform visual and campaign strategy work—[[Bouchafra2026-ts]] on the Sweden Democrats' platform-tailored securitizing memes and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] on TikTok immigration discourse—shows how affordances shape not just exposure but the rhetorical *form* polarized content takes, while [[Zhao2025-ny]]'s treatment of hashtag hijacking and [[Kristensen2025-ni]]'s inquiry into whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization extend the governance question to platform choice itself.

## Misinformation, Fake News, and Epistemic Contestation

A closely related but distinct thread treats misinformation not as a discrete factual failure but as a strategic, narrative, and elite-driven phenomenon that fuels polarization. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country analysis of parliamentarians' tweets finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing—only their conjunction, radical-right populism, does—an argument for treating disinformation as politically strategic rather than a generic information-quality problem, echoed by [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s account of how "CRT Republicans" gain attention and radicalize by connecting to hyper-partisan digital surrogates, and by [[Bennett2025-xs]]'s broader "logic of connective faction," which recasts platform-enabled organization (not just persuasion) as the missing link in democratic backsliding. [[Tai2026-qk]] narrows this to institutional checks on U.S. elected officials' misinformation sharing. Narrative-centered approaches deepen the picture: [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] both show that election-integrity rumors are less about false facts than about the interaction of (often accurate) evidence with partisan interpretive frames and "deep stories" collaboratively performed by influencers, elites, and audiences; [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] finds that repeated cross-outlet exposure to recurring disinformation *narratives* (rather than isolated claims) drives belief; and [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes "narrative polarisation" via computational actantial analysis, showing that YouTube videos on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict are sharply polarized in role-assignment even when surface-level audience comments converge. The "fake news" label itself becomes an object of study in [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]], both showing the term functions as a delegitimizing rhetorical weapon rather than a descriptor. Downstream democratic consequences are traced in Brazil ([[Rossini2026-jn]], [[Rossini2026-mj]]), in the U.S. voter-suppression context ([[Kim2026-wg]]), and in the UK, where [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows that *media narratives about* election interference—independent of interference's actual efficacy—can itself generate asymmetric affective and perceived polarization. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] rounds out this cluster by reframing conspiracy communities (the "Great Reset") not as products of algorithmic radicalization but of symbolic boundary work and affective identity construction, a corrective in the same spirit as [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]]'s finding that UK newspapers' politicized citation of COVID-19 scientists reflects media "use" of science more than scientists' own bias, and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s earlier case study of dissenting counter-hashtag discourse after Charlie Hebdo.

## Elite Strategy, Media Capture, and the Radical-Right Nexus

Several papers push the analytic focus upward from audiences to elites and institutions. [[Balluff2026-bv]] documents Austria's "Inseratenaffäre" as a case of covert media capture via government advertising, showing measurable shifts in visibility and competitor coverage consistent with "paying positive to go negative." [[Iris2026-pg]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] show how radical-right visibility and performance shape both media agendas and mass disinformation concern across Europe, while [[Rodarte2026-dk]] demonstrates how Brazilian parliamentarians actively contest journalistic gatekeeping to construct competing forms of epistemic authority during crisis. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] adds automated non-human actors to this elite ecology, showing social bots function as agenda-builders that disproportionately shape campaign (rather than press) discourse. Together with [[Knupfer2025-vt]], [[Bennett2025-xs]], and [[Tornberg2025-ir]], these studies build a coherent account in which polarization is substantially manufactured and sustained by strategic elite and quasi-elite actors exploiting hybrid media systems—a claim that also runs through [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]'s earlier conceptual mapping of how big data, blurred organizational boundaries, and micro-targeting reshape the practice and study of political communication.

## Measuring Polarization: Computational Tools and Methodological Reckoning

A final, self-reflexive thread interrogates how polarization is measured and modeled. [[Eady2025-vm]] and [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] advance scalable ideology-estimation methods (URL-sharing behavior; LLM-based prompting and averaging) that jointly locate media, politicians, and citizens on common ideological scales, while [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent ideology estimation to YouTube videos via Reddit sharing patterns. [[Lee2026-je]] and [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] probe the political dimensions of LLMs themselves—showing they can infer partisan alignment from nonpolitical text with attendant privacy risks, and that perceived LLM bias undercuts persuasive efficacy along partisan lines. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] extend automated framing analysis beyond fixed categories and single modalities. Most consequentially, [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis of 504 measurement combinations finds that methodological choices about media-slant and polarization operationalization substantially affect the *magnitude* (though not direction) of observed partisan-media–polarization relationships, while [[van-Eck2026-xg]] shows depolarization recommendations are largely evidence-disconnected, and [[Hartmann2025-px]] makes the parallel argument for echo-chamber research. These methodological interventions, together with [[Falkenberg2026-ka]]'s call for global equity, point toward the horizon this literature is building: a set of empirically grounded, cross-nationally comparative, and methodologically self-aware foundations suitable for handbook- and encyclopedia-level synthesis of how partisan alignment, insularity, and affective hostility are produced, amplified, and occasionally checked across today's fragmented, platform-mediated media systems.
