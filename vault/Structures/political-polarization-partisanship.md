# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## The Shape of the Field: Fragmentation as Both Object and Obstacle

Any structure note on polarization must first confront a meta-problem the literature itself keeps surfacing: the field studying fragmentation is itself fragmented. [[Hartmann2025-px]] documents this directly, showing that 129 studies on echo chambers disagree not because the phenomenon is contested but because measurement, region, and platform choices systematically produce divergent findings. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] extends the critique geographically, arguing that polarization research is disproportionately Western and disciplinarily siloed. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] adds a normative twist: even where empirical drivers of polarization are well established, depolarization *recommendations* are largely untethered from evidence, resting instead on unexamined deliberative-democratic ideals. [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis makes the point almost mechanically — 504 operationalizations of "media slant" and "polarization" yield wildly different coefficient magnitudes from the same panel data, though never flipped signs. Together these pieces suggest that much of what looks like scholarly disagreement about *whether* polarization is happening is actually disagreement about *how to look*.

## Echo Chambers Revisited: From Filter Bubbles to Curation and Narrative

The classic empirical arc — Facebook/Twitter studies asking whether platforms trap people in ideological bubbles — remains the topic's backbone. [[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Barbera2015-fw]], [[Flaxman2016-lm]], and [[Messing2014-jc]] collectively pushed back against strong filter-bubble claims in the mid-2010s, showing that individual choice, not algorithmic ranking, does most of the sorting, and that social endorsement can even dilute partisan selectivity. A decade later, [[Nyhan2023-gb]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] — companion papers from the 2020 Facebook/Instagram collaborative studies — complicate this picture: like-minded exposure is prevalent and segregation intensifies at each stage from inventory to engagement, yet experimentally reducing like-minded exposure does not move attitudes. [[Bail2018-fk]]'s bot experiment delivers the more unsettling twist: cross-cutting exposure can *increase* polarization rather than cure it, especially for Republicans — a finding that reverberates through [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s meta-analytic worry that "expose people to the other side" is a normatively comfortable but evidentially shaky prescription.

More recent work complicates the echo-chamber diagnosis further by relocating where the actual sorting happens. [[Green2025-ap]] shows that domain-level partisan-audience scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity — the "curation bubble" is built story-by-story through networked re-sharing, not simply by source choice. [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s Reddit study finds polarization is "sedimented" into platform structure itself — moderation, subreddit splits, and discursive identity together produce cross-ideological silence that can't be reduced to discourse or design alone. [[Elfes2026-jb]] pushes the unit of analysis even further from opinion-based measures, proposing "narrative polarization" — divergence in how actors are cast in stories — and finding, counter-intuitively, that YouTube comments *converge* even while video content remains sharply polarized, with real partisan difference surviving only in deeper narrative motifs. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] arrives at a similar anti-techno-centric conclusion from conspiracy-theory research: "Great Reset" communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity across six platforms, not through platform-specific algorithmic sorting. And [[Schulz2026-ts]] offers perhaps the cleanest reframe: much apparent online polarization is "false polarization," an artifact of who self-selects into posting rather than a real shift in underlying opinion distributions — a claim that dovetails with [[Oswald2025-km]]'s "production-consumption gap," where a small vocal minority's discourse is mistaken for public opinion at large.

## Algorithms, Platforms, and Amplification

A second thread interrogates whether *ranking algorithms themselves* amplify partisan and polarizing content, moving past the "is there a bubble" question to "does the feed cause drift." [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s X field experiment is pivotal: switching users onto the algorithmic "For You" feed shifts attitudes rightward and persists even after the algorithm is switched off, because exposure changes *who users follow* — a mechanism that reconciles the earlier Meta null results ([[Nyhan2023-gb]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]]) with claims of algorithmic amplification. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes a parallel structural shift at the platform level: TikTok's move from "networked publics" (explicit ties) to algorithmically inferred "clustered publics" depersonalizes and further fragments online publics, a transformation other platforms are now imitating. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] examines a governance countermeasure — X's Community Notes — and finds its bridging-based design *structurally* undermoderates the most polarizing content, since such content is precisely what fails to attract cross-partisan agreement; this is echoed at the state level by [[Ventura2026-yc]], whose study of Brazil's X ban shows how a blunt regulatory intervention produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" through asymmetric partisan compliance rather than depolarizing the platform.

## Affective Polarization: Measurement, Drivers, and Elite Amplification

[[Iyengar2019-jj]] supplies the conceptual anchor for the field's affective turn — polarization as identity-based dislike and distrust rather than mere policy disagreement — and much subsequent work operationalizes or extends this. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] traces fake-news sharing to partisan animus rather than ignorance or "chaos" motives, tying affective polarization directly to misinformation behavior. [[Rossini2026-jn]] finds a related dynamic in Brazil, where belief in electoral misinformation predicts *rising* intolerance over an election cycle, and messaging-app news use works indirectly through misinformation belief. [[Marino2026-he]]'s three-year longitudinal study of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook networks complicates the assumption of stable hyperpartisan echo chambers, showing engagement is event-driven and volatile — declining emotional intensity and rising internal debate after Lula's inauguration — a caution against treating "the partisan community" as static. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows that even *narratives about* interference (rather than interference itself) can generate asymmetric affective polarization depending on which side is portrayed as beneficiary, while [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s cross-national "hostile misinformation effect" demonstrates a parallel identity-driven bias: partisans believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by misinformation, moderated by interest, identity strength, and right-leaning orientation.

## Elite and Institutional Amplification: Parties, Media, and "Digital Surrogates"

A distinct strand shifts from mass-level psychology to elite and organizational dynamics. [[Bennett2025-xs]] (with its close variant [[Gillespie2010-as]]) argues that both "technocentric" (algorithm/misinformation) and "institutionalist" (party/elite) explanations of democratic backsliding are individually insufficient, proposing "digital surrogate organizations" as the connective tissue binding fringe networks to party radicalization — illustrated through Trump/MAGA and comparative European cases. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] operationalizes this at finer grain, showing how the sudden elite adoption of "Critical Race Theory" as a talking point reveals a "logic of connective faction": Republicans who adopt hyperpartisan-network-friendly issues are rewarded with engagement and coverage, radicalizing intra-party dynamics. [[Tai2026-qk]] complements this by asking which institutional and ideological positions check or enable elected officials' own misinformation sharing. [[Chadwick2015-ii]] supplies the underlying theoretical scaffolding — the hybrid media system, where older and newer media logics interpenetrate rather than substitute — which [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] apply directly to disinformation, arguing that asymmetric structural features of the American (and broader democratic) media ecosystem, not novel technology or foreign interference per se, best explain post-truth dynamics, and that disinformation flows are themselves symptomatic of institutional legitimacy crises exploited by radical-right actors.

## Media Ecosystems, Insularity, and Cross-Partisan (Non-)Interaction

The Facebook/Twitter/Reddit empirical tradition on insularity — the topic's original empirical core — is carried forward by studies mapping *where* partisan attention concentrates and how cross-cutting interaction (or its absence) shapes discourse. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]'s Italian election study is exemplary: populist-aligned news sources are more insular on Twitter, and insularity correlates with amplification (shares) rather than contestation (comments) on Facebook, with the Five Star Movement uniquely exploiting this asymmetry. [[Eady2025-vm]] and [[Barbera2015-je]] provide the underlying measurement infrastructure — ideal-point and URL-sharing models that place politicians, media, and citizens on common ideological scales — later extended to new content types by [[Lai2024-to]] (YouTube video ideology) and complicated by [[Gaisbauer2025-by]], which critiques prior news-sharing research for oversimplifying partisan leaning to the outlet level, and by [[Mosleh2024-op]], which shows that engagement with partisan/low-quality news diverges markedly across seven platforms, undermining Twitter/Facebook-centric generalizations. [[Zhu2026-tn]] extends this comparatively, finding that alternative-mainstream audience overlap depends on national media systems and far-right parties' institutional access rather than being a fixed property of "alternative media." [[Yoo2026-ev]] and the twin papers [[Pennycook2021-jq]]/[[Lazer2018-mm]] (misfiled title notwithstanding, both centered on the SDSA model of fake-news accusation) show how the *label* "fake news" itself is weaponized asymmetrically by partisan and alternative outlets to delegitimize opponents rather than to describe actual falsehoods — recasting Hallin's spheres of consensus/controversy/deviance as contested terrain rather than settled convention.

## Emerging Methods and Frontiers

A cluster of recent papers extends measurement and normative theorizing into new territory. On method: [[Sarmiento2025-as]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] push framing analysis toward unsupervised and multi-modal detection; [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer political alignment even from nonpolitical text, raising privacy concerns adjacent to polarization research's targeting/microtargeting worries; [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] synthesizes evidence that Facebook's affective "Reactions" (especially anger) function as engagement amplifiers for polarizing and populist content. On normative theory: [[Esau2025-tf]] proposes "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as antidotes to "destructive polarization," explicitly trying to bridge deliberation and polarization research in the spirit of [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s call for transparency about normative commitments. Domain-specific studies — [[Lieu2025-nl]] on climate misinformation's differential polarizing potency by content category, [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] on UK newspapers' selective citation of COVID scientists, [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] on disinformation as recurring cross-media narrative, [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s evidence-frame model of election-integrity rumoring, [[Berlinski2023-rj]] on fraud claims eroding election confidence, [[Gattermann2025-yx]] on far-right electoral performance shaping disinformation concern, and [[Much2026-gu]]'s account of "gendered media spaces" in podcasts sorting young men toward Trump — collectively show the topic's center of gravity moving from abstract echo-chamber debates toward concrete, mechanism-specific accounts of *how* particular content, platforms, and institutional actors convert ordinary information flows into polarizing and democracy-eroding forces. [[Kristensen2025-ni]]'s inquiry into whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization, still underspecified empirically, marks a live edge of this expanding frontier.
