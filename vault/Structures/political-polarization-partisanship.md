# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## The Line of Argument

This body of work traces a decades-long arc from a relatively optimistic empirical claim — that social media exposes users to more cross-cutting content than feared — toward an increasingly pessimistic, mechanistically detailed, and methodologically self-critical account of how digital media architectures, elite strategy, and identity psychology co-produce polarization. The corpus can be read as a series of escalating rebuttals and refinements to the "filter bubble" thesis, moving from platform-level exposure studies, to structural/network accounts of insularity, to theories of elite and organizational radicalization, to recent work questioning whether polarization is even accurately perceived at all.

### From Filter Bubbles to Curated and Clustered Publics

The foundational empirical claim in this collection is [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s finding that Facebook's algorithm modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure but that individual choice — not algorithmic curation — is the dominant filter. This "it's us, not the algorithm" framing set an agenda that much subsequent work complicates rather than overturns. [[Green2025-ap]] shows that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, arguing that "curation bubbles" emerge from networked re-bundling of content by users rather than simple source selection — an argument that both extends and problematises Bakshy's optimism about moderate-seeming sources. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] pushes the architecture question further, arguing that TikTok-style "clustered publics" represent a second-generation shift from people-centric networked publics to item-centric, algorithmically inferred interest neighborhoods — a move that intensifies opacity and depersonalization beyond anything Facebook-era filter-bubble debates anticipated. Against this drift toward algorithmic explanations, Gauthier2026-yc-adjacent work — specifically [[Gauthier2026-iq]] — offers causal field-experimental evidence that X's algorithmic feed *does* shift attitudes rightward, but crucially through a persistence mechanism (algorithm-induced *following* choices) rather than direct exposure effects, reconciling the earlier Meta 2020 null results with newer causal findings. Together these papers suggest the field has moved from asking "does algorithmic curation cause polarization?" toward "through what specific mechanism — following behavior, story-level curation, or clustering logic — does platform architecture channel it?"

### Insularity and the Myth of the Cross-Ideological Town Square

A parallel empirical strand interrogates whether cross-partisan contact spaces actually exist. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] finds that populist-aligned news sources in Italy are more "insular" on Twitter and that insularity predicts amplification (shares) over contestation (comments) on Facebook — establishing insularity as a measurable, consequential property of partisan media ecosystems. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] extends this logic dramatically, showing across 11 political subreddits and 16 years that even discursively similar communities exhibit minimal user, source, and linking overlap; r/politics does not function as a Habermasian town square, and polarization is "sedimented" as a structural precondition rather than an emergent outcome — entangling platform design, moderation, and discourse inseparably. [[Zhu2026-tn]] internationalizes this question, showing that alternative-mainstream audience overlap is generally low but strongly conditioned by national media systems and the institutional access of populist parties, turning "insularity" into a comparative, context-dependent variable rather than a platform universal. [[Ventura2026-yc]] demonstrates how state intervention itself can manufacture insularity: Brazil's X ban triggered asymmetric partisan compliance, producing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" in the platform's news environment — showing that insularity is not only organic but can be induced by regulatory shocks.

### Amplification, Emotion, and the Attention Economy

Several papers dissect the emotional and rhetorical mechanics by which insular communities generate outsized visibility. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review of Facebook Reactions research establishes that negative affect — particularly anger — reliably drives engagement with political content, a finding echoed and extended in [[Marino2026-he]]'s longitudinal study of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook groups, which complicates the "stable echo chamber" assumption by showing affective engagement is highly event-sensitive and unstable, with internal debate and weakening out-group hostility emerging after major political ruptures. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" identifies the elite-level analogue: intra-party factions gain measurable engagement and media-attention rewards for adopting radicalizing talking points (CRT), showing how amplification dynamics link social media metrics to institutional radicalization. This elite-institutional lens is generalized in [[Bennett2025-xs]], which argues that technocentric disinformation research has neglected how platforms enable new "digital surrogate organizations" that pull parties toward illiberalism — explicitly bridging the connective-action literature with institutionalist accounts of democratic backsliding, and positioning [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s CRT case as a specific instance of a broader organizational logic.

### Misinformation, Framing, and Narrative as Polarizing Technologies

A cluster of papers reframes "misinformation" itself as a polarization mechanism rather than a simple truth-deficit. [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s evidence-frame framework shows that election-fraud rumors emerge from the interaction of largely accurate evidence with politically charged frames supplied by partisan audiences — a "call and response" dynamic. [[Rossini2026-jn]] provides longitudinal survey evidence that belief in electoral misinformation directly predicts rising political intolerance in Brazil, with messaging-app use working indirectly through misinformation belief — demonstrating amplification's attitudinal downstream cost. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows how the very term "fake news" is weaponized symmetrically by left and right alternative media to redraw Hallin's spheres of legitimacy and deviance, while [[Elfes2026-jb]] introduces "narrative polarisation" as a distinct dimension, finding — counterintuitively — that YouTube comments *reduce* surface narrative divergence relative to videos, even as deeper narrative motifs preserve partisan difference. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] reinforces the narrative turn, showing that repeated cross-outlet exposure to recurring disinformation narratives (rather than isolated claims) drives belief. [[Lieu2025-nl]]'s experimental work on climate misinformation adds a content-specificity qualifier: it is not rhetorical fallacy type but substantive claim category (attacks on climate solutions) that drives both perceived veracity and partisan polarization — a caution against overgeneralizing about "misinformation" as monolithic. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" closes this cluster by showing that partisans perceive their own side as disproportionately *targeted* by misinformation, a bias amplified by identity strength and right-wing orientation — extending hostile media effect theory into the disinformation domain and linking perception biases to lower institutional trust.

### Measurement, Method, and the Fragility of Findings

A significant meta-scientific strand in this collection interrogates the evidentiary basis of polarization research itself. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies concludes that much apparent disagreement in the field stems from inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than genuine empirical conflict — a diagnosis sharpened by [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis showing that methodological choices about media-slant measurement dramatically affect the *magnitude* (though not direction) of media-polarization associations, revealing systematic asymmetries between conservative and liberal media diets that bipolar difference scores obscure. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s review goes further, showing that depolarization *recommendations* in the literature are almost entirely disconnected from the evidence scholars themselves produce, resting instead on unexamined deliberative-democratic normative commitments. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] and [[Eady2025-vm]] contribute methodological correctives to news-sharing research specifically: the former critiques oversimplified, outlet-level, single-country measures of partisan leaning, while the latter offers a unified URL-based ideal-point model spanning media, politicians, and citizens, finding that ideologically extreme legislators disproportionately drive the polarized information environment — an elite-level echo of [[Green2025-ap]]'s story-level heterogeneity argument. [[Oswald2025-km]] and [[Schulz2026-ts]] push this self-critique furthest, arguing that visible online discourse radically over-represents a small, more extreme, more active minority of users, producing "false polarization" as an artifact of self-selection into posting — a finding with sweeping implications for nearly every observational study in this bibliography that infers population-level polarization from platform trace data. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] adds a final meta-level critique: the field's near-total empirical concentration on the US and a handful of Western democracies undermines any claim to general theory, a point [[Marino2026-he]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]] partially answer by extending analysis to Brazil and comparative European contexts respectively.

### Identity, Culture War, and the Limits of Platform-Centric Explanation

The final thematic arc pushes back against purely structural or algorithmic explanations by foregrounding identity and cultural meaning-making. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] argues that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere not primarily through platform affordances but through symbolic boundary work — reclaiming stigmatized labels, converting personal grievance into collective identity — a claim that directly challenges techno-centric accounts elsewhere in this collection. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] traces how "woke" was politicized into culture-war shorthand through both platform-specific dynamics and cross-platform coordination between Twitter and YouTube, showing depoliticized discourse can be captured by opinion leaders regardless of algorithmic design. [[Much2026-gu]]'s podcast study introduces "gendered media spaces" as a novel, non-ideological sorting mechanism that nonetheless produces ideological consequences (the 2024 youth gender gap), suggesting selective exposure operates along axes — gender expression — that conventional polarization frameworks omit entirely. Rounding out the collection, methodological and NLP-adjacent papers — [[Lee2026-je]] on LLM inference of political alignment from nonpolitical text, [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] on multi-modal and unsupervised framing analysis, [[Lai2024-to]] on latent ideology estimation for video content, [[Gattermann2025-yx]] on far-right electoral performance and disinformation concern, [[Bouchaud2026-np]] on Community Notes' structural undermoderation of polarizing content, [[Karlsson2026-hd]] on media narratives about election interference, [[Kristensen2025-ni]] on alternative platforms, [[Mosleh2024-op]] on cross-platform news engagement, [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] on politicized science coverage, [[Tai2026-qk]] on elite misinformation sharing, and [[Esau2025-tf]]'s normative proposal of deliberative reciprocity as an antidote — collectively supply the expanding methodological toolkit and normative counterpoints through which this next generation of polarization research is being operationalized, tested, and, increasingly, second-guessed.
