# Political Polarization & Partisanship on Social Media

## Mapping the Terrain: From Echo Chambers to Networked Radicalization

The corpus traces a decades-long argument about whether social media *causes* polarization or merely *reveals and amplifies* pre-existing political divisions—an argument that has shifted considerably in its empirical sophistication without fully resolving. Early foundational work like [[Barbera2015-je]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]] established methods for measuring ideology from network structure, finding topic-dependent polarization—political issues produce echo chambers, but non-political events cross ideological lines. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Flaxman2016-lm]] pushed back against strong "filter bubble" narratives, showing individual choice (not algorithms) drives most selective exposure, while [[Messing2014-jc]] suggested social endorsements could actually dilute partisan selectivity. This optimistic-skeptical strand culminates in a remarkable set of large-N field experiments—[[Guess2023-ai]], [[Guess2023-ur]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]], and [[Bail2018-fk]]—which collectively find that removing reshares, chronologizing feeds, or reducing like-minded exposure produces *precisely estimated null effects* on polarization, and in Bail's case even *backfire effects*. Yet [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] complicates this picture: segregation increases along the "funnel of engagement," is sharply asymmetric (a homogeneously conservative corner with no liberal analogue), and concentrates misinformation. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 studies argues that much of this disagreement is not empirical but conceptual—differing operationalizations of "echo chamber" produce differing verdicts—a methodological critique echoed by [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve demonstration that measurement choices (not just substance) drive conclusions about partisan media's polarizing effects, and by [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s finding that depolarization *recommendations* in the literature are rarely grounded in the evidence that generated them.

## Asymmetry as the Central Fault Line

A second major thread insists that polarization research must abandon symmetric, both-sides framing. [[Benkler2018-lw]] is the anchor text here: the "epistemic crisis" is not generic polarization but *asymmetric radicalization* of a right-wing media ecosystem insulated from truth-correcting norms—a claim echoed structurally in [[Bennett2018-ys]]'s "disinformation order" and extended comparatively by [[Heft2021-ky]], who finds U.S. right-wing sites act as a transnational hub for European counterparts. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country analysis gives this claim striking precision: neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation sharing, but their *interaction*—radical-right populism specifically—does. [[Renault2025-uh]] extends the asymmetry claim into the post-fact-checking era, showing Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under X's crowdsourced Community Notes, a bridging algorithm explicitly designed to resist partisan bias claims—while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows the same bridging design structurally *undermoderates* the most polarizing content by requiring cross-partisan agreement. [[Darius2026-xl]] and [[Iris2026-pg]] document a parallel pattern in electoral attention itself: radical-right and populist parties receive disproportionate social-media engagement and news-media visibility relative to their actual vote share, especially on TikTok. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] locates the asymmetry at the individual level too—fake-news sharing is driven by out-party hatred rather than ignorance, with Republicans sharing more, plausibly due to asymmetric *supply* of politically useful content rather than psychological difference. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] offers a meta-level caution, however: this entire asymmetry debate, like the polarization literature generally, risks parochialism by overwhelmingly theorizing from U.S. and Western-European cases.

## Affect, Platforms, and the Mechanics of Engagement

A substantial cluster investigates not ideological content but *affective* dynamics—how platforms reward anger, insularity, and emotional intensity. [[Iyengar2019-jj]] frames affective polarization as rooted in partisan social identity, distinct from issue polarization, with effects spilling into economic and social life. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] show Facebook's emotional reaction affordances systematically reward populist, confrontational content with "Angry" reactions that drive sharing, while "Love" suppresses it—a pattern [[Larsson2026-ro]]'s longitudinal study of Norway's Progress Party corroborates, showing negative sentiment increasingly dominates and drives shares/comments even as likes follow a different, positivity-biased logic. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] introduces "insularity" as a measurable property of partisan news ecosystems in the Italian case, finding populist-aligned sources more insular and that insularity predicts amplification-versus-contestation engagement patterns—a finding given dynamic texture by [[Marino2026-he]], whose three-year longitudinal study of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook networks overturns the assumption that hyperpartisan communities are stable: affective engagement is highly event-sensitive and unstable, particularly around the January 2023 coup attempt. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" shows how intra-party factions (the CRT-adopting Republicans) are rewarded with measurable engagement and media attention for radicalized positioning, extending Bennett and Segerberg's connective action framework—a framework [[Bennett2025-xs]] generalizes into a full theory of "digital surrogate organizations" pulling conservative parties toward illiberalism, using Trump's MAGA transformation as exemplar. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] locates similar dynamics at the micro level of influencer-driven toxicity, where source credibility and parasocial bonds—not just content—legitimize and homogenize audience hostility.

## Platform-Specific and Algorithmic Mechanisms

Several papers interrogate specific platform architectures as polarization mechanisms rather than neutral conduits. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X's algorithm finds a striking asymmetric mechanism: switching to the algorithmic feed shifts users rightward by promoting conservative activist accounts that users then continue following even after the algorithm is switched off—a persistence mechanism that helps reconcile the earlier Meta null results with X's apparently real effects. [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s study of Brazil's X ban documents a complementary "sorting ratchet": conservative users disproportionately circumvented the ban while liberals went silent, durably shifting the platform rightward. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment reframes the mechanism as *normative* rather than attitudinal: engagement-based feeds amplify intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distort perceptions of social norms around political dialogue, while a "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing platform enjoyment. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] challenges platform-agnostic theorizing directly, arguing Reddit's polarization is "sedimented"—an entanglement of moderation, platform design, and discourse that precedes rather than results from actual discursive divergence, since even discursively resonant subreddits show minimal cross-ideological overlap. [[Green2025-ap]] offers a methodological corrective applicable across this literature: domain-level measures of partisan audience mischaracterize story-level reality, since "curation bubbles" unbundle and re-bundle individual stories independent of source-level moderation. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] and [[Nangle2026-yo]] both show how far-right actors tailor visual/affective strategies (memes, "softening" humor, securitizing imagery) to platform-specific affordances—TikTok's youth-oriented virality logic versus Facebook/X's argumentative register—while [[Much2026-gu]]'s "gendered media spaces" concept shows how non-political entertainment podcasts function as an ideological sorting mechanism through gender rather than partisanship, contributing modestly but measurably to the 2024 youth gender gap.

## Misinformation, Narrative, and Epistemic Authority

A final cluster connects polarization to contested truth claims and narrative construction rather than discrete "fake news" facts. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop frameworks—evidence-frames and "deep stories"—showing election-integrity misinformation operates through the *interaction* of accurate evidence with distorted political framing, not through false facts alone, with influencers often supplying evidence while leaving audiences to supply the frame. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] extend this to Brazil's 2022 election, showing belief in electoral misinformation—not mere exposure to disagreement—predicts declining tolerance and trust, while mainstream news paradoxically bolsters democratic resilience even among the losing side's supporters. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" shows citizens perceive their own side as disproportionately targeted by misinformation, moderated by partisan identity strength and right-wing orientation. [[Elfes2026-jb]] pushes the narrative turn further, arguing polarization operates at the level of *actantial roles* assigned to key actors (who is victim, perpetrator, hero)—with the striking finding that comments can actually *converge* across partisan lines even as underlying "narrative motifs" preserve deeper divergence, suggesting polarization and audience ideology may be partially orthogonal. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] reframes conspiracy culture (the "Great Reset") as sustained by symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity rather than platform algorithms or ideological content per se. Finally, [[Rodarte2026-dk]] and [[Yoo2026-ev]] show how political and media actors contest *epistemic authority itself*—government critics mimicking journalistic formats, allies bypassing the press entirely, and "fake news" deployed as a weaponized delegitimizing label rather than a descriptive term—while [[Topinka2026-hb]]'s theorization of AI "slopaganda" and [[Lin2025-xp]]'s and [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]]'s experiments on LLM persuasion point toward where this epistemic contestation is heading: generative AI as both a tool of affective lure and a newly destabilized, perceived-as-partisan messenger whose persuasive power is itself contingent on trust.
