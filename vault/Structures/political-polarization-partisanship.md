# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## Structure Note: Political Polarization & Partisanship

### Framing the field: fragmentation and the call for integration

[[Falkenberg2026-ka]] opens the topic with a diagnosis that many of the papers below implicitly confirm: polarization research is disciplinarily siloed and geographically skewed toward the United States. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] extends this meta-critique methodically, showing via a systematic review that depolarization *recommendations* rarely follow from the evidence scholars themselves produce, but instead smuggle in deliberative-democratic norms. Read together, these two papers suggest the corpus below should be approached less as a settled body of findings than as a set of competing operationalizations and normative commitments — a point reinforced by [[Hartmann2025-px]], whose review of 129 echo-chamber studies argues that disagreement about whether echo chambers even exist is substantially an artifact of inconsistent measurement rather than divergent realities. [[Schemer2026-mh]] makes the same point empirically: a specification-curve analysis of 504 model variants shows that how one measures "partisan slant" and "polarization" changes the magnitude (though not the direction) of the partisan-media–polarization relationship, and reveals a masked asymmetry favoring conservative media effects. Together these framing pieces caution that any single-country or single-metric claim about polarization should be read against a backdrop of methodological pluralism.

### Beyond the US: Brazil, Italy, and comparative hyperpartisanship

A strong cluster of papers pushes the field's empirical center of gravity toward the Global South and Southern Europe, in direct response to Falkenberg's critique. Brazil emerges as a particularly rich site: [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows how Bolsonarist and opposition parliamentarians fought for "epistemic authority" during the Manaus COVID crisis through structurally distinct narrative strategies (derivative reporting vs. direct anti-press authority claims vs. local brokerage), while [[Rossini2026-jn]] demonstrates that belief in 2022 electoral-fraud misinformation directly predicted rising political intolerance over a three-wave panel, with messaging apps operating as an indirect radicalizing channel. [[Marino2026-he]] complicates the assumption of stable hyperpartisan echo chambers by tracking three years of a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network and finding highly volatile, event-driven affective engagement rather than static polarization — a genuinely longitudinal counterpoint to cross-sectional snapshots. [[Ventura2026-yc]] extends this Brazilian arc into platform governance, showing that the 2024 X ban produced an asymmetric "sorting ratchet": conservative users disproportionately circumvented it while liberal users went dark, durably shifting the platform's news environment rightward — a cautionary tale about state intervention in polarized digital publics.

Italy provides a second comparative anchor. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] examines the 2018 election to show how populist parties (M5S, the League) cultivate more insular news ecologies on Twitter, with insularity trading off against Facebook engagement style (amplification vs. contestation) — a nuanced picture in which the "anti-elitist cyber party" M5S turns hybrid-media fragmentation to strategic advantage. [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Paci2025-ag]] approach Italian discourse from the rhetorical and computational-linguistic side, respectively: the former proposes a "Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification" model for how fake-news accusations function as negative-campaigning weapons in Italian newspapers, while the latter finds that even strong LLMs struggle to interpret the implicatures and presuppositions that saturate manipulative Italian political speech — suggesting that computational tools trained on generic corpora may underestimate how much of partisan communication operates below the level of explicit claims. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] rounds out the comparative sweep with Serbia's SNS party, arguing that its coordinated inauthentic behavior mimics authentic grassroots support so effectively that it evades detection frameworks built around centralized bot farms — a reminder that "digital authoritarianism" and "polarization" studies increasingly overlap in hybrid regimes.

### Hyperpartisan networks and elite radicalization

A second strand treats polarization not as a mass-level attitudinal phenomenon but as an elite-network dynamic. [[Bennett2025-xs]] provides the most ambitious synthesis, arguing that "technocentric" disinformation research and "institutionalist" party-politics research need to be fused: platforms enable new "digital surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Stop the Steal, Proud Boys) that pull the Republican Party toward illiberalism in ways neither individual-level persuasion models nor classical party theory can capture alone. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] operationalizes a version of this logic — the "logic of connective faction" — showing empirically how Republican adoption of Critical Race Theory as a talking point tracked ideological extremity, connectivity to hyperpartisan media, and engagement rewards, producing a feedback loop that radicalizes intra-party factions. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country, 32-million-tweet analysis complements this by showing that misinformation-sharing among parliamentarians is not explained by populism or right-wing ideology alone, but by their *interaction*: radical-right populism specifically. [[Tai2026-qk]] narrows the aperture to US elected officials, finding that institutional role and ideology jointly condition misinformation-sharing propensity — a finding consonant with Tornberg's cross-national pattern that structural position, not just attitude, predicts elite misinformation behavior.

### Echo chambers, curation, and the architecture of exposure

A long-running methodological debate about whether digital media actually produce ideological segregation runs through several papers. [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s foundational Facebook study found that individual clicking behavior, more than algorithmic ranking, suppresses cross-cutting exposure — a finding that [[Green2025-ap]] complicates by showing that source-level (domain) partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, meaning that "curation bubbles" form at finer grain than outlet choice. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] pushes back against the idea of any single "town square" subreddit, showing minimal cross-ideological overlap in sourcing, cross-posting, and discourse across 11 political subreddits over 16 years, and reframes polarization as "sedimented" — a precondition of platform architecture and moderation rather than simply an emergent outcome of discourse. [[Schulz2026-ts]] adds a crucial corrective from the opposite direction: much observed online polarization may be *false* polarization, an artifact of self-selection whereby ideologically extreme users disproportionately post, skewing visible discourse relative to actual population-level opinion distributions across 40 countries. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] extends the exposure question to alternative platforms, asking whether platform choice itself (rather than content) drives discursive polarization. Read as a set, these papers suggest the "echo chamber" question cannot be resolved at a single level of analysis — friend networks, algorithms, individual choice, story-level curation, and platform architecture all pull in partially independent directions.

### Measuring ideology and framing at scale

A cluster of largely methodological papers supplies the infrastructure for the substantive claims above. [[Eady2025-vm]] and [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] offer two complementary approaches to scaling political texts and actors: URL co-sharing behavior for ideology estimation (finding, notably, that ordinary highly-engaged citizens — not politicians — share the most polarized news) versus LLM-based "ask and average" prompting validated across languages and text types. [[Lai2024-to]] extends ideology scaling to YouTube video content via Reddit cross-linking, while [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates that LLMs can infer political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical text, exploiting culturally-politicized lexical cues — a capability with clear privacy implications given [[Bouchaud2026-np]]'s and [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s concerns about platform-level partisan sorting. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]], [[Arora2025-tx]], and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] each critique existing computational approaches to news circulation and framing as too unidimensional or too reliant on predefined categories, proposing multi-level ("cartographic"), multi-modal, and unsupervised alternatives respectively — together signaling a methodological shift toward richer, less reductive representations of partisan content. [[Elfes2026-jb]]'s "narrative polarisation" concept, using an LLM-operationalized Greimasian actantial model on Israeli-Palestinian YouTube discourse, exemplifies this shift most directly: it shows that surface convergence in comments can mask persistent narrative-level partisan divergence in how actors are cast as agents, victims, or aggressors — a genuinely novel dimension beyond opinion- or interaction-based polarization measures.

### Misinformation, distrust, and the weaponization of "fake news"

Several papers treat misinformation not as an epistemic failure to be corrected but as a partisan resource to be strategically deployed. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows that "fake news" functions in both left- and right-wing US alternative media almost exclusively as a delegitimizing label rather than a description of fabricated content, reshaping Hallin's spheres of legitimate controversy and deviance. [[Mosca2026-yh]]'s Italian SDSA model makes the same argument structurally. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s cross-national "hostile misinformation effect" — the finding that citizens believe their own political in-group is disproportionately targeted by misinformation — shows how this weaponization becomes internalized as perception, amplified by party identity strength, ideological extremity, and (counterintuitively) electoral winning rather than losing. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] demonstrates a related asymmetric mechanism experimentally: media narratives about election interference increase affective polarization specifically when the *opposing* party is portrayed as the beneficiary, suggesting that coverage of interference can itself become a polarizing vector independent of whether interference succeeds. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] closes this loop at the level of platform governance, showing that X's Community Notes bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates precisely the polarizing content most in need of correction, with effects that vary by national polarization context — a sobering complement to [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s finding on the downstream partisan effects of blunt platform bans. Domain-specific misinformation studies — [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] on politically polarized citation of COVID scientists in UK newspapers, and [[Lieu2025-nl]] on which rhetorical strategies of climate misinformation are most politically polarizing — show that these dynamics generalize beyond electoral misinformation into science communication.

### Identity, affect, and the culture-war register

A final cluster treats polarization as fundamentally an identity and affective phenomenon rather than an informational one. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows how "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere less through shared ideology than through symbolic boundary work — reclaiming stigmatized labels, sharing "deep stories" of grievance, and constructing a "sheeple" out-group — uniting otherwise ideologically disparate secular-libertarian and religious factions. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] traces an analogous identity-appropriation dynamic longitudinally, showing how "woke," a term originating in Black anti-racist discourse, was captured as culture-war ammunition across Twitter and YouTube through both platform-specific and cross-platform coordinated dynamics. [[Much2026-gu]] extends this affective-identity lens to gender, proposing that entertainment podcasts constitute "gendered media spaces" where incidental ideological sorting occurs via masculinity/femininity coding rather than explicit partisanship — helping explain, alongside more conventional persuasion mechanisms, the widening youth gender gap in the 2024 US election. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review of Facebook Reactions research complements this affective focus, showing anger-based engagement as a consistent driver of amplification for populist and far-right political content. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s early case study of the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag, and [[Zhao2025-ny]]'s account of hashtag hijacking, both illustrate how affectively-charged symbolic contestation over hashtags and slogans functions as a recurring low-level mechanism of polarized public debate. Finally, [[Esau2025-tf]] offers the clearest normative counterpoint to this whole affective-identity literature, proposing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as theoretical antidotes to what it terms *destructive* polarization — implicitly reasserting, against van Eck's critique of ungrounded normativity, that some disagreement is healthy and only its destructive variant need be targeted by depolarization interventions.
