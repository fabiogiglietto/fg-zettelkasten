# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## Measuring Polarization: A Fractured Toolkit

Before any claim about *how* platforms polarize can be trusted, the field has to reckon with how unstable its own instruments are. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies shows that disagreement about whether echo chambers even exist is substantially an artifact of divergent conceptualizations and operationalizations rather than a reflection of contradictory realities. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates the same problem experimentally: running 504 specification combinations of media-slant and polarization measures on the same three-wave US panel, the authors find that methodological choices—audience-based versus content-based partisan slant scores, thermometer versus misperception measures—swing coefficient magnitudes by a factor of seven, while also revealing an asymmetry (conservative media diets show stronger associations than liberal ones) that bipolar difference scores conveniently erase. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] pushes further on measurement, arguing that outlet-level partisan scores flatten crucial story- and content-level variation in news circulation, a critique empirically vindicated by [[Green2025-ap]]'s 'curation bubbles': individual stories from ostensibly moderate outlets (the Wall Street Journal, the New York Post) attract wildly divergent partisan audiences depending on which piece is shared, meaning that domain-level partisanship measures systematically mistake heterogeneity for moderation. [[Oswald2025-km]] adds a further distortion: because a small minority of highly active users produce most visible political content, both citizens and researchers who read online discourse as a proxy for public opinion are seeing only the "tip of the iceberg." Read together, these pieces recommend deep methodological humility for any claim in this topic cluster—including the ones that follow. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s review of depolarization scholarship extends the same skepticism normatively: most recommendations for *fixing* polarization are not actually derived from the evidence the same studies produce, but smuggle in unexamined commitments to deliberative-democratic ideals, revealing a persistent evidence/values gap in the literature this note surveys. The oldest touchstone here, [[Bakshy2015-rn]], is worth reading against this backdrop: its finding that individual clicking behavior narrows cross-cutting exposure more than Facebook's algorithm does was itself an artifact of a particular operationalization (friend-network composition, algorithmic ranking, click behavior) whose limits later work would probe more critically.

## Case Studies in Partisan Insularity: Italy, Brazil, and the Latin American Digital Public

The empirical heart of this topic lies in national case studies that show insularity is neither uniform nor symmetric. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]'s study of the 2018 Italian election is foundational: news sources favored by populist parties (Five Star Movement, League) are measurably more insular on Twitter, and — crucially — insularity interacts with a Facebook attention economy in which highly insular sources are *shared* (amplified) while cross-partisan sources are *commented on* (contested), with M5S uniquely able to exploit this asymmetry to shield positive coverage and swarm negative coverage. This Italian portrait of a populist "cyber party" exploiting fragmented hybrid media ecologies rhymes with the Brazilian case in [[Ventura2026-yc]], where the 2024 Supreme Court ban on X produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet": conservative users circumvented the ban en masse while liberal users went silent, and the platform's ideological composition remained skewed right for months after the ban was lifted — showing that even state-imposed content interventions in polarized democracies can entrench rather than dissolve partisan asymmetries. Latin American material also enters through discourse-analytic rather than network-analytic lenses: [[Sadler2025-vu]]'s case study of a Venezuelan influencer's Spanish-language tweets blaming NATO for the invasion of Ukraine shows how narrative technique (disnarration, monologized multivocality) can produce referentially and ethically suspect content without any single falsifiable claim, complicating fact-check-centered models of disinformation. Comparative European work situates these single-country stories within broader media-systemic variation: [[Zhu2026-tn]]'s six-country network analysis of alternative/mainstream audience overlap finds that alternative media's structural position depends on media-system type and on whether right-wing populist parties have institutionalized political access — with Sweden's isolated alternative enclave a striking counterpoint to Italy's more porous populist ecosystem, and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] showing an analogous "who is your scientist" polarization in how UK newspapers of different orientation cite Great Barrington versus John Snow Memorandum signatories, extending insularity dynamics beyond news outlets to expert sourcing itself.

## Hyperpartisan Networks and Elite Radicalization

A second thread traces how hyperpartisan media and networked elites co-radicalize rather than merely reflect existing division. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" is the clearest theoretical statement: intra-party ideological subgroups build connective ties to hyper-partisan digital surrogates, and the resulting Critical Race Theory case study shows that Republicans who adopted the CRT frame were more ideologically extreme, more embedded in right-wing digital networks, and disproportionately rewarded with engagement — a feedback loop that narrows rather than pluralizes party discourse. [[Bennett2025-xs]] scales this argument up into a general theory of democratic backsliding, arguing that "technocentric" (individual-level disinformation) and "institutionalist" (party/elite) explanations must be integrated via a "communication-as-organization" framework in which digital surrogate organizations (QAnon, Stop the Steal, Proud Boys) pull conservative parties toward illiberalism — explicitly building on the same connective-action lineage as Knupfer's faction logic and offering comparative traction for AfD, Orbán, and other cases beyond the US. [[Rothut2026-or]]'s study of the German Querdenken movement shows a parallel meso-level mechanism operating through protest rather than party structures: anti-elite framing and conspiratorial narrative served as bridges connecting grassroots pandemic skeptics to entrenched far-right and international conspiracist networks on Telegram, with the movement's own channels occupying a peripheral rather than central position relative to established extremist brokers. [[Yoo2026-ev]] closes this cluster at the level of vocabulary: comparing right-wing Newsmax and left-wing Occupy Democrats, the term "fake news" itself is shown to function almost identically across the ideological spectrum — not as a description of falsehood but as a weaponized boundary marker that redraws Hallin's spheres of consensus, controversy, and deviance to delegitimize the opposing side.

## Platform Architectures and the Shape of Partisan Publics

A distinct set of papers asks how platform design itself — rather than content or elite strategy — structures partisan sorting. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X is the most causally direct: switching users from chronological to algorithmic feed shifts political attitudes rightward and induces users to follow conservative activist accounts whose influence persists even after the algorithm is switched off, reconciling earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies by locating the mechanism in *which accounts get followed*, not merely what is shown. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] offers a structural counterpart on Reddit: across 11 political subreddits, cross-ideological overlap in users, sources, and cross-posting is minimal even between discursively similar communities, undermining the notion that any subreddit — including r/politics — functions as a cross-ideological town square; polarization here is "sedimented" as a precondition of platform architecture and moderation rather than an emergent outcome of discourse alone. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes a more fundamental morphological shift: TikTok's move from "networked publics" (explicit interpersonal ties) to algorithmically inferred "clustered publics" produces depersonalization, opacity, and intensified subcultural fragmentation, a transformation other platforms are increasingly imitating. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Lai2024-to]] extend this platform-centered inquiry: the former asks directly whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization, while the latter shows that YouTube video ideology can be recovered as a latent variable from cross-subreddit sharing patterns, extending ideal-point estimation from legislators and users to individual pieces of content. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] grounds these architectural arguments in a non-Western case, showing how TikTok's affordances amplify a culturally specific "dogshow" incivility among Filipino Gen Z influencers, blurring entertainment and cyberbullying. Finally, [[Bouchaud2026-np]]'s analysis of X's Community Notes demonstrates that a governance mechanism explicitly designed to counter polarization — bridging-based consensus scoring — structurally *undermoderates* the most polarizing content precisely because such content is least likely to attract the cross-partisan agreement the algorithm requires, a design flaw with direct electoral stakes across the 13 countries studied.

## Affect, Emotion, and the Hostile Perception of the Other Side

Alongside structural insularity runs an affective register of polarization. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] both center Facebook's emotional reaction affordances: across Scandinavian elections, populist parties elicit disproportionate "Angry" reactions that predict sharing more strongly than "Love" reactions do, a pattern Anwar's systematic review generalizes across dozens of studies showing that negatively valenced, politically charged content reliably outperforms positive content in engagement. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] extends the hostile-media-effect tradition into misinformation perception itself, showing across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland that citizens systematically believe their own party is more targeted by misinformation than the opposing party — an effect amplified by political interest, party identity strength, and (counterintuitively) status as electoral winners rather than losers. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] complicates any simple story of media-driven polarization: exposure to fictitious narratives about election interference does not raise aggregate polarization, but produces partisan-asymmetric spikes in perceived division specifically among respondents told the *opposing* party benefited from interference — evidence that discourse about disinformation can itself be a polarizing vector independent of whether interference campaigns succeed. The newest wing of this affective literature turns to generative AI: [[Lee2026-je]] shows that LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical discourse by exploiting culturally politicized cues (Tesla, Taylor Swift, "boomer"), while [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows the flip side — perceiving an LLM itself as politically biased against one's party sharply attenuates its persuasive power, with users pushing back and arguing rather than disengaging, evidence that even AI-mediated political communication is filtered through partisan identity.

## Narrative, Identity, and the Discursive Construction of Division

A final cluster reframes polarization not as opinion distance or network sorting but as competing stories about who the actors are. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes this directly, using an LLM-driven Actantial Model to show that YouTube videos on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict carry sharply polarized narrative roles for key actors, even where user comments substantially converge on surface content — polarization, on this account, can live in produced narrative independent of audience alignment. [[Marwick2026-qd]] generalizes the argument to US disinformation broadly, conceptualizing successful falsehoods as identity-affirming, cathartic cross-platform stories (the "Pee Tape," "Crisis Actor") rather than discrete false claims, which explains why fact-checking and platform-bound moderation struggle against them. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows the same dynamic from the inside: participants in "Great Reset" conspiracy discourse construct collective identity by reclaiming the stigmatized label "conspiracy theorist" and weaving personal hardship into affectively binding "deep stories" against an imagined "mainstream other," uniting otherwise ideologically disparate secular-libertarian and religious-Christian publics — a finding that pushes back against purely techno-centric echo-chamber explanations in favor of Lamont-style symbolic boundary work. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s earlier case of #JeNeSuisPasCharlie is an antecedent instance of the same phenomenon: a counter-hashtag mobilizing dissenting narrative identity against a dominant solidarity frame. Computational methods for surfacing these dynamics at scale are developing in parallel: [[Arora2025-tx]] extends automated framing analysis beyond text to multi-modal (visual and cross-modal) cues in partisan gun-violence coverage, and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] proposes an unsupervised pipeline for detecting emergent frames in polarized discourse without predefined categories — both responding to the same critique Elfes and Marwick voice, that conventional opinion- or interaction-based polarization metrics miss how groups construct opposing realities through narrative and framing rather than mere disagreement.

## Toward Normative Stakes and the Limits of Fixing Polarization

Underlying all these strands is an open normative question, sharpened by [[Esau2025-tf]]'s theoretical proposal that "destructive polarization" should be diagnosed through observable symptoms in user-generated content and countered through "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" — an ideal squarely in the deliberative-democratic tradition that [[van-Eck2026-xg]] shows dominates, often uncritically, the field's prescriptive imagination. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] and [[Lieu2025-nl]] round out the picture by showing how source credibility (influencer status) and specific misinformation content (attacks on climate solutions) interact with partisan identity to determine not just belief change but toxic, echo-chamber-reinforcing audience reaction — reminders that whatever depolarizing interventions researchers propose must contend with mechanisms (source trust, content-specific resonance, platform architecture, narrative identity) that are considerably more entangled than either the "more exposure" or "less exposure" prescriptions reviewed by van Eck et al. suggest.
