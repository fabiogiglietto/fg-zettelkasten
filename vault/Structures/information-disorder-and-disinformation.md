# Information Disorder & Disinformation

## The narrative turn: rethinking what disinformation *is*

A significant cluster of work in this collection pushes back against treating disinformation as a matter of discrete false claims to be checked against ground truth. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] argues that the creator-centric, intent-based definitions dominant after 2016 cannot explain how falsehood actually circulates in a hybrid news system, proposing instead a micro/meso/macro model centred on propagators' judgments. [[Marwick2026-qd]] extends this logic empirically, showing that "successful" disinformation behaves like cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *story* — with characters, catchphrases, and moral stakes — rather than a verifiable proposition, a claim [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] corroborates by showing that cumulative exposure to recurring narratives, not isolated claims, drives belief. [[Sadler2025-vu]] radicalizes this further with a "hermeneutic realist" narratology that dissolves the true/false binary altogether, showing content can be referentially and ethically suspect without being straightforwardly false. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] operationalize this narrative turn empirically for U.S. election rumors, showing that quote-tweet "call and response" dynamics and evolving "deep stories" (a` la Hochschild) do the interpretive work that individual posts cannot, while [[Goel2025-iq]] demonstrates the same phenomenon at the level of source credibility: true mainstream articles are routinely repurposed into misleading narratives, a mechanism invisible to domain-blocklist methods. [[Waight2025-al]] supplies the methodological infrastructure this turn requires — an LLM pipeline for measuring narrative (rather than lexical or topical) similarity across languages — demonstrating on the Ukraine "biolabs" case that lexical measures miss narrative-sharing between Russian state media and low-quality (and even mainstream) U.S. outlets entirely. Meanwhile [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] show "fake news" itself has become a rhetorical weapon deployed by partisan and hyperpartisan actors to delegitimize opponents rather than describe fabrication — a self-referential twist on the same conceptual slippage. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] pushes the epistemology further still into the generative-AI era, arguing that conventional fact categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot capture "emergent facts" produced by probabilistic recombination.

## Coordination, deception, and the "Zombie Internet"

A second throughline concerns the operational anatomy of manipulation on platforms. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] crystallizes this project's core diagnosis with the concept of Deceptive Information Operations (DIOs) and the "Zombie Internet": across a pro-Putin propaganda network, a gambling-promotion operation, and poorly moderated groups distributing adult content and fraud links, the authors find a shared structural logic — deceptive content, exploitation of platform affordances, and coordinated participation — that exceeds any single existing taxonomy (deception theory, strategic information operations, or Meta's own "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" category). This diagnosis is echoed by [[Thiele2025-ol]], which formalizes the "attribution problem" for coordinated social media manipulation (CSMM), arguing CIB conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent and proposing a rational-choice typology linking campaign scale/elaborateness/disguise to principals' stakes. The empirical backbone for this line comes from large platform-provided datasets: [[noauthor_undated-bm]] shows that 49 Meta-identified "deceptive networks" reached tens of millions of US users, with reach dominated by a handful of operations and heavily amplified by ordinary resharers rather than direct exposure — complicating simple causal stories about influence-operation effects. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] offers a critical mirror to this platform self-reporting, showing how Meta's own Newsroom discourse strategically externalizes "inauthenticity" onto outside actors while eliding platform design and hyperpartisan verified accounts. Detection methodology continues to evolve at the margins: Rodriguez_Farres2025-vu... rather [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extends coordination detection to decentralized Bluesky, [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] to Chinese COVID-protest Twitter discourse showing social media's dual-use as both mobilization and repression infrastructure, [[Zhao2025-ny]] to bot-driven "hashtag hijacking," and [[Rogers2026-cy]] proposes "post-truth spaces" — clusters of hard-to-verify or known disinformation sources — as an actionable mapping tool for fact-checkers, demonstrated on Moldovan Facebook discourse about the Ukraine war.

## Elites, parties, and the strategic weaponization of narrative authority

Several papers relocate agency from anonymous trolls to political elites themselves. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds across 26 countries that it is specifically the *combination* of populism and right-wing ideology — radical-right populism — that predicts elite misinformation-sharing, not either factor alone; [[Tai2026-qk]] similarly investigates institutional and ideological "checks" on elected officials' misinformation-sharing. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] shows how intra-party factions (illustrated via the sudden 2020–21 rise of "Critical Race Theory" as a Republican talking point) build connective ties to hyper-partisan media ecosystems that reward ideological extremity with engagement, radicalizing party politics from within. [[Balluff2026-bv]] documents a more classic mechanism — media capture via covert government-advertising bribery in the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre" — showing measurable shifts in coverage favorability once financial arrangements began. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] examines how Brazilian parliamentarians contest journalistic gatekeeping itself during crises, deploying derivative reporting, direct authority claims, or brokerage narratives depending on their relationship to power. Finally, [[Renault2025-uh]] provides an empirical rebuttal to claims of anti-conservative bias in content moderation: even under X's crowdsourced Community Notes system, Republican posts are flagged as misleading 2.3 times more often than Democratic ones — a finding that speaks directly to ongoing platform-governance debates discussed elsewhere in this set.

## State propaganda, information operations, and AI-mediated influence

A distinct but related body of work addresses propaganda emanating from, or laundered through, state and quasi-state actors. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] provides rare organizational detail on Russia's Internet Research Agency via OSINT analysis of employee CVs, showing it functioned less as a spy unit than as an outsourced, market-embedded media/PR company whose recruitment spiked around the Crimea annexation rather than the 2016 US election. [[Copland2025-em]] traces how Sky News Australia became a node in a transnational "network propaganda" system, using dual strategies (native Facebook content for domestic audiences, YouTube content for the US right-wing pipeline). [[Makeev2026-ma]] and [[Beacken2026-zb]] extend this comparative, state-centered lens: the former theorizes a "dual-objective" model of domestic-versus-foreign wartime propaganda in the Russo-Ukrainian war, the latter shows generative-AI propaganda adoption is structurally, not uniformly, shaped across six democratically weakened states. [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Sadler2025-vu]] (again) examine how pro-Kremlin campaigns like #IStandWithPutin exploit rather than evade the platform "infrastructure of truth." [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound itself as an under-examined, TikTok-specific propaganda vector in Ukraine-war disinformation, while [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows how the Sweden Democrats tailor visual securitizing discourse — memes on TikTok, argumentative text on Facebook/X — to manufacture immigration "crisis," including via decontextualized non-EU footage. The generative-AI thread converges most sharply in Waight2026-tz, which demonstrates empirically that state media control is already embedded in LLM training data, causing measurably more pro-regime outputs in lower-media-freedom languages — a mechanism of institutional influence Emilio's [[Emilio2026-ik]] "synthetic reality" framework theorizes at civilizational scale, warning of a "Generative AI Paradox" in which ubiquitous synthetic media licenses wholesale discounting of digital evidence. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a concrete instance: Grokipedia is shown to be a derivative, ideologically slanted, low-reliability-source-heavy fork of Wikipedia. Audience-level consequences of synthetic media are examined by [[Wack2026-bt]] (deepfakes in Kenya, where peer commentary — not the artifact alone — determines persuasive impact) and [[Choi2026-bz]] (a "modality-congruent carryover effect" of authentication difficulty), while [[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates alarm about AI-generated visuals by showing decontextualized *real* video often outperforms AI stills in credibility — an important corrective picked up by [[DeVerna2025-dl]], which shows LLMs are poor fact-checkers absent curated retrieval context, and cite left-leaning sources disproportionately when they do succeed.

## Platforms as governors: moderation, fact-checking, and the political economy of research

A large contingent interrogates the institutions meant to police disinformation, and the compromised evidentiary base available for studying them. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] draw the tobacco/pharma/food analogy explicitly, cataloguing mechanisms — data monopolies, selective funding, performative collaboration — by which platforms shape the science that studies them; [[Pierri2025-hm]] extends this into EU policy specifically, assessing the DSA's Article 40 researcher-access regime as promising but bottlenecked by asymmetries and industry resistance. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers a pointed meta-scientific critique of the Meta2020 partnership itself, arguing platforms change too fast for reactive social science, which is why proactive regulatory disclosure is necessary — a claim empirically sharpened by [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], which shows Facebook's shares-to-views amplification function fluctuates sharply with known governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures), refuting neutral-conduit framings, and by [[McNally2025-dn]], which finds algorithmic ranking changes produce detectable, lagged (~19–24 day) effects specifically on hard news engagement. On the fact-checking side, [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform-funding dependencies via apologia strategies, while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically dismantles Zuckerberg's 2025 framing of fact-checkers as censors, showing removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases. [[Ramos2026-qo]] and [[Xue2025-bp]] probe the *practice* of fact-checking itself — the former proposing "argument-checking" and narrative monitoring as necessary complements after the 2024 Valencia floods conspiracy, the latter showing fact-checks themselves are often emotionally charged rather than neutrally informational. Measurement-focused audits round out this cluster: [[Vincent_undated-re]] tracks rising TikTok mis/disinformation prevalence in Europe; [[Luhring2025-od]] provides a rigorous audit of NewsGuard's stability and biases, cautioning against binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs; and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], and [[Mosleh2024-op]] extend Guess-style URL-exposure methodology to Europe, US 2020 diffusion trees, and cross-platform comparison respectively.

## Effects, audiences, and everyday sensemaking

Downstream of production and platform governance sits the question of consequences. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] offers rare causal evidence — via a compartmental SIRVA epidemic model — that antivaccine tweets measurably reduced US vaccination rates and increased deaths. In Brazil, [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] show electoral misinformation belief both erodes political tolerance and undermines trust in results, with messaging-app use operating as an indirect pathway; [[Ventura2025-sw]] complements this with a WhatsApp-deactivation field experiment underscoring that misinformation dynamics extend well beyond public feeds into private messaging. [[Adam2026-tz]] traces the "marriage" of media exposure and predispositions in conspiracy-belief formation, finding both contagion (alternative media) and mitigation (mainstream media) effects, moderated by populism and mistrust. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a novel "hostile misinformation effect" — a partisan mirror of the hostile media effect — in which citizens perceive their own side as disproportionately targeted by misinformation, with consequences for media trust and perceived electoral integrity; [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows that even *media narratives about* election interference (rather than interference itself) can produce asymmetric polarization. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] steps back from politically charged domains altogether, using digital diaries to show ordinary Australians perceive misinformation largely in mainstream/alternative news headlines around business and economics — a salutary corrective, alongside [[Budak2024-ef]], against overstated claims about algorithmic exposure and societal harm. [[Marwick2025-vx]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]] extend this audience-centered lens to radicalization itself, showing "redpilling" is typically a gradual socialization process rather than sudden conversion, and that ConspiracyTok content is produced disproportionately by young, non-white, and female creators enacting a "generous epistemology" that cuts against the stereotype of the isolated white male conspiracist — a picture broadly consistent with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review concluding that online radicalization is best understood as multi-level and hybrid rather than a discrete online/offline binary.

## Measurement innovations and structural mitigation

A methods-oriented seam runs beneath much of the above. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" as measurable supply-demand imbalances correlating with reduced high-credibility content; [[Nenno2025-xa]] shows news-value profiles of flagged misinformation vary meaningfully — if modestly — across WEIRD and non-WEIRD contexts, echoing [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue call to de-WEIRD-ify the field. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] examine science-communication ecosystems specifically, showing coordinated contrarian amplification of a small set of credentialed experts and UK newspapers' politically polarized citation of COVID scientists respectively; [[Marino2023-9137f448]] and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] extend this to Italian health-information ecosystems, where legacy-media remediation of "Alternative Influence Network" figures and celebrity/malpractice framing drive outsized Facebook engagement. Finally, a set of papers explicitly targets intervention design: [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates scalable Instagram-native prebunking against emotional manipulation with durable five-month effects; [[Spampatti2026-kx]] imports lessons from climate psychology about behavioral outcomes, systems thinking, and researcher self-organization; [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts concludes platform- and journalism-level interventions outrank individual-level fixes; and [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that dominant analogies (infodemic, information warfare, information pollution) each obscure part of a genuinely multilevel complex system, calling for portfolio-style, complexity-aware responses — a fitting closing frame for a topic whose 88 papers collectively refuse any single diagnosis, mechanism, or fix.
