# Facebook Engagement and Content Visibility

## Reactions, Emotion, and the Architecture of Virality

A cluster of papers treats Facebook's Reactions suite — Like, Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry — not as a side feature but as the affective engine of visibility. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review synthesizes 64 studies to show that reactions function as "paralinguistic digital affordances": lightweight, low-effort signals that nonetheless carry distinct emotional valence and differentially predict downstream engagement, with anger consistently the strongest driver of sharing in political contexts. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] operationalizes this claim comparatively across Norway, Sweden, and Denmark, finding that "Angry" reactions positively predict shares while "Love" reactions suppress them — a finding that dovetails with [[Larsson2026-ro]]'s decade-long study of Norway's Progress Party, which shows negative-sentiment posts driving shares and comments while likes track a separate, more positive logic. Together these three papers converge on a key structural insight: different engagement metrics are not interchangeable proxies for the same underlying "popularity" but index qualitatively distinct audience responses, with likes/love signaling affirmation and shares/anger signaling mobilization. This has direct consequences for visibility, since shares (not likes) are the mechanism by which content escapes its original audience — meaning platforms that reward engagement volume are structurally rewarding anger-inflected, often populist content, a dynamic [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] explicitly ties to far-right communication strategies.

## Algorithmic Governance as Active, Fluctuating Curation

A second thread pushes back against "black box" framings of ranking algorithms by showing that their effects are empirically detectable and historically contingent. [[McNally2025-dn]] demonstrates, via decade-long time-series analysis of Guardian articles, that Facebook's News Feed updates produced measurable, lagged (~19-24 day) effects specifically on hard news engagement, arguing that algorithms are sociotechnical systems amenable to systemic audit rather than opaque black boxes. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends this logic directly into the question of visibility, showing that the relationship between shares and views on Facebook is dampened for partisan content and amplified for content meeting journalistic-quality standards — and crucially, that these penalties and rewards intensify sharply around known governance moments like the 2020 "break the glass" election interventions, providing direct evidence that Facebook actively recalibrates visibility rather than passing through a stable, neutral amplification function. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] supplies the conceptual vocabulary for this phenomenon, arguing that "reduction" — algorithmic demotion short of removal — constitutes a core, underacknowledged form of content moderation that is strategically kept invisible to escape the regulatory and reputational scrutiny attached to outright removal. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]], working on Twitter/X rather than Facebook but addressing the same governance question, shows how an algorithmic feed can concentrate visibility around a platform owner and reward "agitating" content independent of ideology — a useful comparative case for thinking about what algorithmic curation does across platforms. [[Guess2023-ai]]'s large field experiment complicates the causal picture: removing reshares substantially reduces exposure to political news and untrustworthy content, confirming reshares as a major amplification mechanism, yet this reduced exposure produces no detectable shift in beliefs or polarization — suggesting that visibility and influence are not the same thing.

## Ideological Segregation and the Limits of Domain-Level Measurement

[[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the foundational reference point here, arguing that individual click choices suppress cross-cutting exposure more than Facebook's algorithm does. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] revisits this claim with 2020-era, platform-scale data and finds substantially higher segregation than earlier web-browsing studies suggested, with a distinctly homogeneous conservative "corner" of the news ecosystem that concentrates most fact-checked misinformation — and, critically, shows that segregation is far higher when measured at the URL (story) level than the domain level. [[Green2025-ap]] generalizes this methodological point into a standalone argument: "curation bubbles" emerge because stories are unbundled from their source domains and rebundled by networked sharing, so that domain-level partisanship scores systematically mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, especially for nominally "moderate" outlets. Read together, these three papers trace an arc from optimistic claims about cross-cutting exposure, toward increasingly granular evidence that engagement-driven visibility segregates audiences more severely than source-level analyses can detect — a problem that resurfaces as a methodological warning throughout the dataset-critique literature below.

## Dataset Methodology: What the URL Shares Dataset Can and Cannot See

A substantial subset of this topic interrogates the infrastructure researchers rely on to study Facebook visibility at all — chiefly Meta's URL Shares Dataset and its descendants. [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] both target the dataset's 100-public-shares censoring threshold as a source of serious, under-scrutinized bias: Allen et al. show it inflates apparent fake-news prevalence by roughly 4x relative to representative panel data, while Giglietto et al. show that monthly trends across 46 countries move in near-identical lockstep, implying that platform-wide ranking changes (not local political events) govern whether URLs cross the visibility threshold that makes them appear in the dataset at all — a finding with direct implications for any cross-country or longitudinal claim built on this data. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] applies the dataset to Germany, France, and Italy and explicitly cites these threshold and coverage limitations as grounds for avoiding direct US-EU comparison, even as it documents a real rise in untrustworthy URL shares around election years. [[Guess2021-ym]] offers the methodological baseline many of these studies build from or react against, using the "Condor" URL-level dataset to establish that low-credibility content is a minority but non-trivial share of circulation, concentrated among older and more conservative users. [[Allcott2019-gn]] complements this by tracking Facebook-versus-Twitter engagement ratios for fake news over time, finding a platform-specific post-2016 decline that is consistent with — though not proof of — the kind of governance interventions [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] documents directly. [[Yang2023-cg]] pushes the critique further by arguing that link-based misinformation measures like these systematically undercount the problem, since image posts (ignored by URL-based methods) actually outnumber link posts and show substantial partisan asymmetry in misleadingness. [[Yang2025-iv]] tackles a parallel methodological vulnerability from the coordination-detection side, showing that timing-based methods for flagging coordinated inauthentic sharing are evadable, and proposing share speed/frequency regularities as a more robust signal — a reminder that the question "what counts as organic visibility" is itself an active methodological battleground.

## Flagging, Fact-Checking, and the Politics of What Gets Suppressed

[[Holt2026-zq]] closes a loop opened by the moderation-as-reduction literature by examining what users actually flag as "False News" using the URL Shares Dataset, finding that reported links skew toward polarized political topics users merely disagree with rather than demonstrably false claims — a finding with pointed relevance now that Meta has shifted toward Community-Notes-style, user-driven flagging. This sits usefully alongside [[Gillespie2022-jx]]'s argument that moderation decisions (visibility reduction) are opaque by design, and alongside [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]], which shows that gatekeeping effects are not limited to Facebook's own ranking but begin further upstream at aggregators like Google News, with emotionally resonant (celebrity, malpractice) health topics generating disproportionate comment-driven engagement regardless of their scientific weight — an early, domain-specific illustration of the same emotion-engagement coupling central to the reactions literature above.

## Literacy and Individual Selection as the Residual Variable

Two papers round out the topic by shifting focus from platform mechanics to user cognition. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s integrative review of 169 studies maps the fragmented construct of "algorithm literacy," arguing that users' understanding of curation systems is itself a contingent, under-theorized variable shaping engagement with algorithmically delivered content. [[Wan2026-ai]] narrows this to political efficacy specifically, tracking Dutch users' actual click behavior in Google Discover to show that individual differences in felt political efficacy condition how much political content people select from an algorithmic feed. Both suggest that the visibility dynamics documented elsewhere in this topic — reaction-driven amplification, governance-driven reach penalties, dataset-threshold artifacts — are filtered through a layer of user literacy and motivation that remains comparatively under-measured, marking a natural frontier for future work bridging the platform-level and individual-level accounts assembled here.
