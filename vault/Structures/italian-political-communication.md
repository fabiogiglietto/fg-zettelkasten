# Italian Political Communication & Elections

## The arc of a research programme

This cluster traces roughly a decade of MINE's core empirical agenda: understanding how Italian elections are fought, amplified, and distorted across the hybrid media system, and how the methods for studying that system have had to evolve alongside the platforms themselves. Read together, the papers move from early theorizing about what "big data" does to political communication, through increasingly sophisticated empirical dissections of the 2018 and 2022 campaigns, toward a current frontier of LLM-assisted methodology and platform-accountability research that now extends beyond Italy.

## A founding diagnosis

[[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] functions as something like the programme's manifesto. Written as digital platforms became central to campaigning, it names three intertwined shifts—blurring organizational boundaries via networked publics, micro-targeted advertising, and strategic amplification in an attention-scarce hybrid media environment—that would structure nearly everything filed under this topic afterward. Its warning that traditional methods (self-reported exposure, fixed lists of "mainstream" sources) are inadequate for a fragmented, socially-mediated public sphere anticipates the entire methodological trajectory the later papers pursue.

The hybrid-media diagnosis itself has deeper roots in the corpus: [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], studying the 2013 Italian election, already shows Twitter functioning as a second screen to televised talk shows, with audience "participation" largely confined to opinion and requests for interaction rather than genuine agenda-shaping. Its finding that TV logic still governs Twitter's political peaks, and that even Beppe Grillo's M5S was nearly invisible in these talk-show-linked conversations, sets up a puzzle the later 2018-election papers resolve: M5S's strength lay not in broadcast-adjacent Twitter chatter but in a separately built, insular digital infrastructure.

## Partisanship, insularity, and coordinated amplification

[[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] picks up that thread directly for the 2018 campaign, showing that news sources favored by M5S and League supporters are markedly more insular on Twitter, and that insularity predicts a Facebook engagement signature—more shares per comment—consistent with amplification rather than contestation. Crucially, M5S emerges as uniquely able to weaponize this dynamic asymmetrically, amplifying favorable stories while contesting unfavorable ones, a pattern the paper links to its identity as an "anti-elitist cyber party" with a long-term investment in digital mobilization infrastructure—directly vindicating the "networked publics" vector flagged in [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]].

This insight about coordinated, non-organic amplification becomes a methodological research programme in its own right. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] operationalizes "coordinated link sharing behavior" across the 2018 and 2019 elections, showing that coordinated networks are dramatically more likely to spread problematic domains and previously flagged disinformation sources, and distinguishing centralized from clustered network topologies. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends this into a living, iterative workflow for the 2022 snap election, addressing the obsolescence problem of static blacklists by continuously surfacing new coordinated accounts from known ones. Its three case studies—an M5S-aligned hyperpartisan echo chamber generating fabricated polls, a religious-clickbait operation, and a proselytizing Messenger-bot network—demonstrate that the same behavioral signature (coordinated, rapid, repeated sharing) captures ideologically, commercially, and religiously motivated operations alike, generalizing the actor-agnostic detection logic first developed for 2018–2019.

## Methodological modernization: from BERT to LLMs

A cluster of more recent, explicitly methodological papers documents how the programme's toolkit has been rebuilt around large language models as the study population scales into the millions of posts. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks embedding models for clustering Italian political news shared before the 2018 and 2022 elections, finding that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large consistently outperforms the Italian-specific UmBERTo model—an important, somewhat uncomfortable finding for a research tradition invested in language-specific tooling. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] generalizes this into a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline spanning political classification, embedding-based clustering, and narrative labeling, and grapples reflexively with what it means to *validate* such a pipeline—arguing that crowdsourced human evaluation is no longer an adequate gold standard once LLMs begin to outperform low-skilled annotators, and that expert coders and multi-level, task-specific validation protocols are now necessary.

[[Paci2025-ag]] pushes this reflexive methodological turn further by directly interrogating the limits of LLMs as instruments for political-discourse research: testing several multilingual models on implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political speech (via the IMPAQTS corpus), it finds a persistent gap between model outputs and expert human interpretation, with Chain-of-Thought prompting helping only modestly. Together these three papers form a methodological throughline: as the programme leans increasingly on LLMs for classification, clustering, and labeling political content, it also produces sober, empirically grounded assessments of where those tools remain unreliable—especially for the pragmatically loaded, implicit language that is core to political rhetoric.

## Platform accountability and visibility

[[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] shifts the object of study from content circulation to platform governance itself, using the Meta Content Library (enabled by the EU's Digital Services Act) to show that Meta's political-content-reduction policy silently throttled Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach up to ten months before its officially announced rollout, and that its 2025 partial reversal favored mainstream politicians while extremist accounts had already adapted by posting more frequently, preserving their aggregate visibility. This paper reframes the programme's long-standing interest in "who gets seen" from an emergent property of audience behavior (as in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]) to a directly engineered, opaque policy effect—raising the DSA transparency regime itself as an object of empirical scrutiny and a new evidentiary resource for the field.

## Widening the frame: comparative and rhetorical extensions

The final set of papers signals the programme's expansion beyond Italy and beyond behavioral/network detection toward media-capture and rhetorical framing. [[Balluff2026-bv]] applies a quasi-experimental difference-in-differences design to the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre," finding that covert government-advertising arrangements produced a "pay positive to go negative" bias pattern—boosting a politician's visibility and depressing rivals' coverage without necessarily improving his own favorability—demonstrating that computational methods honed on Italian data can detect media capture even in ostensibly well-regulated democratic-corporatist systems. [[Mosca2026-yh]] returns to Italian newspapers but reframes "fake news" accusations themselves as a rhetorical weapon, proposing a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model in which politicians deploy such accusations for negative campaigning and outlets amplify them selectively—extending the programme's concern with strategic amplification (first flagged in [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]) into the domain of meta-discourse about misinformation itself. Finally, [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] steps back methodologically, critiquing the field's tendency toward oversimplified, unidimensional measures of political leaning and outlet-level analysis, and proposing a multi-level "political cartography" of story, outlet, and content dynamics—an implicit call to synthesize the granular, behavior-based approaches pioneered in the coordinated-sharing papers with broader comparative ambitions.

## Synthesis

Across these twelve papers, a consistent intellectual arc emerges: from an early recognition that digital platforms destabilize the boundaries and measurement conventions of political communication, through increasingly fine-grained empirical demonstrations of partisan insularity and coordinated amplification in the 2018 and 2022 Italian elections, to a current moment where the field must simultaneously exploit and critically interrogate LLMs as both analytical tools and objects of platform governance. The Italian case—repeatedly framed as a strategically useful "polarized pluralist" testbed—thus serves not only as the programme's original object of study but increasingly as the methodological proving ground from which techniques (coordinated-behavior detection, LLM-in-the-loop validation, DSA-enabled platform auditing) radiate outward to other democracies and other forms of strategic political communication.
