# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## A Methodological Lineage: From CooRnet to Multiplex Networks

This corpus traces a methodological arc that begins with a deceptively simple insight: coordination can be detected without ever adjudicating content truthfulness. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] establishes the foundational move—shifting disinformation research from content/actor classification toward behavior-based detection of accounts sharing identical URLs in anomalously tight time windows—and demonstrates on Italian 2018/2019 election data that such coordinated networks disproportionately carry problematic content. This "coordinated link sharing behavior" (CLSB) paradigm, operationalized in the open-source CooRnet package, becomes the signature tool extended throughout the researcher's subsequent work: [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] applies it to COVID-19 "infodemic" monitoring in Italy, showing how an iterative, content-agnostic workflow can surface previously unknown Covid-skeptic clusters nested within pre-existing political networks, while [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] formalizes this into a continuously updating workflow—seeding from known coordinated accounts, monitoring their best-performing posts, and using near-duplicate detection to surface new actors—applied to the 2022 Italian snap election, uncovering a Five Star Movement hyperpartisan network, a religious-page clickbait operation, and a proselytizing bot network. The VERA-AI line continues this trajectory: [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] documents coordinated gambling-promotion groups whose posting volume explodes after ChatGPT's release, revealing how generative AI intensifies rather than replaces established persuasion drivers, while [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] synthesizes three VERA-AI case studies (pro-Putin propaganda, gambling, unmoderated adult-content groups) into the broader concept of "Deceptive Information Operations," arguing that Meta's narrow CIB/CSH categories cannot capture manipulation that exploits platform affordances and engagement-driven design rather than relying on false content per se. [[Rogers2026-cy]], also from the vera.ai project, complements this with the "post-truth space" concept, mapping Moldovan Facebook discourse around the Russia-Ukraine war to show that influence often flows through "borderline" clusters bridging fringe and mainstream spaces rather than through fringe spaces directly.

Running parallel to this applied lineage is a more formal network-science tradition concerned with *how* to detect coordination at all. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] reframes coordination as a continuum rather than a binary, demonstrating on 2019 UK election data that coordination and automation are orthogonal—bot detection cannot substitute for coordination detection—and that the most tightly coordinated communities are often small activist fringes rather than mainstream party networks. [[Yang2025-iv]] pushes back against timing-based detection's vulnerability to evasion, proposing statistical regularities in sharing speed/frequency as more robust signals. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] and [[Iannucci2025-eg]] extend this into multiplex territory, showing that coordination signals across retweets, replies, mentions, hashtags, and URLs are only partially redundant, and that naive "flattening" of these layers (the dominant practice) discards structurally important communities—multiplex, time-aware models offering the best balance of integration and recall. [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE/t-CANE framework represents a further leap, modeling users as distributions over latent narrative clusters rather than direct interactions, enabling platform-agnostic cross-platform tracing and identifying a tiny set of "bridge users" who seed ~70% of narratives migrating between Truth Social and X. [[Minici2024-tf]]'s IOHunter pushes detection toward graph foundation models combining language and structural signals for out-of-distribution generalization across state-sponsored campaigns. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] synthesizes this entire detection literature into a PRISMA-guided survey, proposing a unifying definition built on actors, synergic actions, and intent, and arguing that coordination is orthogonal to—not synonymous with—harm or inauthenticity.

## Theorizing Coordination: Definitions, Attribution, and Impact

A persistent thread interrogates what "coordinated inauthentic behavior" actually means and whether it is the right lens at all. [[Starbird2019-qv]] supplies the conceptual bedrock here, reframing disinformation as *collaborative work* performed by and through crowds—blurring the line between orchestrated campaigns and organic participation, a tension picked up explicitly by [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]'s critique of creator-intent-centered "fake news" typologies in favor of attention to propagation dynamics. [[Thiele2025-ol]] sharpens this critical project into an "attribution problem," arguing that CIB conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent and proposing a rational-choice typology linking campaign scale, elaborateness, and disguise to a principal's resources and stakes. [[Keller2019-nk]]'s ground-truthed study of the South Korean NIS astroturfing campaign anticipates this move, showing that coordination patterns—not bot-like individual traits—are the most reliable and hardest-to-hide signal, while also demonstrating that even well-resourced, human-staffed campaigns had limited measurable discourse impact. That skepticism about impact recurs as a counter-current throughout the corpus: [[Eady2023-xg]] finds IRA exposure in 2016 was dwarfed by domestic media exposure and uncorrelated with attitude change; [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and its companion [[Di_Marco2026-xu]] formally model coordinated accounts' influence within retweet cascades, finding that real-world coordinated placements resemble random assignment far more than strategically optimal placement, suggesting CIB is often less pivotal than assumed; and [[Appel2026-qr]]'s large-scale FIES study of Meta-identified networks finds that correlations between exposure and downstream beliefs mostly vanish once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled—though it also shows reach is highly concentrated and driven mainly by reshares from non-network accounts, complicating any simple "limited effects" conclusion. [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers a different kind of corrective, finding that a major 2021 Twitter deplatforming event produced disproportionate "ripple effects" on a non-bot, human-driven community—evidence that network interventions can matter even where CIB per se was not the operative mechanism.

## Platform-Specific and National Case Studies

A substantial cluster applies and adapts these methods to specific platforms and polities, testing whether CIB travels across technical and political contexts. On Facebook, [[Song2025-yh]] uses CooRnet-style CLSB analysis to show that both pro- and anti-vaccine communities coordinate, complicating any assumption that coordination implies malice; [[Oprea2025-lf]] documents hyperactive-user amplification across Romanian parties' pages during the 2024 EU election, arguing platforms under-enforce their own anti-manipulation policies. Beyond Facebook, [[Luceri2025-tr]] confronts TikTok's video-first architecture as requiring methodological adaptation beyond text-centric approaches, a concern echoed by [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s attention to sound as an under-examined propaganda vector and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]]'s cross-platform visual analysis of the Sweden Democrats, which shows securitizing meme content concentrated strategically on TikTok to reach younger voters. [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extends detection to the decentralized platform Bluesky, while [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] examine Telegram and Twitter coordination respectively in authoritarian contexts (Russia/Belarus pro-government amplification; China's COVID-19 protest suppression and mobilization), illustrating the "dual-use" nature of coordination infrastructure. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] theorizes a further evolution in Serbia, where SNS-linked networks mimic authentic grassroots support precisely to evade standard CIB detection—a direct challenge to methods premised on identifying synchronized inauthenticity. Election-specific studies anchor this cluster empirically: [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] audits irregular Facebook ads in Brazil's 2018 election via volunteer browser plugins; [[Kansaon2025-id]] traces WhatsApp-based coordination fueling real-world protest mobilization in Brazil's 2022 election aftermath, including attacks on Supreme Court justices; [[Kim2026-wg]] documents geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising in the US 2016 election with measurable turnout effects; and [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceives Philippine influence operations as a form of political brokerage, foregrounding the political-economic infrastructure of manipulation rather than treating campaigns as isolated disinformation events.

## Actors, Labor, and Organizational Structure

A further strand turns from detection toward the organizational and human infrastructure behind coordination. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT analysis of Internet Research Agency employee CVs reconstructs the IRA as a labor-market actor embedded in Russia's media economy—young, inexperienced, Crimea-timed rather than US-election-timed—substantially revising prior assumptions about its workforce and motives. Bastos2025-ol probes whether troll farms carry identifiable visual signatures in profile imagery, while [[Gaw2025-ru]] (above) and [[Thiele2025-ol]] both push toward understanding principals and brokers rather than content alone. At the vernacular end, [[Graham2026-fb]] unsettles the human/bot binary itself through Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon, arguing that authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical property—a direct challenge to detection frameworks premised on a clean authentic/inauthentic divide. [[Tang2026-gu]] documents the industrialization of virality via AI-driven "matrix account systems" in Chinese short-video production, and [[Kim2026-br]] develops an explainable ML framework to trace two decades of suspected state-linked trolling in Korean news comments, finding morally condemning rhetoric—not direct praise—as the dominant and most-amplified strategy.

## The Horizon: Generative AI and Swarm Coordination

The newest frontier concerns how generative AI reshapes coordination itself. [[Orlando2025-ul]] simulates LLM agents in information-operation scenarios, finding that mere "teammate awareness" among agents—without explicit deliberation—produces coordination nearly as strong as full collective decision-making, a sobering result for governance. [[Schroeder2026-im]] extrapolates this into a policy-forum argument about "malicious AI swarms" capable of manufacturing consensus, poisoning training data ("LLM grooming"), and evading synchrony-based detectors, proposing layered governance responses. These sit alongside [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]'s empirical documentation of generative AI's actual uptake within gambling-promotion networks, bridging speculative and observed AI-driven coordination.

## Media Ecosystems and the Limits of the Coordination Frame

Finally, several papers situate coordinated behavior within broader media-ecosystem and propaganda theory, questioning whether CIB is best understood in isolation. [[Benkler2018-lw]] remains the touchstone here, arguing the 2016 "epistemic crisis" stemmed from an asymmetric, radicalized right-wing media ecosystem rather than technology or Russian interference alone—a framing explicitly invoked by [[Copland2025-em]]'s account of Sky News Australia's transformation into a transnational right-wing propaganda node, and by [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction," which traces how digitally networked elites and hyper-partisan media co-produce intra-party radicalization. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] similarly traces how originally organic social-justice discourse ("stay woke") was captured through both platform-specific and cross-platform coordinated amplification. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] shows coordinated retweet networks amplifying contrarian scientific experts whose content later surfaces in low-trust news outlets, while [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Zhao2025-ny]] examine how propaganda campaigns (#IStandWithPutin; hashtag hijacking) exploit, rather than evade, the very infrastructures of trust and attention that platforms provide. [[Darius2026-xl]]'s cross-platform EU election study complicates any assumption that CIB is the primary driver of populist advantage, finding that radical parties' edge lies mainly in organic audience *support* rather than coordinated activity—a useful check against over-attributing political success to coordination. [[Pante2025-pq]] applies similar skepticism reflexively, re-testing claims of inter-state CIB collaboration with modern methods and control datasets. Finally, [[Park2026-tr]], though not substantively about coordination, documents the legal chilling effects facing researchers in this very field—a reminder that the empirical study of coordinated inauthentic behavior is itself conducted under conditions of real professional and legal risk.
