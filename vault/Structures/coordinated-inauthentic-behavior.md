# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## The CooRnet Lineage and the Founding Method

The topic's anchor is the author's own methodological program: coordinated link-sharing behaviour (CLSB) as a content-agnostic, actor-behaviour-focused alternative to fact-checking and bot-hunting. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] establishes the founding argument — that disinformation research should shift from adjudicating truth or flagging bad actors to detecting *coordinated collective action*, operationalized as networks of pages, groups, and profiles sharing identical URLs in tight temporal windows. This produces the empirical signature that recurs throughout the corpus: coordinated entities are disproportionately linked to problematic domains and blacklisted pages, and the balance of "openly political" versus "deceptively non-political" entities in a network predicts different sharing strategies. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] extends this into the pandemic, using CooRnet to power the MINE-FACTS fact-checking tool and documenting how coordinated networks evolve tactics (link-laundering, first-comment link placement, religious-group cover) to evade detection — an early statement of the "content-agnostic beats content-based" thesis that will recur. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] then solves the staleness problem inherent to any list-based approach: a workflow that starts from known coordinated seeds and iteratively surfaces new accounts, applied to the 2022 Italian election to reveal hyperpartisan, click-economy, and religious-proselytism operations side by side — demonstrating that the same behavioural signature (coordinated sharing) can index ideological, commercial, and even devotional agendas indiscriminately. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] applies the same underlying CLSB-derived dataset to trace how "Alternative Influence Network" figures and legacy media co-produce COVID-19 problematic narratives via remediation, showing the method's utility for mapping influence beyond the coordinated core itself.

## VERA-AI, Alert, and Applied Detection Infrastructure

A second cluster represents the operationalization of this methodology into monitoring infrastructure. [[Rogers2026-cy]] introduces "post-truth spaces" as a mapping concept for verification specialists working within the vera.ai project, combining network analysis with source-provenance investigation and an influence metric — demonstrated on Moldovan Facebook discourse about the Russia-Ukraine war, where the analytically interesting finding is that a "borderline" cluster bridging post-truth and mainstream spaces, not the post-truth core itself, drives centrality. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] and [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] both mine the same detection pipeline for case studies — a pro-Putin propaganda network, gambling promotion, and poorly moderated adult-content groups — converging on the concept of a "Zombie Internet" where engagement-driven platform design, not any single actor's intent, sustains manipulation at scale. The gambling case in particular documents an empirical inflection point: post-ChatGPT, coordinated gambling networks show a 13,000%+ volume increase and visibly AI-generated persuasive imagery, suggesting generative AI is intensifying rather than replacing established coordination tactics. Together these three papers argue that Meta's own CIB/CSH enforcement categories are too narrow — built for coordination-plus-inauthenticity, but blind to operations whose harm derives from affordance exploitation and synthetic content rather than fake identities per se.

## Refining and Generalizing Detection Methodology

A dense cluster of recent computational papers pushes the detection methodology itself forward, largely in dialogue with (and sometimes explicit contrast to) the CooRnet/coordination-network tradition. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] both tackle the same core limitation — that single-modality, time-agnostic coordination networks miss real structure — via multiplex, temporally-weighted representations; Iannucci's time-aware multiplex model and Mannocci's comparison of five multimodal operationalizations converge on the finding that no single action type (retweet, hashtag, URL, reply) dominates, and that naive network flattening (the field's dominant practice) discards structurally important nodes. [[Yang2025-iv]] attacks a related vulnerability directly: because timing-based coordination signals are trivially manipulable, the paper proposes exploiting statistical regularities in *speed and frequency* of Facebook link-sharing instead. [[Minici2024-tf]] and [[Gerard2025-br]] push toward generalization and scale: IOHunter combines language models with graph neural networks for cross-campaign, out-of-distribution detection of IO drivers, while CANE/t-CANE abandons interaction-based graphs altogether in favor of latent narrative-cluster affiliation, surfacing a small set of "bridge users" who seed cross-platform narrative migration between Truth Social and X. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] provides the field's synthetic capstone — a PRISMA-based survey of 122 papers proposing a general definition of coordinated online behaviour (actors, synergic actions, intent) along four orthogonal axes (authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, time-variance), explicitly arguing that platform-derived CIB is too narrow and threat-centric a lens for the broader phenomenon these methods actually detect.

## Theorizing Intent, Attribution, and Definition

Running parallel to the computational refinements is a more conceptual reckoning with what "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" should even mean. [[Thiele2025-ol]] argues CIB conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent and proposes a rational-choice attribution model keyed to campaign scale, elaborateness, and disguise, yielding an eight-type typology of principals. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations entirely as political brokerage — covert campaign firms as intermediaries between clients and voters in the 2022 Philippine election — shifting attention from accounts and content to political-economic infrastructure, a move that resonates with Poliakoff's organizational excavation below. These theoretical interventions implicitly critique the network-detection literature for treating coordination as a purely structural signature, divorced from the strategic and organizational logics that produce it.

## Case Studies: State, Party, and Platform-Specific Operations

A substantial empirical wing applies coordination detection (often CooRnet-adjacent methods) to specific actors and contexts. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] uses OSINT on leaked CVs to reconstruct the IRA's organizational hierarchy, showing it as a labor-market actor — recruited around the Crimea annexation, not the 2016 election — that troubles the "foreign influence operation" frame with a more mundane picture of outsourced media-industry labor. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents a structurally different mode of digital authoritarianism in Serbia's SNS networks, which mimic grassroots support rather than running centralized bot farms, evading the detection paradigms built for the IRA case. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Kim2026-br]] extend coordination detection to Telegram (Russia/Belarus regime amplification) and two decades of Korean news comments (state-linked trolling dominated by "condemning" rather than "praising" rhetoric), while [[Copland2025-em]] traces Sky News Australia's dual-platform strategy as a node in transnational network propaganda. [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers an important counter-note: examining Twitter's deplatforming of Arizona election-audit accounts, the authors find that only 10 accounts met coordination thresholds — the network's collapse post-ban was a hub/authority effect, not CIB-driven, cautioning against over-attributing organic political mobilization to coordination. Bastos2025-ol adds a visual dimension, mining the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium's troll-farm imagery for nationally distinctive self-presentation signatures.

## Elections, Platforms, and the Limits of Reach

Several papers interrogate coordination's actual electoral and epistemic consequences, often with skeptical findings. The paired studies [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[noauthor_undated-bm]] (the FIES Meta collaboration) show that 49 deceptive networks reached tens of millions during the 2020 US election, but that most reach came from unaffiliated reshares rather than direct network posting, and that exposure's correlation with downstream political outcomes evaporates once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled — a sobering corrective to causal overreach in the field. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formalizes this skepticism further: a post-hoc influence-maximization framework applied to UK election retweet cascades shows real coordinated accounts achieve far less influence than optimal or greedy placements would predict, resembling random rather than strategic positioning. [[Pante2025-pq]] similarly re-tests earlier claims of inter-state CIB collaboration on X and finds they don't survive rigorous control comparisons. Against this, [[Kansaon2025-id]], [[Oprea2025-lf]], and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extend detection to WhatsApp (Brazilian protest mobilization), Facebook (Romanian EP election hyperactive users), and Bluesky (real-time bot coordination), arguing coordination does translate into real-world consequence in these settings — suggesting the "does coordination matter" question is highly context- and platform-dependent rather than settled.

## Platform-Specific Adaptation and Domain Extension

[[Luceri2025-tr]] confronts the field's text-centric bias directly, adapting network-similarity detection to TikTok's video-first structure. [[Song2025-yh]] applies CLSB/CooRnet methodology itself to pro- and anti-vaccine Facebook communities, showing coordination is ideologically agnostic — a finding that complicates any simple equation of coordination with malice, echoing Mannocci2026's orthogonality argument. [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] applies coordination detection to the 2022 China COVID protests, showing the same infrastructure enabling both connective-action mobilization and state-aligned repression.

## Adjacent Phenomena: Bots, Agents, and Agenda-Building

A final cluster sits at the boundary of CIB proper, examining automated and algorithmic amplification more broadly. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] repositions social bots as fourth-category agenda-builders competing with campaigns, press, and public. [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as a bot-assisted narrative-capture tactic. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] finds a coordinated, overwhelmingly contrarian retweet network amplifying anti-consensus COVID experts — notably *not* explained by bot activity, reinforcing that coordination and automation are separable phenomena. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] documents cross-platform coordinated amplification in the politicization of "woke" discourse. Most speculatively, [[Orlando2025-ul]] uses generative-agent simulation to show that LLM agents spontaneously reproduce classic coordination signatures — network density, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification — once given mere awareness of teammates, suggesting the behavioural signatures this whole literature detects may soon be manufactured by non-human actors at trivial cost, a fitting horizon problem for the methodological program the corpus otherwise takes as given. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]], on campaign persistence and event-appropriation, gestures toward the same temporal-adaptivity theme threaded through Giglietto's iterative-monitoring papers, closing the loop between detection and the moving target it is built to track.
