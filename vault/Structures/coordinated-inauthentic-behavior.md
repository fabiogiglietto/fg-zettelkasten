# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## The methodological core: from CLSB to self-updating alert systems

The backbone of this topic is the line of work descending from [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], which first operationalized "coordinated link sharing behavior" (CLSB) as a way of studying disinformation through *action* rather than content or actor labels. That paper's central move — treating near-simultaneous, repeated URL sharing across pages, groups and profiles as a signal of inauthentic coordination, independent of whether the shared content is itself false — becomes the load-bearing assumption for everything that follows. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] operationalizes this insight for a practical use case (COVID-19 fact-checking with Facta.news), introducing the CooRnet package as a production tool and documenting how coordinated networks evade detection through link laundering, first-comment link placement, and exploitation of religious or community groups. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] pushes the methodology from a static, list-based artifact toward a genuinely self-updating alert system: starting from a seed list of known coordinated accounts, it iteratively surfaces new ones by monitoring overperforming posts, explicitly addressing the problem that any fixed list of "bad actors" decays as networks adapt, get banned, or rebrand. This workflow logic — content-agnostic, actor-agnostic, continuously refreshed — is the direct ancestor of the vera.ai WP4 tooling visible in [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]], both of which apply the same detection pipeline to new problem domains (AI-generated gambling promotion; a broader "Deceptive Information Operations" category spanning propaganda, gambling, and adult-content networks). [[Marino2023-9137f448]] shows a complementary use of the same detected-network infrastructure: once CLSB has identified a covid-skeptic cluster, the analytic question shifts to *who* those networks amplify (an "Intellectual Dark Web" of alternative influencers) and how legacy media remediates their claims back into the mainstream.

Across this arc, [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] marks an important conceptual maturation: it argues that Meta's own CIB/CSH categories, and by extension the CLSB tradition built partly in dialogue with them, are too narrow, because manipulative effectiveness does not require either coordination *or* inauthenticity alone but their convergence with platform-affordance exploitation. This self-critical turn — using the tool to expose the limits of the concept the tool was built to operationalize — is a throughline connecting the earliest CLSB paper to the most recent.

## Widening the lens: platforms, modalities, and structure

A second cluster takes the CLSB logic and asks whether it generalizes beyond Facebook links. [[Song2025-yh]] applies CooRnet directly to compare pro- and anti-vaccine CLSB networks in the UK and US, showing that coordination is ideologically agnostic — it can scaffold misinformation or credible public-health messaging depending on who wields it. [[Yang2025-iv]] tackles a structural weakness of timing-based detection (its trivial manipulability) by proposing sharing-speed and -frequency regularities as more evasion-resistant signals, a direct methodological rejoinder to the CLSB family's dependence on synchronized timestamps. Parallel efforts extend coordination detection to non-Facebook ecosystems: [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapts a "Rapid Spread Network" approach to WhatsApp's closed, encrypted architecture in Brazil; [[Luceri2025-tr]] confronts the video-first affordances of TikTok, where text-centric signals break down; [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] moves detection into real time on the decentralized Bluesky network; [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] traces coordinated forwarding among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus; and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] applies coordination detection to multilingual Twitter activity around China's COVID-19 protests, revealing dual-use infrastructure supporting both protest and repression.

A related methodological front asks not *where* to detect coordination but *how* to model it more precisely. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] both argue that single-modality, time-agnostic approaches (the implicit baseline of early CLSB work) miss important structure, proposing multiplex and temporally-weighted representations that treat retweets, mentions, hashtags and URLs as distinct but complementary coordination layers. [[Minici2024-tf]] and [[Gerard2025-br]] push detection toward foundation-model and cross-platform paradigms — IOHunter fuses language and graph models to generalize across campaigns and platforms, while CANE/t-CANE models users through shared narrative-cluster participation rather than direct interaction, identifying a tiny set of "bridge users" who ferry narratives between Truth Social and X. Bastos2025-ol adds a visual dimension entirely absent from the link-sharing tradition, mining profile imagery in the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium dataset for attribution signals. Together these papers suggest the field is moving from a single robust heuristic (CLSB) toward a toolkit of complementary signals — temporal, multiplex, visual, narrative — that can be combined depending on platform and threat model.

## Does coordination actually matter? Scale, skepticism, and reassessment

A notable countercurrent within the corpus questions how much coordinated activity actually accomplishes. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formally models the influence coordinated accounts could achieve in retweet cascades and finds that real-world coordinated placements look statistically close to random, far below optimal or even resource-matched strategic benchmarks — a sobering check on assumptions that CIB is inherently pivotal. [[Pante2025-pq]] revisits claims of inter-state collaboration between state-linked information operations and finds that, benchmarked against proper control datasets and modern multi-signal detection, much of the prior evidence for coordination does not hold up. [[Simeone2025-vo]] documents a case (the Arizona Election Review community after Twitter's 2021 deplatforming) where network-level effects were dramatic but explicitly *not* attributable to CIB — real human disengagement, not bot withdrawal, drove the collapse of the network's authority structure. And the large Meta-collaboration study reported in [[Appel2026-qr]] (duplicated as [[noauthor_undated-bm]]) complicates the picture from the exposure side: deceptive networks reached tens of millions of users, but overwhelmingly through reshares by ordinary, non-network accounts rather than direct network posting, and correlations between exposure and downstream attitudes mostly evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. Read together, this cluster tempers the CLSB tradition's implicit premise — that finding coordination is tantamount to finding harm — by insisting on downstream measures of actual reach, influence, and causal effect.

## Conceptual reckonings: what is "coordination," "inauthenticity," and "manipulation"?

Several papers step back from empirical detection to interrogate the concepts themselves. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers the most systematic critique, arguing that "coordinated inauthentic behavior" conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent and is poorly suited to attributing campaigns to their covert principals; it proposes a rational-choice typology built around scale, elaborateness, and disguise instead. This echoes [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]]'s argument that Meta's enforcement categories are too narrow, and resonates with [[Graham2026-fb]]'s Reddit "botting" case study, which dissolves the human/bot binary altogether, reframing authenticity as a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical property — a direct challenge to detection frameworks that presume a clean ground truth. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] contributes the observation that manipulation campaigns are not static objects to be detected once but persistent, adaptive processes that appropriate emerging events — a claim that dovetails with the self-updating logic of [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] and motivates why alert systems, not one-off audits, are the appropriate methodological response.

## Case studies: coordination in specific political and national contexts

A substantial share of the corpus applies coordination detection, or coordination-adjacent analysis, to specific national and political settings, building a comparative map of how CIB manifests under different regimes and media systems. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents Serbia's SNS party mimicking authentic grassroots support in ways that evade standard detection, arguing digital authoritarianism is evolving beyond the centralized bot farms that most tools are built to catch. [[Copland2025-em]] traces how Sky News Australia became a node in transnational right-wing "network propaganda," using YouTube and Facebook diffusion patterns rather than direct coordination signals. [[Oprea2025-lf]] applies a manual "Authenticity Matrix" to hyperactive Facebook sharers during Romania's 2024 European Parliament campaign, while [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes Philippine influence operations as a form of political brokerage, foregrounding the political-economic infrastructure behind manipulation rather than its network signature. [[Kim2026-br]] extends the timescale dramatically, building an explainable ML framework to trace two decades of suspected state-linked trolling in Korean news comments, finding that condemning rhetoric — not praise — is the dominant and most-amplified strategy. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] complements this network-and-content-centric literature with an organizational history of the IRA itself, using leaked CVs to show it functioned as a conventional media-labor-market actor rather than a purely covert operation — a useful reminder that behind every coordinated network sits a mundane organizational structure.

## The near horizon: bots, algorithms, and generative AI

A final set of papers gestures toward how the object of study is shifting. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]], [[Zhao2025-ny]], and [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] examine bots and coordinated amplification as agenda-builders and narrative-shapers in electoral and scientific discourse, while [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] show that algorithmic curation and microtargeted advertising can produce coordination-like effects (concentrated attention, targeted suppression) without any detectable coordinated *network* at all — a caution against equating detected coordination with the full universe of manipulation. [[Goel2025-iq]] pushes in a similar direction, showing that source-level, domain-based misinformation metrics (a category adjacent to but distinct from CLSB) miss the widespread repurposing of true mainstream content into misleading narratives. Looking forward, [[Orlando2025-ul]] and [[Schroeder2026-im]] (as [[Schroeder2026-im]]) speculate on what coordination detection must confront next: LLM-based agentic swarms that can spontaneously reproduce the coordination signatures — synchronized amplification, unified messaging, adaptive narrative convergence — that CLSB and its successors were built to catch in human-run networks, but that operate with far greater speed, adaptability, and mimicry of authentic behavior. This positions the entire CLSB/CooRnet/vera.ai lineage at an inflection point: the self-updating alert-system paradigm pioneered in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] was designed to keep pace with human networks that rebrand and evade; the open question raised by this final cluster is whether that same architecture can keep pace with generative, self-optimizing coordination that no longer needs a human hand at all.
