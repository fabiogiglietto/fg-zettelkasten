# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## The Methodological Core: From CLSB to CooRnet

The topic's spine runs through Giglietto and colleagues' decade-long development of Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) as a content-agnostic alternative to fact-checking and bad-actor detection. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] lays the founding argument: disinformation research should shift from adjudicating truth or unmasking individual bad actors toward detecting *collective coordinated action*, operationalized as near-simultaneous, repeated URL sharing across pages, groups, and profiles. This behavioral signature proves empirically tied to problematic domains and known disinformation sources in the 2018–2019 Italian elections, and it establishes the two structural "ideal types" (centralized vs. clustered networks) that recur throughout later work. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] operationalizes this into the CooRnet toolkit and the MINE-FACTS workflow, showing that CLSB-derived actor lists outperform routine fact-checking at surfacing problematic COVID-19 content, while cataloguing evasion tactics (link laundering, first-comment link hiding, religious-group cover) that later papers keep encountering. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] closes the loop methodologically by solving the staleness problem of static actor lists, proposing an iterative monitoring workflow that starts from known coordinated seeds and continuously surfaces new accounts—demonstrated on the 2022 Italian election across a hyperpartisan political network, a clickbait/click-economy operation, and a religious-proselytism network, prefiguring the multi-case "typology of harms" approach that dominates the most recent work.

That approach crystallizes in [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]], which explicitly reframes CLSB-detected networks not as instances of a single motive but as **Deceptive Information Operations** (DIOs)—structurally convergent phenomena (deceptive content + platform-affordance exploitation + coordination) spanning pro-Putin propaganda, gambling promotion, and adult-content/fraud groups, all sheltered by the same governance blind spots ("Zombie Internet"). [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] narrows into one of these cases in depth, tracing how coordinated gambling networks' visual persuasion strategies were transformed by generative AI after ChatGPT's late-2022 release, with a documented structural break in posting volume—showing CLSB detection married to multimodal (image) analysis and diffusion-of-innovation framing. [[Rogers2026-cy]], produced under the vera.ai umbrella, complements this by introducing the "post-truth space" concept for locating hard-to-verify clusters for fact-checkers, using network mapping, provenance investigation, and an influence-via-proximity metric—demonstrated on Moldovan Facebook discourse around the Russia-Ukraine war—explicitly positioning itself alongside CIB, alternative influence networks, and participatory propaganda as one of several complementary lenses. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] extends the coordination-detection logic to Telegram forwarding networks in authoritarian Russia/Belarus, while [[Song2025-yh]] applies CooRnet-style CLSB comparatively (UK vs. US, pro- vs. anti-vaccine) to argue that coordination itself is ideologically agnostic—a claim that sits provocatively against the field's default assumption that CLSB signals malice. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] traces onward consequences of CLSB-identified covid-skeptic clusters, showing how "Alternative Influence Networks" and legacy media co-produce amplification through remediation.

## Sharpening and Contesting the Detection Toolkit

A second cluster interrogates and extends detection methodology itself, often explicitly responding to CLSB's known weaknesses. [[Yang2025-iv]] directly targets the timing-manipulability of coordination signals, proposing sharing-speed/frequency regularities as more evasion-resistant features. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] and [[Iannucci2025-eg]] tackle the multimodality problem from complementary angles—systematically comparing five ways of integrating retweet/reply/mention/hashtag/URL layers and showing multiplex community detection as the most balanced strategy, and formalizing a time-aware multiplex collaboration model validated against 26 benchmark datasets, respectively. [[Minici2024-tf]] and [[Gerard2025-br]] push toward generalization and cross-platform robustness: IOHunter as a graph-foundation-model detector transferable across six state-sponsored campaigns, and CANE/t-CANE as a platform-agnostic narrative-cluster embedding that surfaces "bridge users" driving cross-platform diffusion between Truth Social and X. [[Luceri2025-tr]] and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extend detection into under-studied venues—TikTok's video-first ecosystem and real-time Bluesky bot coordination—testifying to the topic's expanding platform scope. Two papers turn a more skeptical eye on the enterprise: [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formally models the causal influence of coordinated nodes in UK election retweet cascades and finds real CIB accounts achieve influence far below optimal placement, resembling random assignment—suggesting CIB may be less pivotal than assumed—while [[Pante2025-pq]] re-examines claims of inter-state IO collaboration and argues prior findings don't survive rigorous control-dataset benchmarking. Together these serve as an important corrective counterweight within the corpus's otherwise detection-optimistic thrust.

Overarching all of this, [[Mannocci2026-kc]]'s PRISMA survey of 122 papers performs the field's self-reflexive synthesis: it reconciles fragmented industry (Meta's CIB) and academic definitions into a general framework built on actors, synergic actions, and intent, and decomposes coordination research into detection and characterization tasks along four orthogonal axes (authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, time-variance)—effectively providing the conceptual scaffolding that the rest of the corpus operates within, often implicitly. [[Thiele2025-ol]] mounts a parallel theoretical intervention specifically on the *attribution* problem, critiquing "coordinated inauthentic behavior" as conflating inauthenticity with intent, and proposing a rational-choice typology of principals inferred from campaign scale, elaborateness, and disguise. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]], more briefly, flags the temporal adaptiveness of manipulation campaigns as they appropriate emerging events—an observation that recurs empirically in several case studies below.

## Empirical Case Studies: Actors, Platforms, and Political Contexts

A large empirical wing applies coordination detection (or coordination-adjacent analysis) to specific political and platform contexts. On elections and state-linked operations: [[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion entry [[noauthor_undated-bm]] jointly deliver the FIES analysis of 49 Meta-identified deceptive networks in the US 2020 election, the topic's most methodologically rich exposure study, showing reach is concentrated in a few networks, driven mostly by non-network reshares, and that observed political effects vanish under confounder adjustment—an important reality check against inflated claims of influence-operation impact. [[Kim2026-wg]] complements this with direct ad-exposure evidence of geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising in 2016, addressing the null-finding critique of earlier IRA studies. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] provides rare organizational grounding via OSINT analysis of IRA employee CVs, recasting the "troll factory" as a labor-market institution rather than a purely covert one. [[Kansaon2025-id]] extends coordination detection into WhatsApp's closed ecosystem in Brazil, linking coordinated messaging to real-world post-election mobilization, while [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents a harder-to-detect, mimetic form of CIB tied to Serbia's ruling party, and [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes Philippine influence operations as political brokerage infrastructure via interviews with IO practitioners. [[Kim2026-br]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] extend the geographic reach further—two decades of suspected state-linked trolling in Korean news comments, and multilingual coordinated activity around China's COVID-19 protests—both foregrounding how coordination signatures intersect with digital repression and moral-emotional framing rather than simple boosterism.

On media and platform ecosystems: [[Copland2025-em]] traces Sky News Australia's transformation into a transnational right-wing propaganda node via distinct Facebook/YouTube strategies; Bastos2025-ol mines the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium dataset for visual-identity signatures of troll farms; [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows that deplatforming produces network-wide "ripple effects" on authority structure even where CIB was *not* the operative mechanism—a useful boundary case distinguishing coordination from organic hub structures; and [[Graham2025-gp]] offers a case study of #IStandWithPutin as propaganda that exploits, rather than evades, platforms' truth-infrastructure.

## Adjacent and Emerging Phenomena

A final set of papers tests the boundaries of the CIB concept against phenomena that resist clean classification. [[Graham2026-fb]] uses Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon to argue that human/bot and authentic/inauthentic are unstable binaries better understood as culturally negotiated performances—a direct challenge to detection frameworks premised on hard authenticity classification. [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as strategic narrative contest involving bots, while [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] investigate bot/algorithmic influence on agenda-building and account visibility (Ohio midterms; pre-X Twitter feed changes) without invoking coordination detection per se, situating automated influence within broader platform-governance debates. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] identifies a genuinely coordinated, predominantly contrarian retweet network amplifying anti-consensus COVID experts, explicitly distinguishing coordination from bot automation and linking it to news-media pathways. Looking forward, [[Orlando2025-ul]] and [[Schroeder2026-im]] address the field's emerging frontier: generative and agentic AI. Orlando's generative agent-based simulation shows LLM agents spontaneously reproducing IO coordination mechanics—even mere teammate awareness suffices to generate dense, synchronized amplification—while Schroeder's policy piece extrapolates this into the systemic threat of "malicious AI swarms" capable of manufacturing consensus at unprecedented scale. [[Tang2026-gu]] grounds this trajectory empirically in China's "AI slop" creator economy, where matrix-account systems and generative tools industrialize virality itself—suggesting that the coordination methodologies developed for detecting inauthentic political networks will increasingly need to contend with coordination as a normalized production logic of the platformized creative economy, not only as a covert influence tactic.
