# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## The CooRnet lineage: from method to workflow to infrastructure

The methodological core of this collection begins with [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], which reframes disinformation research around *coordinated collective action* rather than content veracity or actor identity — the founding move behind Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) and the CooRnet package. Its Italian-election analysis establishes the recurring empirical signature that structures nearly every later paper in this hub: coordinated networks disproportionately amplify problematic domains, cluster into either centralized or distributed topologies, and blend openly political with deceptively "non-political" entities. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] operationalizes this into an applied tool for fact-checkers (MINE-FACTS), showing CLSB can surface Covid-skeptic networks faster than manual fact-checking and documenting the evasive tactics — link laundering, first-comment link placement, religious-page cover — that coordinated actors use to survive detection. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] then converts the one-shot detection algorithm into a continuously updating *workflow*, addressing the field's central maintenance problem (static lists go stale) by using known coordinated accounts as seeds to surface new ones, applied across three heterogeneous 2022 Italian-election case studies (hyperpartisan, click-economy, religious-proselytism networks). [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends the same underlying CLSB-derived dataset outward from network detection into content and influencer analysis, showing how "Alternative Influence Networks" and legacy-media remediation sustain Covid-skeptic narrative circulation — demonstrating that CooRnet-identified networks are not endpoints but starting points for downstream discourse analysis. Two recent papers push the framework into new content modalities and threats: [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] tracks how generative AI has intensified coordinated gambling-promotion networks on Facebook (documenting an exponential post-volume jump after ChatGPT's release), and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a broader analytic category — spanning state propaganda, gambling promotion, and poorly moderated adult-content networks — arguing that Meta's narrow CIB/CSH categories miss operations whose harm isn't reducible to coordination-plus-inauthenticity. Together these six papers trace an arc from method invention, to applied tooling, to conceptual generalization beyond the original CLSB use case.

## Domain and platform extensions

A second cluster applies coordination-detection logic to new platforms and topical domains. [[Song2025-yh]] uses CooRnet directly to compare pro- and anti-vaccine CLSB on UK/US Facebook, showing coordination itself is ideologically agnostic — it can scaffold institutional health communication as readily as misinformation, with structural and credibility profiles diverging sharply by national context. [[Rogers2026-cy]], produced within the same vera.ai ecosystem, proposes the "post-truth space" as a complementary mapping concept for fact-checkers, using network proximity/influence metrics (rather than coordination per se) to locate fringe clusters bridging into mainstream discourse in Moldovan Russia-Ukraine coverage. Platform-specific adaptations proliferate: [[Kansaon2025-id]] extends coordination detection into WhatsApp's closed, encrypted ecosystem via a "Rapid Spread Network" approach, tracing Brazilian coordinated messaging from misinformation to real-world Supreme Court attacks and street mobilization; [[Luceri2025-tr]] confronts the video-first affordances of TikTok, arguing text-centric CIB methods do not transfer straightforwardly; [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] targets real-time bot-coordination detection on the decentralized platform Bluesky; [[Yang2025-iv]] proposes a Facebook-specific innovation — detecting coordination via statistical regularities in sharing *speed and frequency* rather than manipulable timing signals, directly addressing an evasion vulnerability in earlier CLSB-style methods. Regionally, [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] applies coordination detection to pro-government Telegram channels in Russia/Belarus, and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] to multilingual Twitter activity during China's COVID-19 protests, both showing how authoritarian information ecosystems weaponize the same coordinative affordances that democratic-context studies treat as manipulation signals. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] complicates this further by arguing Serbia's SNS-linked networks are *designed* to evade standard detection by mimicking authentic grassroots support — a direct challenge to the assumption that coordination signatures are reliably distinguishable from organic behavior.

## Methodological innovation and critique within the detection paradigm

A dense technical cluster interrogates and extends the *mechanics* of coordination detection itself. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] systematically compares five ways of operationalizing multimodality (monomodal, layered, flattened, multiplex, intersected), finding an inherent trade-off between integration strength and detection inclusiveness, with multiplex community detection as the most balanced compromise — a finding [[Iannucci2025-eg]] builds on directly by adding a temporal decay kernel to multiplex layers, benchmarking against 26 labeled datasets and arguing that both temporal proximity and modality-preservation are necessary to resist dilution tactics. [[Minici2024-tf]] takes the graph-learning route further with IOHunter, a foundation-model approach fusing language and graph neural signals for cross-campaign generalization, including few-shot and out-of-distribution detection. [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE/t-CANE framework reframes cross-platform coordination altogether, modeling users via shared narrative-cluster participation rather than direct interaction ties, revealing a small set of "bridge users" who seed most narrative migration between Truth Social and X. Two papers turn explicitly critical: [[Pante2025-pq]] re-examines prior claims of inter-state coordination and finds they don't survive rigorous benchmarking against control datasets, while [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formally models the *influence* (not just presence) of coordinated nodes in retweet cascades and finds real coordinated accounts perform far below optimal or even random-matched benchmarks — a structural argument that CIB may be less pivotal to diffusion than commonly assumed. [[Thiele2025-ol]] steps back from detection entirely to theorize the *attribution problem*, critiquing "coordinated inauthentic behavior" itself as analytically inadequate for conflating inauthenticity with intent, and proposing a rational-choice typology of principals' strategic calculus. This critical stratum is essential counterweight to the detection-tool papers: it asks not just "can we find coordination" but "what does finding it actually tell us."

## Elections, platform governance, and the limits of coordination as a threat model

A final group examines coordination's role in electoral contexts and interrogates whether it is really the operative mechanism behind political harm. The Meta-partnered FIES studies — [[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion [[noauthor_undated-bm]] — offer the field's most consequential empirical corrective: deceptive networks reached tens of millions of US users in 2020, but reach was extremely concentrated, driven mostly by *reshares from unaffiliated accounts* rather than direct network activity, and associations with downstream political outcomes (discernment, election legitimacy beliefs) evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled — a sobering caution against causal overreach common in CIB scholarship. [[Simeone2025-vo]] independently supports this skepticism, finding that Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of Arizona "audit" accounts produced huge network ripple effects that were *not* attributable to coordinated bot activity, implying human disengagement rather than CIB was the operative mechanism. [[Oprea2025-lf]] instead documents concrete hyperactive-user amplification across all major Romanian parties' Facebook pages, arguing platforms fail to enforce their own anti-manipulation policies despite regulatory pressure (DSA, EU Code of Practice). Adjacent studies examine coordination as it intersects with algorithmic and platform-owner dynamics: [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] shows Twitter/X's post-Musk algorithm rewarded agitation and Musk-proximity over ideology per se; [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] models bots as "agenda-builders" shaping campaign sentiment; [[Smith2025-kc]] identifies "attention brokerage" as a causal mechanism generating network structure via amplification. Several papers push into emerging or adjacent territory — [[Orlando2025-ul]] simulates LLM agents spontaneously reproducing IO coordination tactics without explicit instruction, previewing synthetic-actor threats; Bastos2025-ol and [[Kim2026-br]] pursue attribution via visual identity and moral-emotional rhetoric respectively in troll-farm datasets; [[Zhao2025-ny]], [[Efstratiou2026-ij]], and [[Goel2025-iq]] extend coordination analysis into hashtag hijacking, science communication ecosystems, and narrative repurposing of mainstream news, respectively; and [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] addresses the longevity and topic-opportunism of manipulation campaigns. Read together, this final cluster pulls the topic back from method toward its ultimate stakes — whether, and under what conditions, detected coordination actually constitutes the democratic threat it is so often assumed to be.
