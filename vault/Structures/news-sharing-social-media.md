# News Sharing & Engagement on Social Media

## The arc of inquiry

This collection traces a three-decade-compressed debate about what actually happens when news moves through Facebook, Instagram, and (as a comparative foil) Twitter/X: does algorithmic and social curation insulate people from cross-cutting views, how should "exposure" and "sharing" even be measured, and what role do platforms play as active gatekeepers rather than neutral pipes. The papers filed here move from foundational descriptive claims about segregation, through increasingly sharp methodological critiques of how segregation and misinformation have been measured, to a newer generation of work that treats the platform itself — its reaction buttons, its reshare mechanics, its "break the glass" interventions — as the object of study.

## The echo-chamber/filter-bubble baseline and its revision

The empirical starting point is [[Bakshy2015-rn]], which used Facebook's own ranking and click logs to argue that individual choice, not the News Feed algorithm, is the primary filter restricting cross-cutting exposure — a claim that pushed back against strong "filter bubble" rhetoric (Pariser, Sunstein) while still documenting real, asymmetric ideological sorting. [[Flaxman2016-lm]] extended this moderating view to web browsing more broadly, finding that social media and search both increase segregation *and* increase incidental exposure to the other side, while the bulk of consumption remains direct visits to habitual outlets — evidence, the authors argue, for both camps in the filter-bubble debate simultaneously. [[Messing2014-jc]] offered an experimental mechanism for why social platforms might reduce rather than reinforce selective exposure: social endorsement cues crowd out partisan-source cues as a selection heuristic, nudging users toward counter-attitudinal but socially vouched-for content.

This relatively sanguine picture is substantially revised by [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]], whose "funnel of engagement" analysis of ~208 million Facebook users during the 2020 election finds segregation indices many times higher than prior web-tracking estimates, rising at each stage from potential exposure to algorithmic feed to actual engagement, and markedly asymmetric — a homogeneously conservative corner of the ecosystem that hosts nearly all Meta-flagged misinformation, with no liberal equivalent. The tension between [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s network-centered explanation and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]]'s emphasis on URL-level (rather than domain-level) curation is picked up directly by [[Green2025-ap]], which argues that domain-level audience measures systematically mistake *heterogeneity* for moderation: individual stories "unbundled" from moderate-scoring outlets can carry sharply partisan audiences, producing the appearance of story-level curation bubbles that source-level analyses wash out entirely. Malhotra's work on tie-severing, [[Malhotra2026-vl]], supplies a dynamic complement to this literature, showing that the composition of friend networks underlying all these exposure estimates is not static: users disproportionately prune cross-cutting ties around political flashpoints (Election Day, January 6th), meaning the networks measured by [[Bakshy2015-rn]]-style designs are themselves partly an output of prior segregative behavior.

## Methodological reckonings: what are we actually measuring?

A strong secondary thread interrogates the instruments used to produce these headline claims. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that web-scraping methodology itself — not just the much-discussed time delay — introduces ~34% content distortion relative to in-situ browser capture, with errors concentrated non-randomly in paywalled and interaction-gated content, directly implicating studies (including some filter-bubble research) that rely on ex-situ scraped corpora. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] critiques the field's reliance on unidimensional, outlet-level political-leaning scores and narrow national contexts, proposing a multi-level "cartography" of story, outlet, and content; this programmatic critique dovetails with [[Eady2025-vm]]'s technical solution — a joint measurement model that places media, politicians, and ordinary users on a common ideology scale purely from URL-sharing behavior, finding that ordinary politically engaged users, not politicians, drive the bulk of polarized sharing. [[Goel2025-iq]] extends the methodological critique to misinformation research specifically: domain-blocklist approaches miss the larger phenomenon of mainstream, factually accurate articles being co-shared with fake news to support misleading narratives, a mechanism illustrated through vaccine and voter-fraud case studies. [[Yang2025-iv]] contributes a parallel technical advance for detecting *coordinated* link-sharing on Facebook that does not rely on easily-gamed timing signals, addressing a known evasion vulnerability in platform-integrity research.

Several papers push this methodological self-scrutiny toward the measurement of exposure to untrustworthy sources specifically. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s US-focused approach to Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising shares of untrustworthy URLs in election years but stable proportions of *views*, and flags that NewsGuard's US-skewed coverage and the 100-share threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset make cross-national comparison fraught. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] uses platform-wide 2020 US data to show untrustworthy-source exposure is low on average but concentrated among a small subset of users, and — notably — that experimentally reducing such exposure produced no detectable attitudinal effects, a null result that resonates with [[Guess2023-ai]]'s large field experiment showing that stripping reshares from Facebook feeds substantially changes the *content mix* users see (less political news, less untrustworthy content) without moving polarization or most political attitudes. Together these two experiments suggest a widening gap between content-level findings of partisan/low-quality skew and the behavioral/attitudinal consequences such skew is often assumed to carry.

## The platform as active curator, not neutral conduit

A more recent cluster treats algorithmic governance itself as the explanandum. [[McNally2025-dn]] uses a decade of Guardian-article engagement data to show that Facebook's News Feed algorithm is not an undifferentiable "black box": hard news is measurably, differentially affected by documented ranking changes (with ~19-24 day lags), evidence marshaled explicitly to support algorithmic auditing under the EU's Digital Services Act. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] pushes further, showing that the relationship between shares and views on Facebook — ostensibly a stable mechanical amplification — fluctuates sharply in ways that track known governance interventions, especially the 2020 "break the glass" measures, with partisan content systematically penalized in reach and professionally-produced content systematically rewarded; this is read as direct evidence that Facebook actively curates rather than passively transmits. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] offers a structurally similar argument for Twitter/X in the immediate post-acquisition period: apparent right-leaning algorithmic advantage evaporates once one controls for agitating content and proximity to Elon Musk, whose account becomes disproportionately central to the algorithmic feed — a study of perverse engagement incentives rather than simple partisan bias. [[Wan2026-ai]] connects this literature to individual psychology, investigating how political efficacy conditions what users click within a fully algorithmic feed (Google Discover), locating agency within, not just against, algorithmic curation. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] extends the gatekeeper lens to Google News Italia's shaping of the health agenda, finding engagement is driven more by topic (celebrity, malpractice) than by source.

## Misinformation and false-news diffusion

A long-running thread independently tracks how falsehood spreads and who spreads it. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] established the foundational large-N claim that false news travels farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than true news on Twitter, attributing this to human novelty-seeking rather than bots. [[Guess2019-ym]] and [[Allcott2019-gn]] refine this for Facebook specifically: fake-news sharing in 2016 was rare overall but concentrated among conservatives and, most robustly, users over 65; and the relative Facebook-to-Twitter ratio of fake-news engagement fell roughly 60% after 2016, suggesting platform-specific interventions mattered. [[Guess2021-ym]] triangulates these findings with the large-scale "Condor" URL dataset, confirming that low-credibility content is a small but non-trivial share of the ecosystem, skewed toward ideologically congenial consumption and older/conservative sharers. [[Holt2026-zq]] complicates the picture from the moderation side: as Meta shifts from fact-checking to Community Notes and user reports, analysis of what users actually flag as "False News" shows they disproportionately report polarizing-but-true content they disagree with, not falsehoods per se — a cautionary note about the "wisdom of crowds" premise underlying this policy shift, and a fitting bookend to [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]'s earlier theoretical argument (via second-order cybernetics and the hybrid media system) that fixating on creator intent misdescribes how false information actually cycles through propagators.

## Emotion, reactions, and the mechanics of mobilization

A cluster centers on Facebook's affective affordances as drivers of circulation. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review of Reactions research establishes the broad pattern — political and controversial content elicits negative, especially angry, reactions, which in turn predict higher engagement — that [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] tests directly across Norwegian, Swedish, and Danish elections, finding populist parties are disproportionately good at eliciting "Angry" reactions and that anger (not "Love") predicts sharing. [[Larsson2026-nl]] extends this temporally across a decade of Norwegian party communication, showing rising negativity (especially among challengers and small left parties, not only the populist right) alongside a *weakening* sharing advantage for negative content over time — suggesting either strategic adaptation or audience fatigue with outrage-driven circulation. [[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]] is an earlier methodological precursor to this cluster, testing (and largely debunking) the idea that raw Facebook Like counts predict electoral outcomes, while still finding traces of a popularity–viability relationship.

## Insularity, populism, and partisan audience structures beyond the US

A comparative, Europe-heavy set of papers examines how partisan and populist communities structure news circulation outside the US two-party frame. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] shows that during Italy's 2018 election, Five Star Movement and League supporters inhabited more insular Twitter news ecosystems, and that insularity correlates with amplification (shares) over contestation (comments) on Facebook — with M5S uniquely able to convert positive coverage into shares and negative coverage into comment-based pushback. [[Darius2026-xl]] generalizes the populist-advantage finding across the entire 2024 European Parliament election: radical-right (and some far-left populist) parties enjoy outsized audience *support* relative to posting activity, most dramatically on TikTok, tied to Euroscepticism, emotional appeal, and anti-elitist rhetoric rather than simple output volume. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] interrogate the alternative-media side of this picture: audience overlap between alternative and mainstream outlets is generally low but context-dependent, rising where populist parties have institutional access, while named-entity analysis of Nord Stream 2 coverage finds alternative and legacy media reference largely the same actors — differing instead in relational network structure and in how often alternative outlets cite other media. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] traces a related dynamic over a longer horizon and across platforms, showing how "woke" discourse was synergistically politicized on Twitter and YouTube through coordinated, asymmetric amplification by conservative opinion leaders. [[Lukito2026-il]] and [[Much2026-gu]] extend the cross-platform lens to right-leaning media's Twitter/Truth Social curation strategies and to podcasts as "gendered media spaces" sorting young men and women into different incidental political diets — both suggestive that studying any single platform (or Facebook/Twitter alone) understates the fragmentation of the contemporary news ecosystem, a concern [[Mosleh2024-op]] raises directly in arguing that engagement with partisan and low-quality news diverges meaningfully across seven platforms.

## Theoretical framing and adjacent concerns

[[Chadwick2013-ns]]'s hybrid media system framework underwrites much of this literature's vocabulary — interdependence between old and new media actors, the "political information cycle" displacing the elite-controlled news cycle — and is explicitly invoked by both [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] and [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]. Two papers sit more at the edges of the topic but inform its boundaries: [[Wedel2026-ix]] questions whether "news" is even the right category for understanding young adults' informational engagement with platforms like TikTok and Instagram, proposing "informativeness" as a broader audience-centered construct; and [[Wang2026-ub]], on the failed academic migration from Twitter to Mastodon, is a reminder that the platform ecology underlying all these news-sharing studies is itself unstable — audiences, and the data infrastructures researchers depend on, can fragment or vanish as quickly as the governance regimes studied in [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and Efstratiou2025-gs] change beneath them.
