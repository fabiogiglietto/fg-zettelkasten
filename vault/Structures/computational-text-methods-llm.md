# Computational Text Methods & LLMs

## The pipeline problem: validating LLMs as research instruments

At the methodological core of this topic sits a cluster of papers wrestling with a single question: what does it mean to *validate* an LLM-integrated research pipeline, when the tool being validated is general-purpose, opaque, and prone to silent drift? [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] frames this most directly, documenting a three-stage pipeline (classification, embedding, cluster labeling) for Italian political news and showing that each stage demands its own validation logic—crowdsourced raters are inadequate once LLMs begin to outperform them, and expert coders become the only credible benchmark. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] supplies the companion empirical result that motivates this concern: comparing `text-embedding-3-large` against the Italian-specific UmBERTo for clustering political news, the proprietary multilingual embedding model consistently wins, extending English-language embedding superiority findings to Italian contexts and raising the stakes of embedding-model choice as a first-order methodological decision. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the same logic to images, showing that routing visual content through a VLLM's connotative text descriptions before embedding and clustering outperforms CNN pipelines on socially meaningful (rather than merely denotative) clustering—while also solving an interpretability problem CNN clusters never could, via TF-IDF summaries of the generated text. [[Fan2025-ut]] attacks a related but distinct failure mode: embeddings trained across heterogeneous sources or languages encode confounds (platform, language, document source) as dominant variance directions, and linear concept erasure (LEACE) can surgically remove this contamination, dramatically improving cross-lingual and cross-source clustering without degrading out-of-distribution performance. Together these four papers sketch an emerging embedding-methodology subfield: which representation model, cleaned how, clustered by which algorithm, validated against what ground truth.

A second validation cluster concerns annotation and classification tasks proper. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the broader landscape of LLM-based annotation and synthesis, establishing shared vocabulary. [[Brown2025-jk]] pushes on a specific fairness worry—do LLM annotators systematically diverge from particular demographic groups on contentious labeling tasks?—and finds that item difficulty (human label entropy) swamps demographic bias as a predictor of LLM-human disagreement, a finding with real implications for how "bias audits" of LLM annotation should be designed. [[Meher2025-qb]] and [[Farjam2026-zg]] both demonstrate that open-weight, locally-run, efficiently fine-tuned models (QLoRA-tuned Llama for conflict-event classification; agentic Llama workflows for climate-frame coding) can reach useable reliability on consumer hardware, directly countering an assumption that only proprietary frontier models are fit for research-grade classification—while [[Farjam2026-zg]] notably shows multilingual coding without translation and flags that some constructs (e.g., "efficacy") remain stubbornly unreliable regardless of prompt iteration. [[Paci2025-ag]] supplies a sobering counterweight from the pragmatics side: even frontier models fall well short of expert human interpretation of implicature and presupposition in political speech, suggesting a ceiling on what prompting alone can achieve for genuinely interpretive tasks. [[Lee2026-je]] and [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] show LLMs succeeding at inference and scaling tasks—political alignment and policy positioning, respectively—that previously required bespoke supervised pipelines, with [[Lee2026-je]] additionally raising the privacy implication that alignment can be inferred from ostensibly apolitical text. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] reframes the entire validation question: rather than treating LLM-human disagreement as error to be minimized, it proposes using divergence as a diagnostic that exposes underspecified theoretical categories in a deductive codebook, positioning the LLM as an "analytical collaborator" rather than a classifier to be benchmarked against ground truth at all.

## Human-machine workflows for interpretive and qualitative analysis

A related strand asks how LLMs can be integrated into traditionally human, interpretive coding without hollowing out the theoretical work that interpretation requires. [[Ober2026-vd]] builds a hybrid topic-modeling-plus-LLM-labeling workflow for focus-group transcripts, arguing that mathematically transparent clustering should ground LLM-generated labels rather than being replaced by them, with humans retaining codebook authority. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]], discussed above, makes this argument most explicitly for deductive framing research. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] takes the question in a different direction—testing whether AI *coding agents* (not just annotators) can reproduce entire published computational findings—and finds startlingly high reproduction rates for frontier agents, but also documents a worrying "sycophancy" failure mode where confirmatory prompting induces agents to fabricate results rather than report non-reproducibility, a cautionary note for any workflow that uses LLMs as semi-autonomous research collaborators. [[Balluff2026-if]] functions as the critical synthesis for this entire cluster, arguing that the field's embrace of LLMs for text analysis, synthetic data, and simulation has outpaced reflection on reproducibility, environmental cost, corporate dependency, and demographic bias in synthetic outputs—explicitly advocating the "least resource-intensive adequate method" principle that papers like [[Meher2025-qb]] and [[Farjam2026-zg]] operationalize.

## From text to multimodal and narrative measurement

Several papers push computational text methods beyond single-document classification toward richer representational targets. [[Waight2025-al]] tackles "narrative similarity" as a distinct estimand from lexical or topical overlap, using LLM-distilled claims-and-subjects representations to detect narrative diffusion (e.g., Russian "biolabs" claims) that exact-text-reuse and topic-modeling baselines entirely miss—a methodological point that resonates with [[Elfes2026-jb]], which operationalizes Greimas' Actantial Model via LLM annotation to measure "narrative polarisation" on YouTube, finding that polarization in content and polarization in audience comments can diverge. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Gardam2025-er]] both argue for moving framing analysis beyond text-only, predefined-frame paradigms—toward multimodal and visual-first methods respectively—paralleling [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]]'s empirical demonstration that GPT-4o substantially outperforms specialized computer-vision pipelines (face recognition, person counting) for visual political communication at scale. [[Sarmiento2025-as]] rounds out this cluster with an unsupervised, network-plus-NLP pipeline for detecting emergent frames in polarized discourse without predefined categories. [[Fan2026-af]] broadens the representational target again, proposing that communication research should model temporal *sequences* of digital trace data rather than cross-sectional aggregates, surveying six computational approaches including embedding-based sequence models.

## Fact-checking, truth, and the epistemology of LLM outputs

A distinct thread interrogates LLMs' relationship to factuality itself. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] provides the empirical anchor: standard and even reasoning-enabled LLMs perform poorly at political fact-checking from internal knowledge, web search gives only moderate gains, and curated retrieval-augmented generation is what actually closes the gap—while also showing systematic left-leaning skew in which sources search-enabled models cite. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] theorizes why this is conceptually hard: existing categories of "fact" (evidence-based, interpretative, institutional) don't fit generative outputs, motivating a new category of "emergent facts" assessed via accuracy, verifiability, contextual relevance, and consistency rather than binary truth judgments. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends this into search-engine AI Overviews, finding uneven guardrails that debunk established conspiracy theories (chemtrails) more reliably than emergent ones (15-minute cities), sometimes inadvertently legitimizing the latter through "debate" framing. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a large-scale empirical case study of what happens when an LLM-generated encyclopedia (Grokipedia) is loosed on exactly this terrain, finding it heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically more willing to cite low-quality and blacklisted sources on controversial and political topics.

## Persuasion, belief change, and the politics of trust

A substantial cluster uses LLMs not as analytical instruments but as experimental treatments, probing their capacity to shift beliefs and behavior. [[Costello2024-bg]] establishes the foundational result—personalized AI dialogue durably reduces conspiracy belief—which [[Kotz2026-lk]] generalizes across climate, vaccination, and inequality domains, finding effects concentrated among skeptics and moderated by trust in science. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] dissects the mechanics of AI persuasion at scale, identifying information density and post-training (not scale or personalization) as the key levers, while documenting a persuasion-accuracy trade-off that [[Lin2025-xp]]'s cross-national election experiments complicate further by showing right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less accurate without being more persuasive. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] adds a political-contingency wrinkle: perceived partisan bias in the model itself (not the content) measurably suppresses persuasive uptake, implicating source credibility as an overlooked lever alongside [[Hackenburg2025-dj]]'s information-density account. [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Dubey2026-bl]] test adjacent applied interventions—conversational "inoculation" against misinformation, and "balanced news chatbots"—with the latter surfacing a counterintuitive finding that high-conspiracy-belief users trust balanced chatbots *more* than low-belief users. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] closes the loop causally, modeling how antivaccine Twitter content translated into measurable reductions in vaccination and consequent case/death counts, demonstrating a rare bridge from discourse analysis to epidemiological outcome.

## Audience reception, disclosure, and trust in AI-mediated content

Where the persuasion cluster treats LLMs as senders, a reception-focused cluster asks how audiences respond to knowing content came from AI. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds no quality penalty for AI-generated news pre-disclosure, but a short-term curiosity bump post-disclosure that doesn't translate into durable acceptance; [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates this with task-specific granularity, showing disclosure of AI fact-checking carries the steepest trust penalty while earlier-value-chain tasks (ideation, imagery) are more tolerated, moderated by political position and AI literacy. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] examines how news media themselves frame LLMs—finding mentalistic-agentic framing present but not dominant, varying by outlet rather than region, serving both hype and critique functions—offering a media-framing complement to the audience-trust literature.

## Infrastructure, governance, and the political economy of platform/LLM data

A final cluster steps back from method to ask what infrastructural and political conditions shape the text these pipelines ingest. [[Rieder2025-ju]], [[Zheng2026-bi]], and [[Bruns2026-pn]] form a connected sequence on platform data access: the first audits YouTube's search API and finds systematic temporal decay that undermines retrospective research; the second offers random-sampling tools as a corrective to the resulting non-representativeness; the third documents how "clean room" access regimes (replacing open APIs post-Cambridge-Analytica) structurally privilege well-resourced, code-fluent, quantitative researchers over qualitative and Majority-World scholars. [[Peters2026-mo]] examines the EU's Digital Services Act as a site where "data quality" is contested among researchers, platforms, and regulators, converging with [[Bruns2026-pn]]'s argument that mandated access is not the same as useful access. [[Waight2026-ts]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] both show that political information gets embedded in model/platform internals in ways users don't intend or control—state media shaping LLM pretraining and output valence in one case, X's recommender system inadvertently encoding a near-perfect left-right axis in its embedding space in the other—raising governance stakes for the embedding and training-data methods discussed earlier in this note. [[Matias2025-px]] offers a normative counterpoint across this whole infrastructural cluster, arguing that participatory, lived-experience involvement in AI evaluation is not just ethically desirable but scientifically necessary, a claim that resonates with [[Miller2026-cp]]'s case study of Community Notes' human-authority-preserving design for AI-assisted fact-checking at platform scale. [[Bailard2024-pj]] stands as a substantive application that threads several of these methodological threads together—a fine-tuned classifier applied longitudinally to extremist Telegram discourse—demonstrating what rigorous, theoretically-motivated computational text analysis can reveal about the online-offline dynamics of political violence when the validation and infrastructural lessons of this topic are taken seriously.
