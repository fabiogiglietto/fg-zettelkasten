# Platform Governance, APIs & DSA-Era Research Access

## From API Access to Regulated Access: A Field Reconfiguring Itself

The papers gathered here converge on a single, uneasy premise: the empirical study of platforms is being reshaped as fast as the platforms themselves. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodises this history from the "laissez-faire" API era through the 2018 Cambridge Analytica rupture to a fragmented present of four official access regimes plus unofficial workarounds; Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] elegise the Twitter APIs specifically, with the latter documenting a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024 following the $42,000/month paywall. [[Yang2026-tq]] confirms this at corpus scale across five disciplines: after tripling since 2010, empirical social media research plateaued and declined in 2022–2024 as API access closed. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] frames the arc from "Wild West to Walled Garden," and [[Bruns2026-yv]] extends the eulogy from research infrastructure to the public sphere itself.

## The DSA as Hinge — Promise, Friction, and Bureaucratic Capture

Article 40 of the DSA sits at the centre of this collection as both hope and stress-test. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] treat the regulation as a genuine paradigm shift from "independence by permission" to a binding legal right, while cautioning that early implementation reveals opaque procedures, resource asymmetries, and platforms' strategic delay. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows how even the technical construct of "data quality" was politically contested during the Delegated Regulation's drafting, with academics and NGOs pushing quality language into the final text against platform resistance. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] extend these worries into election research, cataloguing uneven eligibility, WEIRD-centric access, and the persistence of a "price, proficiency, or permission" barrier. [[Crosset2026-mq]] reframes the whole regulatory landscape through a security-studies lens, arguing that DSA-style "flow optimisation" governs platforms not by controlling content but by bureaucratising transparency, auditing, and reporting — a diagnosis that helps explain why compliance can coexist with substantive opacity.

Empirical audits of the new access tools sharpen the point. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained inconsistencies; [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's Data API is "forgetful by design," with search results decaying after 20–60 days in ways that make DSA-compliant systemic risk research infeasible; [[Jurg2025-ur]] documents inconsistent publisher-context labelling and opaque removal statements during the 2024 EU elections. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] mobilises DSA-mandated transparency data to reveal severe cross-lingual disparities in moderator allocation — findings only made visible *by* the regulation, yet only partially interpretable *because of* it.

## Industry Entanglement and the Politics of Collaboration

Running underneath the access debate is a sharper worry about who gets to know what. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] estimates that ~80% of high-profile social media research is "industrially saturated" through author, editor, or reviewer ties, with most undisclosed; [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] extends the analogy to tobacco and pharma, and [[Heiss2026-qv]] argues that platforms' exclusive control over data creates a structurally novel conflict-of-interest problem that open science practices alone cannot fix. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers the sharpest methodological verdict on the flagship Meta 2020 collaboration: even the "apex" of empirical platform social science lacks temporal and poetic validity, and one-off field experiments cannot keep pace with platforms that mutate underneath them. [[Allen2025-ot]] and [[Gauthier2026-iq]] show what the alternative looks like — platform-independent browser-extension experiments and independent field studies that produce non-null findings on X's algorithmic effects, in tension with the Meta studies' famously null results. [[Brady2026-ln]] pushes further, running a full-scale algorithm redesign experiment on Bluesky rather than negotiating with an incumbent platform.

## Meta, X, and the Content-Moderation Turn

A large cluster of papers reads platform policies as objects of critical analysis in their own right. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] provides the sharpest empirical case: Meta's political-content-reduction policy cut re-elected Italian MPs' Facebook reach by 72%, began ten months before Meta's announced global rollout, and was never disclosed in DSA compliance reports — while extremists offset per-post declines through posting volume. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[McNally2025-dn]] similarly detect algorithmic governance interventions through their downstream signatures, contradicting "black box" fatalism. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] analyse Meta's and other platforms' discursive management of these very changes — how "coordinated inauthentic behaviour," "changecraft," and creator-benevolence framing displace responsibility outward. [[Donovan2025-ws]] traces the whole 2016–2021 arc of behavioural-rather-than-veracity moderation, followed by the post-2021 retreat that [[Moran2025-qn]] captures ethnographically as the "end of trust and safety."

The X case runs in parallel. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] shows the algorithm rewards agitating content and Musk proximity more than ideology per se; [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] demonstrates that X's recommender inadvertently encodes users' Left–Right position as a linear direction in its embedding space; [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents durable partisan sorting after Brazil's ban; and [[Renault2025-uh]], [[Bouchaud2026-np]] show Community Notes systematically flags Republicans more and undermoderates polarising content by design — a serious challenge to the Musk/Zuckerberg replacement narrative that [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] examine from the fact-checkers' side.

## Beyond "Social Media": New Objects, New Methods

Several papers argue that even a fully implemented DSA would be studying a moving target. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorises TikTok's shift from "networked" to "clustered" publics; [[Tornberg2026-lc]] and [[Boyd2026-op]] argue the "social media" object is dissolving into algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private spheres, AI-mediated communication, and parasociality — with corresponding obsolescence of network-analytic and API-based methods. [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows Andrew Tate's post-deplatforming "Tate-space" on YouTube as an ambient ideological ecology that moderation cannot outpace, while [[Simeone2025-vo]] provides a rare counter-case where targeted deplatforming did produce durable network collapse. [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles algorithmic and user-choice contributions to echo chambers and rabbit holes, finding platform-wide conservative drift but no radicalisation pathway.

## Toward Infrastructure Thinking

A final thread reframes the whole problem as one of *research infrastructure* rather than data access. [[Schulte2026-df]] argues multi-platform election observability must be built as a reusable public good; [[Bechmann2026-dr]] proposes moving beyond causal-effects paradigms to treat platform collective behaviour as democratic infrastructure; [[Mahl2026-hc]] gathers expert consensus that platform governance and journalism ecosystems, not individual-level literacy, are the highest-leverage intervention sites. Empirical benchmarks like [[Vincent_undated-re]] and the URL Shares Dataset critiques in [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] show why: without stable, quality-controlled, independently maintained infrastructure, even privacy-preserving platform datasets encode algorithmic artefacts that overwhelm the social signals researchers wish to study. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] illustrate the parallel case for ad transparency, [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] close the loop by tying the whole enterprise to democratic accountability.

Read together, the collection traces an argument in three movements: the open-API era produced a distinctive but structurally dependent scholarship; its collapse and the DSA's arrival have reconfigured access from platform philanthropy into regulated right; but the platforms, the objects of study, and the industry–academic entanglements have all shifted enough that the field must now build durable, independent infrastructure — methodological, institutional, and legal — rather than negotiate case-by-case with adversarial partners.
