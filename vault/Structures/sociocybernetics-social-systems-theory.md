# Sociocybernetics & Social Systems Theory

## Sociocybernetics & Social Systems Theory

The papers filed here are not, strictly speaking, a corpus of "systems theory" scholarship in the classical Luhmannian sense — none of them cites Luhmann's *Soziale Systeme* at length, and only one engages his apparatus directly. What unites them is a recurring epistemological move: a refusal to locate the phenomenon under study (misinformation, agency, polarization, fake news, internet architecture) in a single level, a single actor-type, or a single observer's judgment, and an insistence instead on treating the phenomenon as *emergent from observation-dependent, recursively coupled, multilevel dynamics*. This is the sociocybernetic gesture — second-order observation, structural coupling, emergence from operative closure — even where the vocabulary is different. Read together, these six pieces trace an arc from foundational cybernetic epistemology, through its application to internet-era communication problems, to its newest and most urgent test case: whether it can be extended to make sense of artificial agents as social participants.

### Second-order observation as method

[[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] is the most explicitly Luhmannian text in the set and functions as something like the theoretical anchor for the whole topic. Its central move — shifting from the *creator's* intent to the *propagator's* judgment, and insisting that "information is observer-dependent" — is a direct application of second-order cybernetics (von Foerster, Bateson) and Luhmann's own observer-theoretic stance to the empirical problem of fake news. Truth, here, is not a property of content but an outcome of a chain of situated judgments; societal consensus is structurally unlikely, and coexisting incompatible truths are the *normal* operating condition of the hybrid media system rather than a pathology to be corrected. This paper supplies the epistemological grammar — micro/meso/macro levels, emergence, non-reducibility to a single typology — that recurs, sometimes without citation, across the rest of the set.

### Multilevel complexity as diagnostic and critique

[[Frischlich2025-vn]] and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] extend this same diagnostic instinct to adjacent problem spaces. Frischlich et al. argue that the dominant analogies for misinformation (virus, weapon, pollutant) each illuminate one level of a four-level system (micro/meso-social/meso-platform/macro) while obscuring the others, and that policy failures follow directly from mistaking a partial analogy for the whole system — a point that rhymes closely with Giglietto's rejection of single-typology accounts of fake news circulation. Both papers treat their object as a *complex adaptive system* whose behavior cannot be inferred from any one component in isolation, and both call for portfolio- or framework-level responses rather than single-lever interventions. Falkenberg et al.'s brief but pointed critique of polarization research — fragmented across disciplines, geographically parochial — can be read as the same complaint pitched at the level of the *research field itself*: scholarship on a multilevel, globally variable phenomenon has itself failed to achieve the systemic, non-parochial vantage point its object requires. Together these three papers model a consistent sociocybernetic critique: single-frame, single-region, or single-actor accounts of digital-social phenomena systematically misdescribe systems that are constitutively multilevel and recursively coupled.

### From social capital to socially-driven infrastructure

[[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] applies a cognate logic one layer down, into internet architecture itself. Its argument that centrality, trust, and influence — concepts imported from social capital theory (Bourdieu, Coleman, Putnam) — must be reconciled with networking notions of centrality (degree, betweenness, closeness) is an early, infrastructure-facing instance of the same insistence that technical and social layers are structurally coupled and cannot be optimized independently. The prosumer/network-prosumer trajectory it describes anticipates the platform-level ("meso-II") tier that Frischlich et al. later formalize, and it reads retrospectively as a proof of concept for taking social-theoretic categories seriously as *design constraints* on technical systems rather than post hoc commentary on them.

### The frontier: can machines be systems' participants?

The two most recent papers push the same sociocybernetic questions onto artificial agents, and mark the topic's live edge. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes machine agency as a "demarcation problem" resolved not by internal capability but by socially granted and monitored status — an explicitly constructivist, observer-dependent account of agency that echoes Giglietto's observer-dependent account of information. Agency, like truth, is not a property sitting in the object but a status conferred and sustained by social practice. [[Ng2026-og]] operationalizes this insight at scale: it insists that agentic AI cannot be modeled as isolated task-completing units but must be treated as a Multi-Agent Social System exhibiting strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, and distributional instability — properties that are unmistakably systems-theoretic (structural coupling, operative interdependence, emergent population-level dynamics) even though the paper's lineage runs through multi-agent RL and computational sociology rather than Luhmann. Read against Anicker, Ng's empirical demonstration that agent "status" (karma) is jointly and recursively produced by network position and neighbor influence gives formal, data-driven flesh to the claim that agency is relational and system-conferred rather than intrinsic.

### The arc

Taken as a whole, the six papers trace a coherent trajectory: a second-order-cybernetic epistemology of observer-dependent, multilevel emergence ([[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]) is generalized into a diagnostic critique of single-analogy, single-discipline accounts of digital-social pathologies ([[Frischlich2025-vn]], [[Falkenberg2026-ka]]); is shown to have infrastructural stakes when social-capital categories are folded into network design ([[Sofia2012-802a21dc]]); and is finally extended to the hardest current case — whether artificial agents can be admitted as participants in social systems at all, requiring social theory itself to function as a structural prior for their design and governance ([[Anicker2024-vp]], [[Ng2026-og]]). This progression — from epistemology, to critique, to infrastructure, to artificial participants — is the throughline that makes sociocybernetics and social systems theory the load-bearing foundation for observing complex digital social systems rather than a merely decorative theoretical reference.
