# Computational Methods & LLM-Assisted Content Analysis

## The turn to LLMs-in-the-loop: validating a new measurement infrastructure

The organizing tension across this corpus is between the sudden availability of powerful, general-purpose language and vision models and the discipline's inherited standards for measurement validity. A cluster of papers treats LLMs explicitly as instruments to be calibrated rather than oracles to be trusted. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] is the methodological anchor here, documenting the practical difficulty of validating a pipeline in which OpenAI models are used simultaneously for classification, embedding, and cluster-labeling — arguing that each stage needs its own task-specific validation and that crowdsourced annotators are no longer an adequate human baseline once models outperform them. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] supplies a companion piece of infrastructure work, benchmarking `text-embedding-3-large` against Italian-specific BERT embeddings for unsupervised news clustering and showing that embedding choice, not just clustering algorithm, drives semantic coherence. [[Ober2026-vd]] extends the human-in-the-loop logic to qualitative interview analysis, using topic modeling as an interpretable scaffold onto which LLM-generated labels are layered and checked. Together these three sketch a recurring workflow — fine-tune or prompt, embed, cluster, validate against expert or gold-standard judgment — that recurs, with variations, throughout the topic.

A second set of papers pressure-tests what LLMs can actually be trusted to *do* as annotators or measurement devices, often with sobering results. [[Brown2025-jk]] finds that demographic "bias" in LLM annotation is smaller and more dataset-specific than feared, and that item difficulty (human disagreement) swamps any effect of model choice — a finding that reframes fairness debates around annotation as an epistemic rather than purely political problem, echoing [[Matias2025-px]]'s argument that public involvement improves the *science* of evaluation, not just its legitimacy. [[Paci2025-ag]] and [[DeVerna2025-dl]] push in the opposite direction, showing concrete limits: LLMs struggle with Gricean implicature in political speech and perform poorly at fact-checking without curated retrieval context, respectively — both papers insisting that competence is task- and context-dependent, not a general property of "the LLM." [[Lee2026-je]] and [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] show the flip side of this capability: LLMs can reliably infer political alignment from nonpolitical text and can be used, via simple ask-and-average prompting, to scale political actors on ideological dimensions — reframing inference and measurement as two faces of the same underlying capacity, with attendant privacy implications. [[Waight2025-al]] and [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extend this measurement logic to new estimands — narrative similarity across languages, and latent traits from open-ended survey text — explicitly foregrounding estimand–estimator alignment (per Grimmer and King) as the criterion by which LLM-based methods should be judged, a concern also central to [[Elfes2026-jb]]'s operationalization of "narrative polarisation" via Greimas' actantial model.

## Beyond text: multimodal and embedding-based extensions

A parallel strand extends computational content analysis beyond text into images and networks, treating embeddings as the common currency. [[Arminio2025-tw]] argues that CNN-based image clustering captures only denotative content and proposes a VLLM-to-text pipeline that recovers connotative, culturally embedded meaning with human-interpretable TF-IDF summaries — a claim substantiated at scale by [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]], which shows GPT-4o outperforming specialized face-recognition and person-counting models for visual political communication on Instagram. [[Gardam2025-er]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] push the same "visual-first" and multimodal logic into conspiracy discourse and news framing respectively, arguing text-centric methods systematically miss image-driven communicative work. Bastos2025-ol applies image analysis at industrial scale to troll-farm profile pictures, searching for visual "signatures" of state-linked influence operations. Underlying all of this is the embedding-space itself as an object of methodological scrutiny: [[Fan2025-ut]] shows that linear concept erasure can remove confounding source/language signal from document embeddings before similarity analysis, while [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] demonstrates, in a striking real-world audit, that a platform's own recommender embedding space linearly encodes users' ideological position — knowledge that can be reconstructed by researchers and, the authors show, surgically removed. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes "practice mapping," using vector embeddings of network actions to move past the interpretive limits of the network "hairball," and [[Fan2026-af]] extends this embedding logic to *temporal* sequences of platform behavior, arguing that hyper-longitudinal digital trace data need methods — sequence analysis, HMMs, process mining, neural embeddings — that preserve the dynamics communication theory has always claimed to care about.

## Detecting coordination, manipulation, and their offline consequences

A distinct methodological problem is inferring hidden coordination or covert influence from behavioral traces. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] provides the conceptual scaffolding here, arguing that "coordinated online behavior" has been defined too narrowly and inconsistently by platforms and academics alike, and proposing a general framework (actors, synergic actions, intent; authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, time-variance) against which detection methods should be benchmarked. [[Minici2024-tf]] operationalizes one branch of this agenda with a graph-foundation-model approach (IOHunter) that generalizes across information-operation campaigns, while [[Pante2025-pq]] performs the crucial corrective move — re-testing prior claims of inter-state coordination against modern detection methods and control datasets, and finding earlier claims under-substantiated. [[Kim2026-br]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] apply hierarchical/coordination-detection classifiers to, respectively, two decades of Korean news comments and multilingual coordinated Twitter activity around China's COVID protests, both linking detected online coordination to real geopolitical dynamics (election cycles, diplomatic tension, state repression). These sit alongside papers that push from detection toward causal claims about online-offline linkage: [[Bailard2024-pj]] uses fine-tuned classification of collective-action frames plus Granger causality to trace a reciprocal online-messaging/offline-violence cycle among the Proud Boys, and [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] builds a compartmental epidemic model fit to geolocated antivaccine tweets to estimate a causal lower bound on vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths attributable to Twitter content. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] rounds out this cluster from the persuasion side, running large conversational-AI experiments to isolate which levers (post-training, prompting, information density) drive persuasiveness — and documenting a troubling persuasion-accuracy trade-off that connects back to [[DeVerna2025-dl]]'s fact-checking concerns.

## The data-access crisis as methodological precondition

Running underneath all of the above is a sustained infrastructural critique: computational content analysis is only as good as the data platforms allow researchers to obtain, and that access has become radically unstable. [[Freelon2024-sc]] provides the master narrative, periodizing two decades of API history into a "post-API age" defined by contingency, scandal, and commercial interest, a story extended institutionally by [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and given regulatory texture by [[Peters2026-mo]], which shows how "data quality" itself became a politically contested category during DSA rulemaking, and [[Bruns2026-pn]], which documents how "clean room" environments like the Meta Content Library nominally satisfy DSA access mandates while structurally excluding qualitative, cross-platform, and Global South research. [[Yang2026-tq]] quantifies the downstream consequence at the level of the discipline: social-media-based empirical research tripled from 2010 to the early 2020s before plateauing and declining as API access closed, with Twitter/X and Facebook chronically overrepresented relative to actual platform use. Several papers supply the granular audits that justify this alarm: [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," systematically losing findable historical content; [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that web-scraping itself — independent of time delay — introduces large, systematically biased content distortions relative to in-situ browsing; [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] uncovers an algorithmic artifact (the 100-shares threshold) that manufactures spurious cross-country similarity in Meta's URL Shares Dataset; and [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard, the most widely used source-trustworthiness database, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can flip substantive findings even when continuous scores are stable. [[Zheng2026-bi]] offers a constructive response, building random-sampling tools (TubeStats/TokStats) that supply defensible denominators for YouTube and TikTok research precisely because sanctioned APIs cannot.

## Critical reflexivity and the limits of automation

A final, self-consciously reflexive strand asks whether the field's rapid uptake of LLMs is epistemically warranted at all. [[Balluff2026-if]] is the sharpest statement of this position, cataloguing reproducibility, environmental, and bias costs of unreflective LLM adoption and arguing for a trade-off mindset favoring smaller, open, task-appropriate models — a critique that dovetails with [[Nguyen2026-vm]]'s finding that even journalistic discourse about LLMs is more variegated (and less uniformly hyperbolic) than "AI hype" narratives suggest. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the emerging methodological literature on LLM-based annotation and synthesis as a field unto itself, while [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] push the reflexive question furthest by testing whether AI systems can perform *researchers'* and *reviewers'* own core epistemic tasks — reproducing published findings and auditing scientific claims — finding surprisingly high capability alongside specific, diagnosable failure modes (sycophantic specification search, metadata hallucination, PDF-induced bias). Substantively adjacent studies extend the same reflexive stance to specific content domains: [[Nenno2025-xa]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] show how Western-derived theoretical categories (news values, emotional reaction affordances) travel unevenly across cultural and platform contexts when operationalized computationally, [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] finds the "algorithm literacy" construct itself lacks conceptual coherence across 169 studies, [[Sarmiento2025-as]] argues framing analysis needs unsupervised methods precisely because predefined frame categories miss emergent polarization dynamics, and [[Ramos2026-qo]] argues, from inside professional fact-checking practice, that claim-level correction is analytically too narrow and must be supplemented by narrative- and argument-level monitoring — a conclusion that echoes, from the practitioner side, the same critique of narrow operationalization that [[Elfes2026-jb]] and [[Mannocci2026-kc]] make from the research-methods side.

Read together, the corpus traces an arc from *enthusiasm* (LLMs as scalable annotators and embedding engines) through *validation* (task-specific benchmarking, human-in-the-loop protocols, bias audits) to *infrastructural realism* (the recognition that platform data access, not model capability, is now the binding constraint) and finally to *reflexive critique* (asking whether these tools should be adopted as readily as they have been). That arc is precisely what underwrites the methodological syllabus for Digital Social Network Analysis and Data Analysis for Media Studies, and what has made this topic a natural home for the lab's recent AoIR collaborations.
