# Political Polarization & Audience Engagement

## From filter bubbles to curation bubbles: revisiting the empirical basics

Any structure note on this topic must begin with the long shadow of [[Bakshy2015-rn]], whose Facebook study established the now-canonical decomposition of exposure into network composition, algorithmic ranking, and individual choice — attributing most of the observed ideological sorting to the latter. This decomposition still organizes debate: [[Green2025-ap]] revisits it methodologically, arguing that domain-level partisan-audience scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, so that "curation bubbles" are more precisely a networked re-bundling of individual stories than a property of sources. Gaisbauer2025-px-adjacent concerns recur in Gaisbauer2025-uh... more precisely, in Gaisbauer2025-uh itself the critique is that most news-sharing research flattens political leaning into a single dimension and privileges the outlet level — exactly the problem Green's story-level cartography and Eady's et al. joint-scaling model ([[Eady2025-vm]]) try to solve. Eady's contribution — a common-scale ideology estimator built purely from URL-sharing — shows that ordinary politically engaged citizens, not politicians, propagate the bulk of extreme content, and that electorally safe legislators share disproportionately polarized news, linking audience-level dynamics back to elite incentives. [[Lai2024-to]] extends this ideological-scaling logic to a new unit of analysis, YouTube videos, inferred from Reddit cross-posting, while [[Mosleh2024-op]] insists that none of these single-platform pictures generalize: engagement with partisan and low-quality news diverges sharply across seven platforms, a warning echoed by [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] and [[Hartmann2025-px]], whose systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies concludes that most cross-study disagreement is an artifact of incompatible operationalizations rather than a real empirical dispute. Together these papers push the field toward more granular, comparative, and methodologically self-aware measurement — a trajectory that [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] radicalizes by calling for polarization research that is not just methodologically pluralistic but geographically equitable, given its near-total concentration on the US.

## Affect, reactions, and the mechanics of engagement

A second cluster asks not whether audiences are exposed to cross-cutting content but how they *feel* about what they see, and how that affect drives circulation. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review of Facebook Reactions research establishes the baseline finding that anger is a uniquely powerful engagement driver for political and populist content, a pattern empirically confirmed across Scandinavian election contexts in [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] (Angry reactions predict sharing; Love reactions suppress it) and in a decade-long single-party case study, [[Larsson2026-ro]], which shows Norway's Progress Party's shift toward negative sentiment under a new leader tracking increased shares and comments while likes remain tied to positivity — suggesting "lightweight" and "heavy" engagement follow different affective logics. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] complements this at the ecosystem level: insular, populist-favored news sources on Italian Facebook receive more shares per comment, while cross-partisan sources become sites of contestation via comments — indicating that amplification and argumentation are structurally distinct modes of audience response, with the Five Star Movement uniquely able to convert both positive and negative coverage into favorable attention economics. [[Marino2026-he]] pushes this affective lens longitudinally, showing that a Brazilian pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network's emotional engagement was far from the stable echo chamber assumed in the literature, instead swinging dramatically around Lula's inauguration and the January 2023 coup attempt — declining love-reactions, rising internal debate — suggesting hyperpartisan community boundaries are event-contingent and porous rather than fixed. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] extends the affect-and-source logic to toxicity: influencer-sourced misinformation provokes qualitatively different, more homogeneous toxic reactions than identical content from ordinary users, mediated by credibility legitimation and parasocial "community enmeshment." Underlying all of this, [[Oswald2025-km]] offers a structural caution: visible engagement — comments, reactions, shares — is generated by a tiny, unrepresentative fraction of users, so affective dynamics observed in platform data may say more about a vocal minority than about "public opinion" writ large.

## Insularity, entanglement, and the platform-specific structure of division

A parallel line asks where and how partisan insularity is instantiated at the level of platform architecture and community structure. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] is the most structurally ambitious of these: across 11 political subreddits over 16 years, cross-ideological overlap in users, sources, and even discourse is minimal, leading the authors to reject the "sedimentation" of Reddit polarization as reducible to any single cause (design, moderation, discourse) — it is an entanglement of all three. This resonates with [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]'s broader theoretical claim that platforms like TikTok are transforming "networked publics" into algorithmically "clustered publics," a shift toward item-centric, opaque, behaviorally-inferred groupings that intensifies subcultural fragmentation independent of any user's explicit political choices. [[Zhu2026-tn]] takes the comparative-structural approach cross-nationally, showing that alternative-mainstream media audience overlap is shaped less by fixed platform properties than by national media systems and the institutional access of populist parties — producing, e.g., a uniquely isolated alternative-media enclave in Sweden. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] asks the more targeted question of whether alternative platforms themselves drive discursive polarization, situating itself within this same debate about platform-level versus content-level causes. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] shows an analogous segregation dynamic in a scientific-expert context: UK newspapers of different political orientation cite different scientists' pandemic views, and those scientists themselves segregate in co-authorship and Twitter-sharing networks — evidence that insularity can propagate from elite epistemic communities into partisan media ecosystems. [[Esau2025-tf]] provides the normative counterpoint, arguing conflict is not inherently pathological and proposing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as antidotes specifically to "destructive" polarization, a term whose empirical grounding is itself scrutinized by [[van-Eck2026-xg]], which finds that scholarly depolarization recommendations are usually untethered from the evidence base and instead reflect implicit deliberative-democratic ideals.

## Algorithms as causal levers: from null results to targeted interventions

A crucial recent shift is toward experimental, causally identified interrogations of *algorithms themselves* as drivers of polarization, moving past the correlational insularity literature above. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s X field experiment is pivotal: switching users onto the "For You" algorithm shifts political attitudes rightward and increases engagement, but switching it *off* does not reverse these effects, because the algorithm's persistent legacy is who users choose to follow — a mechanism that helps reconcile the earlier, much-discussed null results from Meta's 2020 studies. [[Brady2026-ln]] operationalizes this insight into intervention: a "diversified extremity" algorithm on Bluesky reduces amplification of intergroup/moralized/emotional content and improves accuracy of social-norm perceptions relative to an engagement-based feed, without denting platform enjoyment — reframing the causal pathway to animosity as running through *misperceived norms* rather than direct persuasion. [[Smith2025-kc]]'s "attention broker" concept offers a network-mechanistic account of how amplification (retweets with attribution) causally accelerates the formation of new following ties, a micro-foundation that helps explain why algorithmic exposure (as in Gauthier) can durably reshape network structure. At the more disruptive end, [[Ventura2026-yc]] shows that even blunt platform interventions — Brazil's ban of X — produce lasting partisan "sorting ratchets," with conservative users disproportionately evading the ban and the platform's news environment shifting durably rightward. Together, this cluster argues that engagement-optimizing design is not a neutral backdrop to polarization but an active, sometimes irreversible causal force — one that thoughtfully redesigned algorithms might, per Brady, partially counteract.

## Elites, misinformation labeling, and the politics of moderation

A final constellation examines how political elites and moderation systems shape — and are shaped by — polarized audiences. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" shows how intra-party ideological factions (exemplified by Republicans adopting the CRT frame) forge symbiotic ties to hyperpartisan media that reward extremity with engagement and attention, radicalizing party politics from within. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents a related but distinct elite strategy — Brazilian parliamentarians contesting journalistic epistemic authority during a health crisis via derivative reporting, direct authority claims, or local brokerage — while [[Tai2026-qk]] asks how institutional and ideological position moderate elected officials' own misinformation-sharing. The politics of *labeling* misinformation is itself polarized: [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows "fake news" functions as a weaponized delegitimation label in both left- and right-wing alternative media, and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" demonstrates that citizens across three European countries systematically perceive their own party as more targeted by misinformation than their rivals, moderated by partisanship strength and right-wing orientation. Contemporary crowdsourced moderation is scrutinized in two companion papers: [[Renault2025-uh]] finds Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under X's cross-partisan Community Notes system, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] argues this is a structural design flaw — bridging-based algorithms systematically undermoderate the most polarizing content precisely because it cannot attract cross-partisan agreement, with acute implications for elections. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] extend this elite/audience nexus to interference narratives and targeted suppression advertising respectively — showing that even the *discourse about* interference (not just interference itself) can produce asymmetric partisan effects, and that geo-racially targeted suppression ads measurably depressed turnout among their intended targets. [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis serves as a fitting methodological capstone to this whole topic, showing that the strength (though not direction) of the core partisan-media/polarization relationship is highly sensitive to how "slant" and "polarization" are operationalized — a sobering reminder, alongside [[Hartmann2025-px]] and [[van-Eck2026-xg]], that much apparent disagreement in this literature is a methodological artifact rather than a substantive one. Rounding out the picture, computational advances such as LLM-based political-alignment inference ([[Lee2026-je]]), narrative-level polarization measures ([[Elfes2026-jb]]), unsupervised framing detection ([[Sarmiento2025-as]], [[Arora2025-tx]]), and identity-work analyses of conspiracy communities ([[Grusauskaite2026-po]]) point toward a maturing methodological toolkit oriented at capturing polarization's narrative, affective, and algorithmic dimensions beyond simple exposure counts — a toolkit whose necessity is underscored by earlier hybrid-media-system studies of Twitter-TV interplay ([[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]), hashtag dissent ([[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]), and the propagation-centered account of "fake news" cycles ([[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]) that first established many of these questions.
