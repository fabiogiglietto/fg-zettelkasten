# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

### From opinion polling to lived exposure

The papers filed here span three complementary vantage points on how AI is being received: the self-reports of intensive users [[UnknownUnknown-db]], the discursive scaffolding built by stakeholders over the pre-GenAI decade [[Richter2026-bt]], and the national-attitudinal snapshots of a general public still forming its stance [[Fattorini2026-bo]]. Read together, they trace an arc from *who talks about AI publicly*, through *what publics have come to think*, to *what workers actually experience once they use it*. The through-line is that attitudes are neither uniform nor reducible to information deficit: they are shaped by occupational exposure, national discursive infrastructure, and direct hands-on use.

### Ambivalence, exposure, and the U-shape of concern

Both the Italian survey [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and Anthropic's user study [[UnknownUnknown-db]] converge on a picture of *ambivalent engagement* rather than technophobia or techno-utopianism. Italians increasingly see AI as threatening (rising from 36% to 51% between 2023 and 2024) even as tool adoption doubles, and overwhelmingly favor permitting-but-regulating generative systems. Anthropic's respondents likewise fold enthusiasm about productivity into worry: about a fifth voice displacement concerns, and — strikingly — those concerns follow a U-shape with respect to reported speedup, so that both the disempowered and the most-accelerated worry most. This complicates the intuition that hands-on experience simply dissolves anxiety. Fattorini et al. find that chatbot use *reduces* threat perceptions among Italians, while the Anthropic data show that heavy productive use can *heighten* awareness of exposure. Direct experience, in other words, seems to shift the *content* of concern from symbolic (humanoid robots, sci-fi replacement) to structural (job scope, wage effects).

### Structural determinants: occupation, demography, and country

Where Anthropic emphasizes occupational exposure and career stage — early-career workers are markedly more worried and benefit less personally from AI gains than seniors [[UnknownUnknown-db]] — the Italian data foreground age, gender, and education, with women and older respondents expressing systematically more caution [[Fattorini2026-bo]]. Both papers thus reject a monolithic "public" and insist on cleavages. Richter and colleagues [[Richter2026-bt]] add a further layer: national discursive ecologies themselves differ. The German Twitter discourse institutionalised around academia, advocacy, and government by 2021, while the US discourse became a tech-influencer arena dominated by individual industry-adjacent voices. Such divergent stakeholder configurations plausibly seed the very cultural and regulatory frames within which publics like the Italian one (embedded in the EU regulatory imagination) come to voice their "critical ambivalence."

### Who benefits, who narrates, who is heard

A common normative worry across the three papers is *distributional*: not only of AI's material benefits but of discursive standing. Anthropic finds that productivity gains accrue most often to users themselves, less to employers or AI companies — but early-career workers are the exception, capturing less personal upside [[UnknownUnknown-db]]. Fattorini et al. document sharply differentiated acceptance across domains, with the strongest resistance precisely where AI encroaches on labor autonomy: worker selection and management (25.6% support), journalism, and artistic production [[Fattorini2026-bo]]. Richter et al. sharpen this into a governance question: if US public AI discourse is essentially a conversation among industry actors and their influencer proxies, then the workers reporting displacement concerns and the publics expressing ambivalence about algorithmic management are structurally under-represented in the very arenas where AI's social meaning is set [[Richter2026-bt]].

### Methodological complementarities

Methodologically the three studies illustrate the range of empirical strategies now converging on this topic: nationally representative survey waves [[Fattorini2026-bo]], platform-scale user surveys triangulated with behavioural log data [[UnknownUnknown-db]], and longitudinal social-network analysis of stakeholder discourse [[Richter2026-bt]]. Each has characteristic blind spots — Anthropic's sample is self-selected among Claude users; the Italian data are single-country; the Twitter analysis stops in 2021 and is confined to one platform. But together they suggest a research agenda in which discourse analysis, attitudinal surveys, and behavioural traces are read as mutually calibrating, rather than as competing accounts of what "the public" thinks about AI.
