# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## National Attitudes and the Ambivalence of Everyday Use

Two large-scale surveys anchor the empirical core of this topic by asking what ordinary publics think about AI as it becomes infrastructural. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] tracks Italian attitudes across 2023–2024 and finds a pattern the authors call "critical ambivalence": rapid growth in everyday AI tool use (chatbots, voice assistants, smart home devices) accompanied by persistently low self-assessed understanding and rising perceptions of AI as a threat to humanity. Crucially, acceptance is domain-specific—high for security applications, low for journalism, education, and worker management—suggesting that Italians are not technophobic so much as discriminating about where they want AI involved. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] reports a structurally similar story for the United States: roughly half of adults now use AI chatbots (up sharply from 2024), yet views of AI's societal and personal impact tilt negative even among heavy young users, and confidence in both government regulation and corporate stewardship is low and falling. Read together, these two national surveys converge on a striking decoupling of adoption from endorsement—use is rising even as trust and optimism are not—which cuts against any simple diffusion-of-innovation story and instead points toward AI being absorbed into daily life under conditions of persistent public unease. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a theoretical scaffold for interpreting this pattern, proposing that trust in generative AI be studied as a multilevel phenomenon (individual, institutional, societal) inherited from, but distinct from, trust in mass and social media; it functions as a conceptual bridge between the survey papers in this cluster and the more targeted disclosure experiments below.

## Trust, Disclosure, and Persuasion in AI-Mediated Communication

A second cluster of studies narrows from general attitudes to the specific mechanics of trust and persuasion when AI is the messenger, particularly in journalism and political communication. [[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] form a tight sub-conversation on AI disclosure in news: Gilardi finds that Swiss readers judge AI-generated, AI-assisted, and human-written news as equivalent in quality when authorship is hidden, and that disclosure even produces a short-term curiosity bump in willingness to continue reading—though this does not convert into durable acceptance of AI journalism. Mattis complicates this picture by disaggregating "AI disclosure" into task-specific stages of the news value chain, showing that Dutch audiences penalize every disclosed AI use, but far more severely for fact-checking than for idea or image generation, with the penalty moderated by political position and AI literacy rather than by topic—a finding that argues against the generic AI-label manipulations used in earlier work, including implicitly Gilardi's. Sbaraini-Fontes extends the comparison from news production to information access more broadly, finding that Italian audiences trust AI-curated search results more than AI-produced news, implying that the *configuration* in which AI appears (intermediary versus producer) shapes trust independently of the underlying model. Together these three papers suggest that "trust in AI" is not a single attitude but a fine-grained judgment contingent on task, role, and disclosure framing—directly operationalizing the multilevel, context-sensitive view that [[Suk2026-ai]] calls for theoretically.

This concern with disclosure and persuasion extends beyond journalism into political communication. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that merely labeling a chatbot as AI (an EU AI Act–style identity disclosure) does little to blunt its persuasive power in direct conversation, but disclosing the chatbot's *persuasive intent* roughly halves attitude change—reframing meaningful transparency as a matter of revealing purpose rather than machine identity. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] approaches persuasion from the opposite direction, showing that *perceived* political bias—rather than disclosed intent—also suppresses an LLM's persuasive effect, by provoking more argumentative pushback rather than disengagement. Read side by side, Rauchfleisch and DiGiuseppe jointly suggest that AI persuasion is fragile along two independent axes: it weakens when audiences suspect a hidden agenda, and separately when they suspect a political slant, even though in both studies the underlying content remains factually accurate. This body of work collectively revises a first wave of AI-persuasion research that treated persuasive capacity as a fixed property of the model, showing instead that it is highly contingent on the social and political framing audiences bring to the interaction—a conclusion that resonates with the "context-dependent acceptance" Fattorini documents at the level of national publics.

## Worker-Reported Productivity, Displacement, and the Labor Behind AI

A third strand turns from audiences to workers, asking how AI reshapes labor from the inside. The Anthropic survey report [[UnknownUnknown-db]] provides the largest worker-facing dataset in this set, linking ~81,000 Claude users' self-reported productivity and displacement concerns to observed occupational AI exposure. Its central finding—that displacement anxiety rises with exposure, that productivity gains cluster at both the top and bottom of the wage distribution, and that gains accrue disproportionately to users themselves rather than employers—offers an empirically grounded counterpoint to the more impressionistic, affect-laden public attitudes captured in Fattorini and Gottfried: workers directly using AI in their jobs report concrete, if unevenly distributed, benefits, even as a fifth voice genuine displacement fears, with a notably U-shaped relationship between perceived speedup and perceived threat.

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] pushes further into the labor *behind* AI systems rather than labor shaped by using them, examining AI red-teaming as a sociotechnical practice that inherits the outsourcing, precarity, and psychological toll documented in earlier content-moderation scholarship. Its argument that red-teaming encodes hidden value judgments and exacts underexamined mental-health costs on crowdworkers complements the Anthropic report's optimistic productivity narrative with a reminder that AI's apparent "self-improvement" rests on invisible, often outsourced human labor—a tension also central to [[Baym2026-tr]], which surveys a decade of worsening platform conditions (wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious creative labor, data extraction) and explicitly names generative AI's appropriation of user-generated content as an intensification of these harms. Baym's call for researchers to move beyond diagnosis toward interdisciplinary intervention echoes Gillespie's call for a coordinated empirical research agenda on red-teaming labor, suggesting a shared disciplinary diagnosis: critique of platform power has not kept pace with its concentration, and AI is accelerating dynamics already visible in social media's political economy.

[[Cotter2019-js]] (despite predating the generative-AI wave) rounds out this labor cluster with a case study of how platform algorithms reconfigure an ostensibly non-digital profession—tattoo artistry—compelling practitioners to perform unpaid, often unrecognized labor to satisfy Instagram's visibility logics. Read alongside the AI-specific papers, it suggests that the dynamics worker-facing AI research is now documenting (algorithmic dependence, blurred labor/leisure boundaries, pressure to perform for the system) are not unique to generative AI but are a continuation of platformization dynamics that generative AI intensifies rather than invents—tying this labor-focused cluster back to Baym's broader claim that AI represents an acceleration, not a rupture, of the social media harms scholars have tracked for a decade.

## Synthesis

Across these twelve papers, a coherent arc emerges: broad national surveys (Fattorini, Gottfried) establish that public adoption of AI is outpacing public trust and understanding; a tighter empirical cluster (Suk, Gilardi, Mattis, Sbaraini-Fontes, Rauchfleisch, DiGiuseppe) shows that this trust gap is not uniform but highly sensitive to disclosure design, task, and perceived intent or bias; and a labor-focused cluster (the Anthropic report, Gillespie, Cotter, Baym) shows that behind these public-facing attitudes sits a more concrete, unevenly distributed set of worker experiences—productivity gains for some, displacement anxiety for others, and often-invisible human labor sustaining the systems themselves. The throughline connecting all three clusters is a shared skepticism toward techno-deterministic narratives of AI's effects, replaced by an emphasis on context, configuration, and power: attitudes and labor outcomes alike depend less on what AI *is* than on how it is disclosed, deployed, and who bears its costs.
