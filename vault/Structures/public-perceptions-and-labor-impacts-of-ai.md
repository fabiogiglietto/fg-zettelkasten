# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Two publics, two levels of exposure

The survey evidence here splits cleanly into studies of *general publics* reacting to AI as an ambient cultural presence and studies of *workers* encountering AI as a tool embedded in their daily practice. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] anchor the first strand, offering near-contemporaneous national snapshots — Italy and the United States, respectively — that converge on a similar diagnosis: rapid growth in everyday AI tool use (chatbots, voice assistants, AI-enabled devices) is running well ahead of self-assessed understanding, and rising familiarity is not translating into rising trust. Fattorini's Italians describe "critical ambivalence" — usefulness expectations paired with mounting perception of AI as a societal threat (up from 35.8% to 51.0% in a single year) — while Gottfried's Americans show declining confidence in both government regulation and corporate stewardship of AI even as half the adult population now uses a chatbot. Both studies converge on a striking demographic pattern: women in both countries express more caution/skepticism than men, and both find that direct hands-on experience (chatbot use, "critical" engagement) tends to soften negative perceptions relative to purely symbolic or media-derived images of AI (robots, sci-fi tropes). The fact that these findings replicate across two very different national media and regulatory environments suggests the ambivalence is not an artifact of any one country's discourse but a more structural feature of how generative AI is currently being absorbed into public consciousness — exposure, not experience, drives fear; experience moderates it.

## Theorizing and testing trust in AI-mediated communication

[[Suk2026-ai]] supplies a conceptual scaffold for this cluster, arguing that trust in generative AI needs a multilevel treatment — individual, institutional, societal — borrowed from decades of mass-media and social-media trust research, but modified because generative systems introduce genuinely new dynamics of interactive, generative output. This framework helps make sense of two more targeted experimental studies that sit downstream of the national-attitude surveys. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] shows that Swiss readers cannot distinguish AI-generated, AI-assisted, and human-written news on quality grounds when authorship is undisclosed, but that disclosure of AI involvement produces a short-lived curiosity bump in willingness to continue reading rather than any durable embrace of AI journalism — a finding that nicely operationalizes Suk's claim that trust judgments are contingent on institutional framing (the "AI" label) rather than any intrinsic property of the message. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] extends the same logic into the political domain, showing that LLM persuasiveness on economic misconceptions collapses substantially when users are primed to see the model as politically biased against their party — not because arguments change, but because perceived source neutrality does. Together these two experiments suggest that "trust" in AI content is less about the content's demonstrable accuracy than about socially-constructed cues (disclosure, imputed bias) that determine how audiences are willing to engage — precisely the institutional-level dynamic Suk's framework anticipates, and a pointed complication for any naive hope that persuasive or informational gains from LLMs will translate uniformly across a politically polarized public.

## Worker-reported productivity, displacement, and the asymmetry of gains

Turning from publics to workers, [[UnknownUnknown-db]] offers the closest thing in this set to direct, large-scale self-report data on AI's labor effects: an 81,000-respondent survey of Claude.ai users linked to Anthropic's own usage telemetry. Its central finding echoes the ambivalence documented in the national surveys, but grounded in lived professional experience rather than abstract expectation: workers in occupations with higher measured AI exposure report more displacement anxiety, early-career workers are more anxious than senior ones, and the productivity gains that *are* reported (mostly framed as expanded task scope rather than raw speed) accrue disproportionately to the workers themselves rather than to employers — except, tellingly, for early-career workers, for whom this self-benefit pattern weakens. This dovetails with Gottfried's finding that AI use in the U.S. is now a genuine workplace phenomenon (38% of employed adults report work-task use) even as broad societal pessimism persists, and it gives an empirical labor-market referent to the abstract "will AI take my job" anxieties that both national surveys register as a rising undercurrent rather than a settled belief.

## The hidden and unevenly distributed labor of building AI

A second, structurally distinct labor strand concerns not people using AI in their jobs but people *whose labor makes AI systems function or governs their harms* — labor that is largely invisible to the publics surveyed above. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] argues that AI red-teaming, now central to safety and policy discourse, is a sociotechnical practice organized by value judgments, outsourced labor arrangements, and serious psychological costs (secondary trauma, moral injury) that closely echo the earlier history of commercial content moderation — a lineage made concrete by [[Tonneau2025-bv]], which documents systematic underinvestment in human moderation for non-English, especially Global South, languages across major platforms, leaving millions of EU users effectively unmoderated. Read together, these two papers show how the human infrastructure underpinning both AI safety (red-teaming) and platform governance (moderation) reproduces the same pattern: internally defined harms, precarious and geographically stratified labor, and a persistent gap between what platforms claim to protect against and whom they actually protect. This is a labor-side counterpart to the demand-side ambivalence found in the public surveys — ordinary users may worry abstractly about AI harms and bias, while a largely hidden workforce absorbs the concrete costs of trying to prevent them, with linguistic and economic inequality built into who does that absorbing.

## Platform-adjacent labor and the ambiguity of "AI-era" work

Finally, [[Volpe2026-um]] sits at a slight tangent to the rest of the set — its focus is platform algorithmic pressure and creator labor rather than AI systems per se — but it belongs here because it dramatizes, at the level of individual gig workers, the same tension between authentic self-presentation and structurally-compelled performance that recurs across the collection: just as red-teamers and moderators perform emotionally taxing labor under corporate-defined value systems, and workers in the Anthropic survey narrate productivity gains within employer-set terms, Volpe's micro-influencers perform care, expertise, and ethical commitment as adaptive survival strategies within an algorithmically demanding attention economy. Read across the whole set, a common thread emerges: whether the subject is a national public forming impressions of chatbots, a worker gauging AI's effect on their career, or a red-teamer or moderator absorbing the human costs of keeping AI systems safe, attitudes and experiences of AI are consistently shaped less by the technology's raw capabilities than by the institutional and platform structures — disclosure practices, outsourcing arrangements, algorithmic incentives, perceived political neutrality — within which people encounter it.
