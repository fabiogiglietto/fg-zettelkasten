# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

### The shape of public opinion: ambivalence, not technophobia

At the broadest level, several papers in this collection map how ordinary publics feel about AI once it moves from headline to habit. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] offers the most explicitly longitudinal picture, tracking Italian attitudes across 2023–2024 and finding a "critical ambivalence" that resists both techno-utopian and technophobic framings: usage of AI tools grows fast, but self-reported understanding lags, and support is sharply context-dependent — high for security applications, low for journalism, hiring, and art. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] complements this with a U.S. snapshot showing that adoption (roughly half of adults now use chatbots) has outpaced trust, with majorities across age groups expecting net societal harm and declining confidence in government's capacity to regulate AI. Read together, these two survey-based accounts converge on a striking pattern: familiarity with AI tools is not producing reassurance. If anything, in both countries, direct use coexists with — and in Italy's case even predicts — rising perceptions of AI as a societal threat. This decouples the "digital divide" logic (in which exposure breeds comfort) from what seems to be happening with AI, where domain-specific risk perception and demographic cleavages (age, gender, education) do more explanatory work than raw exposure. [[Suk2026-ai]] provides a theoretical scaffolding for interpreting these findings, proposing that trust in generative AI must be studied as a multilevel phenomenon — individual, institutional, societal — borrowing from decades of mass-media and social-media trust research. This framework helps explain why the Fattorini and Gottfried surveys find such heterogeneous, context-bound attitudes: trust is not a single dial but a bundle of judgments operating at different levels simultaneously.

### Trust and disclosure in AI-mediated information

A tightly linked cluster of papers zooms into one especially contentious domain of public attitude: AI in journalism and news search. [[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] each test how disclosure of AI involvement changes audience trust and engagement, and together they complicate a naive "disclosure improves accountability" narrative. Gilardi et al. find that quality perceptions of AI-generated versus human-written news are statistically indistinguishable pre-disclosure, but that disclosure itself produces a curiosity-driven bump in short-term engagement without translating into durable acceptance — suggesting audiences are intrigued by AI authorship without endorsing it. Mattis et al. push further by disaggregating "AI disclosure" into task-specific stages of the news value chain, showing that all disclosures reduce trust, but unevenly: fact-checking by AI incurs the steepest penalty, while earlier-stage tasks like idea or image generation are more tolerated. This granularity argues against the generic AI-label paradigm used in most experimental work, including implicitly in Gilardi et al., since it reveals audience concern is task-sensitive rather than binary. Sbaraini-Fontes et al. extend the comparison outward from within-newsroom production to competing information intermediaries, finding that AI-generated search results are trusted more than AI-involved news — implying that trust doesn't hinge on AI-ness per se but on the institutional frame surrounding it (search engine vs. news brand). Together these three studies suggest that "meaningful transparency" is a much more fractured design problem than blanket disclosure policies assume, a conclusion that dovetails with [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]]'s persuasion-focused finding (discussed below) that disclosing mere AI identity does little, while disclosing intent or specific task involvement moves attitudes substantially.

### Persuasion, political bias, and the contingency of AI's influence

A second thread interrogates AI not just as an object of attitudes but as an active persuasive agent, and here the papers trace an arc from unconditional capability to conditional, governable effect. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the baseline: frontier conversational AI reliably out-persuades even elite, coached, professionally incentivized human persuaders, both on stated policy attitudes and on real monetary donations, with the advantage traced to raw informational throughput (fact density) rather than rapport or perceived humanness. This sets up two natural questions the remaining papers pursue — can this power be constrained by regulation-style disclosure, and is it contingent on how the source is perceived politically? [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] answers the regulatory question directly: EU AI Act–style identity labeling barely dents persuasion, but disclosing the chatbot's persuasive intent roughly halves its effect — again reinforcing the Mattis/Gilardi lesson that not all disclosures are equal, and that "what the system is trying to do" matters more than "what the system is." [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] answers the political-contingency question, showing that LLM persuasiveness on economic misconceptions drops substantially when users are told the model is biased against their party, an effect driven by increased motivated pushback rather than disengagement. Read as a sequence, Hackenburg et al. establishes that AI persuasion is extremely powerful in a depoliticized, credibility-neutral setting; Rauchfleisch et al. shows this power can be meaningfully checked by targeted transparency; and DiGiuseppe et al. shows it is also checked, even without any disclosure intervention, simply by activating partisan suspicion about the source. Together they paint AI persuasion as formidable but not unconditional — bounded by both regulatory design and by the same partisan psychology that governs human-source credibility.

### Worker-facing effects: productivity, displacement, and the labor behind the systems

The final strand shifts from audience/citizen attitudes to workers' lived experience of AI, at two very different points in the AI value chain. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], Anthropic's large-scale survey of Claude.ai users, offers a rare worker-reported complement to task-exposure models of AI's labor impact: workers in occupations with higher measured AI exposure report more displacement anxiety, productivity gains are concentrated among both the highest- and lowest-paid occupations (mostly via expanded task scope rather than pure speed), and early-career workers are both more anxious and less likely to report personally capturing the gains than senior workers. This self-report data usefully triangulates against — and partially validates — the exposure-based approaches common in AI economic-impact research, while also surfacing distributional concerns (who captures productivity gains) that task-based measures cannot see. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] examines the opposite end of the AI production process: not those using AI systems downstream, but the largely invisible workers who build their safety guardrails through red-teaming. Drawing a sustained parallel to the history of commercial content moderation, the paper argues that red-teaming is a sociotechnical practice bound up with contested value judgments (whose harms count), precarious outsourced labor arrangements, and serious psychological costs (secondary trauma, moral injury) that current organizational safeguards do not adequately address. Placed alongside the Anthropic survey, Gillespie et al. rounds out the labor picture: workers experience AI not only as a tool that reshapes their tasks and job security (as captured in the productivity/displacement self-reports) but, for a specific and growing workforce, as a site of direct, hidden, and hazardous labor performed to make these same systems seem safe and trustworthy to the publics described in the opinion surveys above.

### Synthesis

Across these eleven papers, a coherent arc emerges: publics are adopting AI faster than they are coming to trust or understand it (Fattorini, Gottfried, Suk); the specific configuration and disclosed intent of an AI system — not merely its presence — determines whether that ambivalence hardens into distrust or softens into curiosity (Gilardi, Mattis, Sbaraini-Fontes, Rauchfleisch); AI's demonstrated persuasive power is real but governable, contingent on perceived political neutrality and on what is disclosed about its purpose (Hackenburg, DiGiuseppe, Rauchfleisch); and beneath all of this, workers — both those redeployed by AI in their daily jobs and those laboring to build its safety infrastructure — are reporting effects, benefits, and harms that existing exposure-based or purely technical accounts of AI's societal footprint fail to capture (UnknownUnknown-db, Gillespie). The throughline is that AI's public and labor-market reception is not a fixed reaction to a fixed technology, but a highly conditional response shaped by disclosure design, institutional framing, political identity, and occupational position.
