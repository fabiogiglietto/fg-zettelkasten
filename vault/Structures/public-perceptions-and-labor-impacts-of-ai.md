# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Two Publics, Two Kinds of Evidence

The papers filed here split along a methodological fault line that turns out to also be a conceptual one. One cluster relies on large-scale representative or platform surveys to describe *what people already believe and do* about AI — [[Gottfried2026-ww]], [[Fattorini2026-bo]], and [[UnknownUnknown-db]] are the clearest instances, each mapping a national or platform population's attitudes, usage, and self-reported economic experience. The other cluster runs controlled experiments to isolate *causal mechanisms* behind specific reactions — trust in AI-labeled news ([[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]]), persuasion by AI interlocutors ([[Hackenburg2026-ud]], [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]]), and acceptance of AI as an information intermediary among conspiracy-prone users ([[Dubey2026-bl]]). Two additional papers step outside both traditions to theorize or ethically interrogate the field itself: [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a conceptual scaffolding for trust research, and [[Gillespie2026-aa]] shifts the lens from audiences/users entirely onto the invisible workers who build AI's safety infrastructure. Read together, the set traces an arc from broad-brush attitude description, through mechanism-level experimentation on trust and persuasion, to a labor-and-governance critique that asks whose experience of AI has been left out of the survey instrument altogether.

## National and Platform-Level Attitudes: Ambivalence as the Norm

The descriptive surveys converge on a picture of *ambivalence rather than rejection*. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] names this directly as "critical ambivalence" among Italians — rising AI tool use alongside rising perception of AI as a societal threat, with acceptance sharply domain-dependent (high for security, low for journalism and worker management). [[Gottfried2026-ww]] finds the same structural tension in the U.S.: chatbot adoption climbing toward half the population even as majorities expect societal harm and distrust both government regulation and corporate self-governance. Both papers show this ambivalence is not evenly distributed — gender, age, and direct experience with AI tools systematically shape optimism versus caution, with women and older or less-engaged respondents more skeptical in both national contexts. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] complicates the picture from the labor side: displacement anxiety tracks *observed* occupational AI exposure rather than free-floating fear, and productivity gains cluster paradoxically at both the top and bottom of the wage distribution, with early-career workers reporting more concern and less personal benefit than seniors. Together these three suggest that public attitudes are not simple technophobia or technophilia but a rational-seeming response to heterogeneous, unevenly distributed personal stakes — exposure, benefit, and threat do not move together for everyone.

## Trust as a Configurable, Task-Specific Variable

The journalism-and-trust cluster pushes this heterogeneity down to the level of specific disclosures and configurations, showing that "trust in AI" dissolves under scrutiny into many separately-moving parts. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that undisclosed AI-written news is judged no differently from human-written news on quality, but disclosure produces a short-lived curiosity bump rather than durable acceptance. [[Mattis2026-gu]] disaggregates further: disclosure penalties are universal in direction but vary enormously by *which task* in the news production chain is disclosed (fact-checking hurts most, idea generation least), and by the reader's politics and AI literacy — yielding five distinct audience segments rather than one public. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] adds a further axis of variation: the same underlying technology is trusted differently depending on whether it appears as a search intermediary or as a newsroom author, with search enjoying a marked trust advantage. [[Suk2026-ai]] tries to give this scattered empirical picture a home, proposing that trust in generative AI must be theorized across individual, institutional, and societal levels by analogy with — but not reduction to — the mass-media and social-media trust literatures. Read against the disclosure studies, the multilevel framework looks less like abstract theorizing and more like a needed corrective: task, context, and configuration effects are exactly the kind of institutional-level variation that a purely individual-differences model of trust would miss.

## Persuasion and the Limits of Neutrality

A third strand asks not whether people trust AI outputs but whether AI can *change their minds*, and here the two papers productively disagree in scope while agreeing on mechanism. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the headline result that frontier AI out-persuades even elite, coached human debaters and professional canvassers, on both attitudes and real-money donations, and traces this advantage to sheer information throughput — AI simply deploys more fact-checkable claims per unit time. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] takes this baseline persuasive power as given and asks what happens when the *perceived neutrality* of the persuader is compromised: a brief partisan-bias warning cuts belief correction by roughly a quarter, operating through increased user pushback and counter-argument rather than disengagement. Read together, these findings suggest AI persuasion has two independent levers — raw informational density and perceived source credibility — and that the second can substantially throttle the first once users are primed to see the model as a partisan actor rather than a neutral information source. [[Dubey2026-bl]] offers a partial counterpoint from the conspiracy-belief literature: contrary to the expectation that distrustful users would reject an AI news intermediary, high-conspiracy-belief individuals rated a "balanced" chatbot as *more* trustworthy and useful than low-conspiracy-belief users did — suggesting that perceived neutrality's effects on trust are not simply a matter of priors about mainstream authority, and that motivated reasoning about AI messengers can cut in counterintuitive directions.

## The Labor Left Out of the Survey

If the bulk of this topic's papers ask what publics *think* about AI, [[Gillespie2026-aa]] insists on asking who is *doing the work* that makes today's AI systems seem safe and trustworthy enough to survey opinions about in the first place. Its account of red-teaming as outsourced, psychologically costly, value-laden labor — structurally parallel to the early history of commercial content moderation — has no direct empirical counterpart in the attitude surveys above, but it reframes their scope. The productivity and displacement findings in [[UnknownUnknown-db]] describe workers *using* AI; Gillespie's essay describes workers who make AI usable, and whose working conditions remain invisible precisely because red-teaming's harm categories, labor arrangements, and psychological costs are defined internally and proprietarily by the same companies whose chatbots are the subject of the trust and persuasion experiments elsewhere in this set. Taken as a whole, the topic traces a movement from measuring what the public believes about AI, to explaining why those beliefs vary by task and disclosure, to demonstrating AI's real persuasive and economic force in people's lives, and finally to insisting that any full account of "labor experiences with AI" must include the hidden workforce sustaining the systems everyone else is being surveyed about.
