# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## The Shape of Public and Worker Experience with AI

This cluster traces a single underlying question across several registers: how do ordinary people — as citizens, news audiences, workers, and conversational partners — actually perceive, use, and get shaped by AI systems, as distinct from how systems are designed or how capable they are in the abstract? The papers filed here range from large-N national surveys to tightly controlled persuasion experiments to essays on the labor hidden behind AI safety work, but they converge on a shared empirical move: taking self-reports and behavioral traces from the public and from workers as primary data, rather than treating "impact" as something to be inferred solely from model benchmarks or economic indices.

### National attitudes: ambivalence, not technophobia

Two large survey efforts anchor the population-level view. [[Gottfried2026-ww]]'s Pew survey of U.S. adults documents a familiar paradox: adoption is surging (chatbot use near half the population, smart devices spreading) while sentiment tilts negative, including among the heaviest-using younger cohorts. [[Fattorini2026-bo]]'s Italian data tell a structurally similar story — rapid growth in tool use paired with a persistent exposure/understanding gap and rising perceptions of AI as a societal threat — but frame it explicitly as "critical ambivalence" rather than simple fear, with acceptance highly context-dependent (welcomed in security applications, rejected in journalism, hiring, and art). Read together, these two papers suggest that public AI attitudes are not converging toward either enthusiasm or backlash but stabilizing into a durable, domain-sensitive skepticism that tracks who is affected and how directly. [[Suk2026-ai]] supplies a conceptual scaffold for interpreting this pattern, arguing that trust in generative AI should be theorized across individual, institutional, and societal levels by analogy with — but not reduction to — decades of mass-media and social-media trust research; it is less an empirical contribution than a lens for organizing findings like those in Gottfried and Fattorini.

### Worker-reported productivity, displacement, and the labor behind the systems

A second thread turns from attitudes to lived occupational experience. The Anthropic survey of Claude users ([[UnknownUnknown-db]]) operationalizes "worker experience" directly: workers in occupations with higher observed AI exposure report more displacement anxiety, productivity gains concentrate among the highest- and lowest-paid groups (mostly via expanded task scope rather than raw speed), and early-career workers feel both more threatened and less likely to personally capture AI's gains than senior workers. This complements the attitudinal surveys by showing that concern is not evenly distributed across the public but patterned by actual exposure and career stage — a finding that links discourse-level ambivalence (Fattorini, Gottfried) to material stakes. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] inverts the lens to look at the invisible workers who build AI's guardrails: red-teamers. It argues that red-teaming, like content moderation before it, is being normalized as a technical safety procedure when it is in fact a sociotechnical labor system — one that embeds contested value judgments about harm, is increasingly outsourced and precarious, and exacts real psychological costs on the people doing adversarial, transgressive work. Where the Anthropic report asks how AI changes work for the broad public, Gillespie asks how AI's own production depends on an under-examined occupational class whose displacement concerns and productivity narratives are rarely surveyed at all — an implicit rebuke to narratives (echoed in some displacement discourse) that treat this labor as readily automatable rather than as skilled, harmed, and organizationally obscured.

### Persuasion, trust, and the limits of AI's conversational power

A third cluster probes a more specific behavioral question: when people actually talk to AI, how persuasive is it, and what conditions that persuasion? [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the baseline capability claim at scale — frontier conversational AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, an advantage traced to sheer information throughput rather than rapport, and one that survives into costly real-money behavior. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] then interrogate how this power can be blunted through disclosure and framing. DiGiuseppe shows that simply priming users to believe a model is politically biased against them meaningfully dampens belief change, operating through increased pushback and counter-argument rather than disengagement — a direct extension of partisan motivated-reasoning theory into the chatbot setting. Rauchfleisch shows a parallel but distinct result for transparency regulation: disclosing that one is talking to an AI does almost nothing to reduce persuasion, but disclosing the AI's persuasive *intent* roughly halves it. Together these three papers sketch a coherent empirical finding that policy discourse has not yet absorbed: the public is not simply vulnerable or simply skeptical toward AI persuasion — persuasive power is highly contingent on perceived source motives (political and strategic), and the right regulatory lever is revealing *what the system is trying to do*, not merely what it is.

### Trust in AI-mediated information and news

A closely related set of studies narrows this same disclosure question to the journalism context specifically, asking how audiences evaluate news and search content once AI's hand becomes visible. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that Swiss readers cannot distinguish AI-assisted, AI-generated, and human-written news on quality grounds when blind, but disclosure produces a short-lived curiosity bump in engagement that fails to translate into durable acceptance. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates the picture by disaggregating "AI disclosure" into specific newsroom tasks, showing that trust penalties are not uniform — fact-checking disclosures sting most, idea/image generation least — and that reactions cluster into distinct audience segments shaped by political position and AI literacy, challenging the simpler binary disclosure effects assumed in earlier work (including, implicitly, the news-specific framing in Gilardi). [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends the comparison across ecosystem configurations, finding that Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-involved news, suggesting that the locus of AI's role in the information chain — intermediary versus producer — itself shapes legitimacy judgments. Read against the persuasion cluster, these three papers suggest a consistent asymmetry: in direct one-on-one conversation, mere AI identity disclosure barely matters (Rauchfleisch), whereas in mediated news contexts, identity/process disclosure matters quite a lot and unevenly by task (Mattis, Sbaraini-Fontes) — implying that the "meaningful transparency" problem is not a single design question but one that depends heavily on the institutional and relational context in which AI involvement is revealed.

### Synthesis

Across this topic, a layered arc emerges: broad national surveys (Gottfried, Fattorini) establish that public sentiment toward AI is ambivalent, unevenly distributed, and shaped by direct exposure rather than abstract awareness; worker-facing data (Anthropic survey, Gillespie) ground that ambivalence in material occupational stakes, from displacement anxiety to the hidden psychological costs of safety labor; and the persuasion and trust experiments (DiGiuseppe, Hackenburg, Rauchfleisch, Gilardi, Mattis, Sbaraini-Fontes) show that these attitudes are not fixed dispositions but actively contingent on framing, disclosure, and perceived intent — with Suk's multilevel framework offering a way to knit individual, institutional, and societal findings into a single theoretical account. The overall trajectory of inquiry moves from "what do people think about AI" toward "under what specific conditions of exposure, disclosure, and labor arrangement does that thinking — and AI's power over it — change," a question this set of papers answers more through accumulated particulars than through any single unifying theory.
