# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

This cluster of papers traces a common arc: from broad national surveys of how ordinary people feel about AI, to close-grained studies of how workers experience it on the job, to critical accounts of the discourse and political economy that shape both. Read together, they suggest that public "attitudes" toward AI are not a single, stable variable but a family of context-dependent judgments—about specific tasks, disclosures, and occupations—that are themselves partly manufactured by the industry actors under study.

### National attitudes: ambivalence, not technophobia

Two large-scale survey studies anchor the descriptive end of this topic. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] tracks Italian public opinion across 2023–2024 and finds what the authors call "critical ambivalence": rising use of generative AI tools alongside persistently low self-assessed understanding, and highly context-dependent acceptance (welcomed for security, rejected for journalism, art, and worker management). [[Gottfried2026-ww]] finds a structurally similar pattern in the US—rapid adoption of chatbots and smart devices co-existing with net-negative expectations about AI's societal and personal impact, even among heavy young adult users, and declining trust in both government regulation and corporate stewardship. Together these two national snapshots establish a key empirical puzzle that recurs throughout the topic: adoption and skepticism are rising in tandem, and demographic cleavages (age, gender, education, political identity) consistently shape which AI applications people find legitimate. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a theoretical scaffold for making sense of this pattern, arguing that trust in generative AI needs to be modeled multilevel—individual, institutional, societal—by extending decades of media-trust scholarship rather than treating AI trust as sui generis.

### Trust in AI-mediated information: task and disclosure matter more than the label

A tight sub-cluster of experimental studies drills into one domain where these general attitudes cash out concretely: news and information intermediaries. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] shows that Swiss readers cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, and that disclosure of AI involvement produces only a short-lived curiosity bump in engagement rather than durable acceptance. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates the disclosure story further: using task-specific rather than generic AI labels, it finds uniformly negative trust penalties, but with a clear hierarchy (fact-checking punished most, idea/image generation least) and audience segmentation into distinct preference profiles moderated by political position and AI literacy rather than by news topic. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] adds a comparative twist, finding that Italian audiences trust AI-mediated search results more than AI-produced news—suggesting that the *configuration* in which AI appears (intermediary vs. producer) matters as much as the fact of AI involvement itself. Read against [[Suk2026-ai]]'s multilevel framework, these three studies operationalize exactly the kind of task- and institution-specific trust dynamics that a media-trust-derived model predicts: general skepticism from the national surveys resolves, at the experimental level, into finely graded, task-contingent judgments rather than blanket rejection or acceptance.

### Worker-reported experience: productivity gains, displacement anxiety, and hidden labor

A second strand shifts from audiences to workers, asking not how people feel about AI in the abstract but how it reshapes their labor. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], drawing on ~81,000 Claude.ai users, finds that self-reported displacement concern tracks observed occupational AI exposure, that productivity gains are concentrated at the wage extremes and driven more by expanded task scope than raw speed, and that early-career workers report both higher anxiety and lower personal benefit than senior professionals—linking survey self-report directly to usage-based exposure metrics. This "worker as respondent" perspective is complemented by two studies that examine the *invisible* workers who build and maintain AI systems rather than merely use them. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] reframes AI red-teaming as sociotechnical labor structurally analogous to content moderation—outsourced, value-laden, and psychologically costly—and warns against narratives of imminent automation that erase this labor from view. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] provides an empirical counterpart at industrial scale, showing via DSA transparency data that platforms systematically under-resource content moderation in Global South languages, meaning the invisible labor of moderation is not only precarious but unevenly distributed by linguistic geography. Together these three pieces push against a purely user-centric view of "AI labor experience," insisting that the people producing safety and trust infrastructure for AI systems are themselves a labor population whose displacement anxieties, working conditions, and exposure to harm deserve the same empirical attention paid to white-collar chatbot users.

### Beyond attitudes: discourse, decoys, and political economy

Finally, [[Vertesi2026-lv]] steps back from survey-based measurement to interrogate the discursive terrain on which all these attitudes and labor experiences are formed. Its argument that AI critique is routinely captured by five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) offers a useful corrective lens for the rest of the topic: public ambivalence documented in [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]], anxieties about displacement in [[UnknownUnknown-db]], and even scholarly attention to red-teaming labor in [[Gillespie2026-aa]] can all be partly read as responses to a discourse actively shaped by AI firms to direct scrutiny toward safety, disruption, or technical particulars and away from the underlying concentration of capital and infrastructural power. This framing does not discount the empirical findings elsewhere in the topic, but it asks that they be situated within the broader "Project of AI"—a caution that public attitudes and worker reports are themselves partly endogenous to industry narrative-making, not simply exogenous reactions to a neutral technology.

### Synthesis

Across these ten papers, a layered picture emerges: broad national ambivalence ([[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]]) is substantiated at finer grain by experimental work on trust and disclosure in specific domains like journalism ([[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]]), theorized through frameworks borrowed from media trust research ([[Suk2026-ai]]), and grounded in concrete labor experience both on the user side ([[UnknownUnknown-db]]) and on the hidden production side ([[Gillespie2026-aa]], [[Tonneau2025-bv]]). [[Vertesi2026-lv]] supplies the critical vantage point from which to read all of this: attitudes and labor experiences are not simply "found" by survey instruments but are actively shaped by a discourse whose decoys direct public and scholarly attention toward safety and disruption while obscuring the political economy that structures who builds, moderates, and bears the costs of AI systems.
