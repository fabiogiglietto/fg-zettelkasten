# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## National and Public Attitudes Toward AI

Two nationally representative surveys anchor the descriptive core of this topic: [[Fattorini2026-bo]] on Italian publics and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] on American adults. Despite different methodologies and years, they converge on a similar diagnosis — rapid growth in everyday AI use (chatbots, voice assistants, search summaries) running well ahead of self-reported understanding, and attitudes that are neither naive enthusiasm nor blanket technophobia but a wary, context-dependent ambivalence. Fattorini's Italians accept AI readily in security applications but resist it in journalism, education, and worker management; Gottfried's Americans report rising chatbot use even as majorities say AI is advancing "too quickly" and expect it to erode privacy and produce net societal harm. Both studies emphasize demographic fracture lines — age, gender, education — and both note the striking pattern of heavy-user skepticism, especially among the young: familiarity does not straightforwardly breed trust. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a conceptual scaffold for making sense of this terrain, proposing that trust in generative AI must be theorized at individual, institutional, and societal levels simultaneously, borrowing from decades of mass-media and social-media trust research. Read together, these three papers suggest that public opinion on AI is less a single attitude to be measured than a layered, culturally inflected negotiation — one for which existing survey snapshots ([[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]]) supply data that a multilevel framework like [[Suk2026-ai]] is only beginning to organize theoretically.

## Trust, Persuasion, and the Limits of AI Credibility

A second cluster interrogates not just whether people trust AI in the abstract, but how that trust (or its absence) mediates AI's actual influence. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the ceiling case: frontier LLMs reliably out-persuade even elite, coached human debaters and professional canvassers, an advantage traced to sheer information throughput rather than to perceived humanness or rapport. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] immediately complicates this picture by showing that persuasive power is not source-independent — a brief cue that the model is "biased against your party" cuts belief correction by roughly a quarter, operating through increased argumentative pushback rather than disengagement. Together these two experiments imply a conditional model of AI persuasion: extraordinarily powerful under conditions of perceived neutrality, but attenuated once partisan suspicion is activated — a boundary condition [[Hackenburg2026-ud]]'s apolitical framing does not test but [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] makes central. [[Dubey2026-bl]] approaches the same trust-persuasion nexus from the angle of individual differences, finding a counterintuitive result: users with stronger conspiracy beliefs report *more* trust in a "balanced" news chatbot than low-conspiracy users, driven by perceived usefulness and trust rather than ease of use. This unsettles the intuitive expectation that conspiracy-minded users would be most resistant to algorithmic mediation, and — alongside [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]]'s finding that perceived bias is what matters, not actual content — points to a broader theme: AI's persuasive and trust-generating power is governed less by the accuracy or balance of its outputs than by audience-side priors about the messenger.

## Disclosure and Trust in AI-Assisted Journalism

A closely related pair of studies narrows this question to a specific domain — news production — and specifically to the effect of *disclosure*. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that undisclosed AI-generated and AI-assisted news is rated equivalent to human-written news on credibility, readability, and expertise, but that disclosing AI involvement produces a short-term curiosity bump in willingness to continue reading, one that does not translate into durable acceptance of AI journalism. [[Mattis2026-gu]] pushes further into the mechanics of disclosure, showing that once task-specificity is introduced — distinguishing idea generation from fact-checking from full article writing — every type of AI disclosure lowers trust, with fact-checking penalized most heavily and idea/image generation least. Crucially, [[Mattis2026-gu]] finds no news-topic moderation but strong moderation by political position and AI literacy, and identifies five distinct audience segments rather than a single "AI-skeptical public." Read against [[Gilardi2026-hw]]'s finding that undisclosed quality is indistinguishable from human work, the pairing suggests that audience skepticism toward AI journalism is a labeling effect layered on top of content that audiences cannot otherwise detect or fault — a dynamic that echoes the messenger-not-message logic found in [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Dubey2026-bl]] above, now transposed into the news domain.

## Worker Experiences: Productivity, Displacement, and the Labor Behind AI

Where the previous clusters center audiences and citizens, two papers shift attention to workers — both those using AI and those laboring to make it safe. [[UnknownUnknown-db]]'s analysis of ~81,000 Claude.ai users provides the topic's most direct evidence on self-reported productivity and displacement: workers in high-AI-exposure occupations report proportionally more displacement anxiety, productivity gains cluster among both the highest- and lowest-paid workers (chiefly through expanded task scope rather than raw speed), and early-career workers report both weaker personal benefit and greater fear of displacement than senior professionals — a distributional asymmetry that resonates with the age-based fracture lines seen in the national attitude surveys ([[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]]). [[Gillespie2026-aa]] inverts the lens entirely, arguing that the invisible labor *behind* AI safety — red-teaming — deserves the same sociological scrutiny as the workers who use AI's outputs. Drawing an explicit parallel to content moderation, the paper insists that red-teamers' psychological toll, precarious outsourced labor arrangements, and the value judgments embedded in their work are being obscured by narratives of eventual automation. Placed alongside [[UnknownUnknown-db]], this pairing broadens what "labor experience with AI" means in this literature: not only the displacement and productivity effects felt by AI's end-users, but the hidden, structurally precarious labor of those who build the guardrails those users benefit from.

## Who Shapes the Discourse

Underlying all of these attitudinal and experiential findings is the question of who produces the public discourse that audiences and workers are responding to in the first place. [[Richter2026-bt]] supplies a longitudinal, comparative answer, tracing Twitter-based AI discourse in the US and Germany from 2012–2021. It finds a striking divergence: German discourse institutionalized, with academic, advocacy, and governmental accounts gaining prominence particularly after 2018 (coinciding with EU regulatory debates), while US discourse became increasingly dominated first by industry and then by individual tech influencers. The paper argues these formative-era stakeholder configurations left durable imprints on each country's subsequent GenAI regulatory and public discourse — a claim that offers a plausible upstream explanation for some of the cross-national attitudinal contrasts visible in [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]]: publics inherit not a neutral information environment but one shaped by who got to speak, and how, during AI's formative decade. Read as a whole, this topic traces an arc from that upstream discourse-shaping, through the audience-level trust and persuasion dynamics it enables or constrains, into the disclosure-sensitive judgments people make about AI-assisted content, and finally down to the concrete, unevenly distributed productivity and displacement experiences — and hidden labor costs — that AI generates for the people who use and build it.
