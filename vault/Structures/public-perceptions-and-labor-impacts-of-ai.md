# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

The papers gathered here approach "public attitudes toward AI" from two largely separate vantage points that this note tries to draw into conversation: large-scale survey work measuring what publics and workers *say* about AI, and structural/critical work asking what such measurement itself obscures or naturalizes. Read together, they trace an arc from national opinion polling, through domain-specific trust experiments in journalism, to worker self-reports on productivity and displacement, and finally to reflexive critiques of the discourse and labor infrastructure that make AI systems possible at all.

### National and cross-national attitude surveys

The most straightforward empirical anchor is a pair of nationally representative opinion studies. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] documents the U.S. picture in 2026: chatbot adoption is now mainstream (about half of adults), yet views of AI's societal and personal impact skew negative even among heavy young users, and confidence in both government regulation and corporate stewardship is low and falling. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] offers an Italian counterpart across 2023–2024, finding a similar co-existence of rising usage and rising unease—what the authors term "critical ambivalence" rather than technophobia—with acceptance sharply conditional on domain (high for security, low for journalism, hiring, and art). Together these two surveys establish a robust cross-national pattern: adoption and skepticism are rising in tandem, publics differentiate sharply by use-case rather than holding a single global attitude, and socio-demographic cleavages (age, gender, education, direct experience) consistently structure both perceived understanding and evaluative valence. Both papers push back against simple "knowledge deficit" models of public opinion, showing that even heavy users remain uncertain or wary, and that experience with AI tools reduces some fears (e.g., humanoid-robot imagery, threat perceptions) without eliminating deeper concerns about societal control and regulation.

### Trust in AI-mediated information and journalism

A cluster of studies narrows this attitudinal question to the news domain, asking how disclosure of AI involvement shapes perceived credibility and engagement. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that Swiss readers cannot distinguish AI-assisted, AI-generated, and human-written news on quality grounds when authorship is blind, but that *disclosing* AI involvement paradoxically boosts short-term willingness to keep reading—an effect the authors attribute to curiosity rather than genuine endorsement, since it doesn't carry over to future intentions. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates the disclosure story by disaggregating "AI use" into specific journalistic tasks: every task-specific disclosure lowers trust, but the penalty is smallest for early-value-chain tasks (idea/image generation) and largest for fact-checking, and these effects are moderated by political position and AI literacy rather than topic—yielding five distinct audience "trust profiles" rather than a single homogeneous skeptical public. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] adds a further wrinkle: Italians trust AI-generated *search* results more than AI-involved *news*, suggesting that the configuration in which AI appears—intermediary versus institutional producer—matters as much as the mere fact of AI involvement. Read against [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], which shows that perceived political bias in a conversational AI measurably degrades its persuasive power (via increased pushback and argumentation, not disengagement), a common thread emerges: trust and persuasive efficacy are not fixed properties of AI outputs but are highly contingent on source framing, task context, and perceived neutrality—effects that quality-blind or generic-label experiments would miss. [[Suk2026-ai]] provides a theoretical scaffold for this empirical cluster, arguing that trust in generative AI needs a multilevel framework (individual, institutional, societal) analogous to, but distinct from, decades of mass-media and social-media trust research; it retroactively organizes the disclosure and bias-perception findings as instances of individual- and institutional-level trust dynamics still awaiting a fuller societal-level account.

### Worker-reported productivity and displacement

Shifting from attitudes-toward to experiences-with AI, [[UnknownUnknown-db]] uses Anthropic's Claude.ai user survey to connect self-reported productivity and displacement concerns to observed occupational exposure data. It finds a graded, exposure-correlated rise in displacement anxiety, a U-shaped relationship between speedup and perceived threat, and a productivity story dominated by "scope expansion" rather than raw speed—with gains disproportionately captured by workers themselves rather than employers, though less so for early-career workers, who also report the highest displacement concern. This paper functions as the empirical bridge between the attitude surveys above and the labor-critical papers below: it shows concretely how the abstract unease documented by Pew and Observa cashes out in occupation-specific, career-stage-specific worker experience, while also illustrating how industry-run surveys (Claude classifying its own users' open-text responses) blur the line between disinterested measurement and platform self-narration.

### Structural critiques: labor, discourse, and political economy

The final two papers step back from measuring attitudes to interrogating the infrastructures and discourses that attitude research too often takes for granted. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] reframes AI red-teaming—ostensibly a technical safety practice—as a sociotechnical labor system inheriting the value judgments, outsourcing patterns, and psychological harms of commercial content moderation, arguing that the "workers" shaping AI safety are as consequential and as under-studied as the "publics" who use AI's outputs. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] goes further, arguing that most AI critique and accountability work—including, implicitly, survey-based attitude and disruption narratives—functions as a "decoy" that individualizes or technicizes what is actually a project of political-economic world-building by capitalized actors. Its "disruption decoy" concept directly challenges how displacement is framed in papers like [[UnknownUnknown-db]]: workforce disruption talk, it argues, can obscure corporate restructuring and infrastructural power concentration rather than illuminate it.

### The arc of inquiry

Taken as a whole, the set moves from *description* (what do people believe and do with AI, at national scale and in specific domains like news) to *mechanism* (why trust and persuasion shift with disclosure, bias perception, and task framing) to *lived consequence* (how workers experience productivity gains and displacement fears unevenly across career stage and occupation) to *reflexive critique* (whose labor and which discourses construct the very categories—safety, disruption, trust—that the survey literature measures). The productive tension running through the folder is between attitude/experience research that treats public and worker perception as the primary object of study, and the two critical pieces that ask whether such perception research, however rigorous, risks measuring the shadows cast by a political-economic and labor apparatus (red-teaming, content moderation-style ghost work, "Project of AI" network power) that remains largely invisible to respondents themselves.
