# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## The arc of inquiry

This body of work traces a movement from *capability* to *consequence*: it begins by asking what conversational AI can now do to human minds and workplaces, then asks how publics perceive and regulate that capacity, and finally asks how workers and institutions are actually being reshaped by it on the ground. Read together, the papers form a rough pipeline — persuasion mechanics, trust and disclosure, national/demographic attitudes, and labor/discourse effects — that converges on a single tension: the gap between what AI systems are technically capable of and what publics understand, trust, or are equipped to contest.

## Persuasion as a measurable, exploitable lever

The empirical core of this capability question is the trio of large-scale persuasion experiments. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] establishes that conversational AI's persuasive power is driven less by scale than by post-training and information density — and that this density trades off against factual accuracy, a troubling finding given how casually "bigger model, better answers" is often assumed. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] pushes this further, showing frontier AI out-persuading even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the same fact-density mechanism implicated and, crucially, translating into real financial behavior (charitable donations), not just survey attitudes. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complicates the picture by showing this persuasive power is not unconditional: perceived partisan bias in the model attenuates persuasion substantially, revealing that source credibility judgments — long central to political communication research — apply to LLMs much as they do to human messengers. Together these three papers make the case that persuasion is a quantifiable, engineerable property of AI systems, but one bounded by perceived neutrality and vulnerable to a truth/persuasion trade-off.

## Transparency, disclosure, and the limits of "meaningful" trust

A second cluster asks what happens when publics are *told* about AI's role, rather than merely exposed to its outputs. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] delivers a sharp finding that reframes the regulatory debate: labeling content as AI-generated (the EU AI Act's approach) does almost nothing to blunt persuasion, whereas disclosing *persuasive intent* cuts effects roughly in half — suggesting current transparency regimes target the wrong object ("what a system is" rather than "what it is trying to do"). This resonates with the journalism-specific studies: [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds quality perceptions of AI-assisted or AI-generated news are indistinguishable from human-written news pre-disclosure, but disclosure triggers a short-lived curiosity bump rather than durable acceptance; [[Mattis2026-gu]] finds disclosure effects are uniformly negative but vary by task (fact-checking penalized most, ideation least) and are moderated by political position and AI literacy, yielding a typology of five distinct audience postures toward transparency. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends this into a comparative register, showing AI-mediated search results are trusted more than AI-produced news — suggesting trust is configuration-dependent, not a fixed property of "AI" as a category. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers the connective theoretical scaffold for this cluster, proposing that trust in generative AI must be studied at individual, institutional, and societal levels simultaneously, building on — but distinct from — legacy media-trust frameworks.

## National attitudes: ambivalence, not technophobia

Zooming out from specific interactions to general publics, [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] provide large-scale snapshots of how ordinary people talk about and use AI in Italy and the US respectively. Both find rising adoption (chatbots, voice assistants, smart devices) running ahead of self-assessed understanding, and both document context-dependent acceptance — high for security/safety uses, low for journalism, hiring, and creative work — rather than blanket enthusiasm or rejection. Fattorini's "critical ambivalence" framing and Gottfried's finding that majorities (even younger heavy users) expect net societal harm converge on the same picture: adoption and skepticism are rising together, and demographic cleavages (gender, age, education) shape both symbolic imaginaries (humanoid robots vs. chatbots) and instrumental trust. These survey-based national portraits supply the base-rate context against which the more granular experimental findings above (on persuasion, disclosure) should be read: the publics being persuaded or asked to trust AI news are, on average, exposed but not well-informed, and already primed toward wariness.

## Labor, discourse, and the sociotechnical underside

The final thread turns from audiences to workers and the discourse-producing infrastructure of AI itself. [[UnknownUnknown-db]]'s analysis of 81,000 Claude.ai users grounds the abstract question of "AI's economic impact" in worker self-report, finding that perceived displacement risk tracks observed occupational exposure, that productivity gains cluster paradoxically at both the top and bottom of the wage distribution, and that gains mostly accrue to workers themselves rather than employers — an empirically richer, more ambivalent story than either utopian or dystopian automation narratives. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] pulls back the curtain on a specific, underexamined labor category — AI red-teamers — arguing this work replicates the outsourcing, value-opacity, and psychological toll documented in earlier content-moderation scholarship, and warning against automation narratives that erase the humans currently doing this labor. [[Brown2025-jk]] examines a related but distinct labor substitution: LLMs performing human annotation work, finding that demographic bias in LLM annotation is dataset-specific and swamped by item difficulty — a more reassuring finding about a narrower question than the more encompassing displacement and red-teaming stories. Finally, [[Baym2026-tr]]'s decade-later reflection functions as a discourse-level bookend to the whole topic: it argues that the structural harms of platformized tech — wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction — have intensified under generative AI specifically, and calls for research that moves past diagnosis toward interdisciplinary intervention, a call that the empirical papers in this collection (on persuasion mechanics, disclosure design, and worker experience) can be read as partially answering.

## Synthesis

Across these fourteen papers, a coherent argument emerges: AI systems have rapidly become more persuasive and more embedded in daily informational and occupational life than public understanding, trust calibration, or regulatory design have kept pace with. The persuasion studies show *what* AI can now do rhetorically; the disclosure and trust studies show how fragile and misdirected current transparency remedies are; the national surveys show that public ambivalence is real but underinformed; and the labor and discourse papers show that the people producing, being displaced by, or laboring within AI systems are bearing costs and reaping benefits unevenly, often invisibly. The collection's throughline is that treating AI as a monolithic object of attitude ("do people like AI?") is inadequate — perception, trust, and labor experience are all configuration-specific, task-specific, and identity-specific, demanding the same granularity of empirical attention that this set of studies begins to supply.
