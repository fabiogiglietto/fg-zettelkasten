# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster traces a shared question—how ordinary people and workers actually experience, evaluate, and are reshaped by AI systems—but approaches it from strikingly different vantage points: nationally representative opinion surveys, platform-level worker self-reports, persuasion experiments, and critical political-economy essays. Read together, the papers move from *what people say they think* about AI, through *what workers report happening to their labor*, to *what critical theorists argue is being obscured* by both of those framings.

## National attitudes: ambivalence, not technophobia

Two large survey studies anchor the descriptive end of the topic. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] finds Italians expressing "critical ambivalence" toward AI—rising tool adoption alongside a widening gap between exposure and self-assessed understanding, with acceptance sharply conditional on domain (high for security, low for journalism, hiring, and art). [[Gottfried2026-ww]] documents a structurally similar pattern in the U.S.: chatbot use has become mainstream (roughly half of adults), yet views tilt negative even among heavy young users, and trust in both government regulation and corporate stewardship of AI is low and declining. Both studies converge on a counterintuitive finding—usage and skepticism rise together rather than substituting for each other—and both show attitudes fracturing along demographic lines (age, gender, education), suggesting that "public opinion on AI" is better understood as multiple context-dependent publics than a single attitude. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a conceptual scaffold for this terrain, proposing that trust in generative AI must be theorized at individual, institutional, and societal levels simultaneously, building on—but not reducible to—prior media-trust research; it functions almost as a meta-commentary on why studies like Fattorini's and Gottfried's find such heterogeneous, level-dependent results.

## Trust in AI-mediated information

A tighter sub-cluster narrows from general attitudes to the specific question of trust in AI-produced or AI-assisted content. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] shows that Swiss audiences cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, but that *disclosure* of AI involvement paradoxically boosts short-term willingness to continue reading (curiosity rather than endorsement) without changing long-term intentions. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates the disclosure story further: task-specific AI disclosures in journalism uniformly *reduce* trust, with the penalty scaling with the task's proximity to core journalistic judgment (fact-checking hit hardest), and audience reactions clustering into distinct profiles rather than a single "AI-averse public." [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] adds a comparative twist, finding Italian audiences trust AI-generated *search results* more than AI-involved *news*—suggesting that the configuration in which AI appears (intermediary vs. producer) matters as much as the fact of AI involvement itself. Together these three papers suggest that "trust in AI" is not a stable disposition but a highly contingent judgment shaped by task, disclosure framing, and institutional context—directly extending Suk's multilevel argument into empirical territory.

## Persuasion: can AI change minds, and does it matter who people think is talking?

A second empirical cluster asks whether conversational AI can shift beliefs and behavior, and under what conditions. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] delivers the most striking result: frontier AI systems out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, an advantage traced to sheer information throughput (fact density) rather than rapport, with effects extending to real charitable donations. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this into policy-relevant domains—climate, vaccination, inequality—showing that AI dialogue is especially effective at moving *skeptics*, the audience segment most resistant to conventional science communication, with trust in science as the key amplifying moderator. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] supplies an important boundary condition to this otherwise triumphant persuasion narrative: when users are told the same model is politically biased against their party, its persuasive power drops by roughly a quarter, driven by increased argumentative pushback rather than disengagement. Read as a sequence, these three papers establish that AI's persuasive capacity is real and potentially domain-general, but critically contingent on perceived neutrality—a finding that qualifies both Hackenburg's demonstration of raw persuasive superiority and the more optimistic public-communication framing in Kotz.

## Worker-reported productivity, displacement, and hidden labor

A third cluster shifts from publics-as-audiences to workers-as-subjects. The Anthropic report ([[UnknownUnknown-db]]) offers the topic's most direct worker self-report data: displacement anxiety tracks observed occupational AI exposure, productivity gains concentrate at the wage distribution's extremes, and gains typically accrue to workers themselves rather than employers—though early-career workers report both greater anxiety and lesser benefit. This large-N survey sits in productive tension with two more ethnographic accounts of AI-adjacent platform labor. [[Volpe2026-um]] examines Italian micro-influencers navigating algorithmic production demands, finding that ostensibly ethical/community-oriented content performances double as reputational labor and precarity-management strategies—a bottom-up account of how platform infrastructures (adjacent to, though not identical with, generative AI) discipline creative work. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] pushes further into infrastructure invisible to end users: AI red-teaming, reframed as sociotechnical labor bearing structural resemblance to commercial content moderation, complete with outsourcing, ill-defined harms, and psychological toll on workers. Where the Anthropic survey captures self-reported optimism about productivity, Gillespie and Volpe surface the underside of that productivity—precarious, often invisible labor whose costs are not well captured by aggregate survey metrics.

## Political economy and critique of the discourse itself

The final two papers step back to interrogate the very terms in which public and worker experience of AI gets discussed. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits a decade-old warning about social media's structural harms (wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction) and argues all four have intensified under generative AI, implicating the same platforms and actors now reshaping AI discourse. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] radicalizes this critique methodologically, arguing that AI accountability research itself risks being captured by "decoys"—ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, and regulatory framings—that redirect scholarly and public attention away from the underlying political economy of capital concentration and network power. Read against the rest of the topic, Vertesi's argument functions as a challenge to much of the survey- and experiment-based work above: attitude surveys (Fattorini, Gottfried), productivity self-reports (UnknownUnknown-db), and even persuasion experiments (Hackenburg, Kotz, DiGiuseppe) risk becoming exactly the kind of "disruption" or "ontological" decoy that displaces attention from AI's role in restructuring capital and labor markets. Baym's more measured retrospective substantiates this warning empirically, tracing how critique-generating scholarship has coexisted with, rather than checked, accelerating wealth concentration among AI infrastructure owners.

## Synthesis

Taken as a whole, the topic traces a layered picture: publics report ambivalent, context-dependent, often anxious attitudes toward AI (Fattorini, Gottfried); those attitudes are highly sensitive to framing, disclosure, and perceived neutrality rather than fixed (Gilardi, Mattis, Sbaraini-Fontes, DiGiuseppe); AI's persuasive and productive capacities appear genuinely powerful and asymmetrically distributed (Hackenburg, Kotz, UnknownUnknown-db); and this capacity is undergirded by precarious, often hidden labor (Volpe, Gillespie) operating within a political economy that critical scholars argue is being systematically obscured by the discourse used to describe it (Vertesi, Baym). The productive tension across the cluster is less about disagreement over facts than about where to locate the unit of analysis—individual attitude, worker experience, or structural power—and each paper implicitly stakes a claim about which level best explains what is actually happening to people under AI.
