# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

### Framing the field: political economy versus public opinion

Two papers in this collection set the terms for how everything else should be read. [[Baym2026-tr]] insists that the real story of platform-era technology — now inherited by generative AI — is one of accelerating wealth concentration, precarious labor, and data extraction, and that scholarly critique has largely failed to change this trajectory. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] radicalizes this diagnosis, arguing that AI is best understood not as a technology but as a "Project" — a world-building enterprise through which capitalized actors consolidate power — and that much of what passes for accountability research (debates over AI's ontology, inevitability, disruption, safety, and regulation) functions as a "decoy" that distracts from this underlying political economy. Read together, these two pieces suggest a methodological caution for the rest of the topic area: surveys of attitudes and self-reported labor experiences, however rigorously collected, risk measuring surface-level reactions to a process whose deeper drivers (capital, infrastructure, network power) remain invisible to respondents. The empirical papers below can be read as case studies that both populate and complicate this critique — showing real worker and public responses, while leaving open whether those responses target the structural forces Vertesi and Baym describe.

### National publics and the texture of ambivalence

A cluster of survey studies maps how general publics feel about AI, converging on a picture of ambivalence rather than straightforward technophobia or enthusiasm. [[Fattorini2026-bo]], tracking Italian national surveys across 2023–2024, finds a persistent gap between exposure and understanding and a context-dependent acceptance — high for security applications, low for journalism, education, and worker management — that the authors label "critical ambivalence." [[Gottfried2026-ww]]'s Pew survey of U.S. adults in 2026 echoes this: chatbot adoption has become mainstream (roughly half of adults), yet views of AI's societal and personal impact skew negative across age groups, and confidence in both government regulation and corporate responsibility is low and declining. Notably, both studies find usage and skepticism coexisting in the same populations — Italians increasingly use generative tools while fearing societal threat; young Americans are the heaviest chatbot users yet the most pessimistic. This pattern suggests that adoption is decoupled from endorsement, a theme that resonates with Baym's observation that platform harms persist even as usage deepens. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a theoretical scaffold for interpreting this ambivalence, proposing that trust in generative AI must be studied across individual, institutional, and societal levels, borrowing from — but distinct from — the mass-media and social-media trust literatures that both Fattorini and Gottfried implicitly draw upon.

### Trust, disclosure, and persuasion in AI-mediated information

A second cluster narrows from general societal attitudes to the specific question of how people evaluate AI-generated or AI-assisted content, particularly in journalism and political persuasion. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that Swiss readers cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, but that disclosure of AI involvement produces a short-term curiosity bump in engagement that does not translate into durable acceptance. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates this picture by disaggregating "AI disclosure" into specific journalistic tasks: all disclosures reduce trust, but the penalty is steepest for fact-checking and mildest for idea/image generation, with effects moderated by political position and AI literacy rather than topic — a finding that cautions against treating "AI disclosure" as a monolithic treatment. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends the trust question beyond journalism to search intermediaries, finding that Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-involved news, suggesting that the configuration in which AI appears — not just its presence — structures audience trust. Together these three studies push back against a simple "algorithmic aversion" story, revealing structured, task- and context-dependent trust judgments.

Persuasion research sharpens the stakes of this trust landscape. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] shows that frontier AI systems reliably out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the advantage traced to information throughput (fact density) rather than rapport — a capability with direct implications for the political power Vertesi and Baym worry about. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] offers a countervailing mechanism: this persuasive power is contingent on perceived neutrality, since merely telling users an LLM is politically biased measurably reduces belief correction, operating through increased argumentative pushback rather than disengagement. Read against Hackenburg's demonstration of AI's raw persuasive capacity, DiGiuseppe's finding suggests that public trust (or its absence) is not incidental but a structural lever on AI's societal influence — tying the trust-and-disclosure literature directly back to questions of democratic power.

### Worker-facing accounts: productivity, displacement, and hidden labor

Where the preceding clusters examine publics as audiences or citizens, two papers turn to workers as direct stakeholders in AI systems, with sharply different vantage points. The Anthropic-authored [[UnknownUnknown-db]] survey of 81,000 Claude users quantifies worker-reported productivity and displacement, finding that occupations with higher observed AI exposure report more displacement concern, that productivity gains cluster among the highest- and lowest-paid workers (chiefly through expanded task scope), and that gains accrue mostly to users themselves rather than employers — complicating narratives of straightforward worker replacement while still registering real anxiety, especially among early-career workers. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] inverts the frame by examining AI's hidden workforce rather than its downstream users: red-teamers who probe generative AI for harms face precarious labor arrangements, outsourcing, and psychological tolls (secondary trauma, moral injury) that closely mirror the history of commercial content moderation. Where UnknownUnknown-db measures how ordinary knowledge workers experience AI's productivity effects, Gillespie shows how an entirely different, largely invisible labor force absorbs AI's safety costs — together suggesting that "labor experiences with AI" spans both the widely surveyed user-worker and the largely unexamined ghost worker, and that self-reported productivity gains for some may be subsidized by unexamined harms to others.

### Synthesis

Across these twelve papers, a consistent tension emerges between measured public sentiment — ambivalent, task-dependent, often surprisingly stable or even skeptical despite rising adoption — and the structural forces (concentrated capital, persuasive capability, hidden labor arrangements) that Baym, Vertesi, and Gillespie argue actually govern AI's trajectory. The survey and experimental literature (Fattorini, Gottfried, Gilardi, Mattis, Sbaraini-Fontes, Suk, DiGiuseppe, Hackenburg, UnknownUnknown-db) offers increasingly fine-grained instruments for capturing attitudes, trust, and self-reported labor effects, but the political-economy papers warn that these instruments may still be measuring reactions to decoys rather than to the underlying concentration of power and risk-shifting onto precarious workers that Gillespie documents most concretely. A productive next step for this line of inquiry would be connecting the attitudinal and productivity surveys more directly to the labor-arrangement and political-economy critiques — asking not just what people believe and report, but whose interests those beliefs and reports ultimately serve.
