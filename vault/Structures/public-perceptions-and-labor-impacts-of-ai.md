# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## The persuasive power of conversational AI and its political limits

A tight cluster of large-scale experiments anchors this topic empirically, tracing an escalating argument about what makes AI persuasive and where that persuasion runs into social friction. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] establishes the baseline mechanism: across tens of thousands of conversations, persuasiveness is driven less by model scale or personalization than by post-training choices and, above all, by *information density*—the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims an AI can marshal—while the same density that persuades also erodes factual accuracy. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] pushes this claim to its logical extreme, pitting frontier AI against elite human persuaders (world-champion debaters, professional canvassers) and showing AI wins even against coached experts, with the advantage again traceable to information throughput rather than rapport; strikingly, constraining AI to human writing speed collapses its edge, pinpointing throughput, not "humanness," as the active ingredient. Together these two papers reframe AI persuasion as a question of informational bandwidth rather than charisma or data-driven targeting—directly undercutting popular fears about microtargeting-style manipulation.

[[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complicates this picture by showing that persuasion is not a context-free capability but a *socially embedded* one: merely telling participants that a chatbot is politically biased against their party cuts belief-correction by roughly a quarter, operating through increased argumentative pushback rather than disengagement. This adds a crucial caveat to the other two papers' demonstrations of raw persuasive power—AI's rhetorical advantage is contingent on perceived neutrality, situating conversational AI persuasion within the same partisan-motivated-reasoning dynamics long studied in human political communication.

## National publics and the texture of AI attitudes

A second strand moves from laboratory persuasion to population-level attitude surveys, asking what ordinary publics believe about AI as it diffuses into daily life. [[Gottfried2026-ww]]'s Pew survey of U.S. adults documents rapid adoption (half now use chatbots) alongside net-negative expectations about AI's societal and personal impact, declining trust in government regulation, and sharp demographic cleavages (age, race, gender). [[Fattorini2026-bo]] finds a structurally similar pattern in Italy—rising tool use paired with a persistent gap between exposure and self-assessed understanding—but frames the result as "critical ambivalence": domain-specific acceptance (high for security, low for journalism, art, and worker management) rather than blanket technophobia or technophilia. Both studies converge on the finding that attitudes are not simply a function of familiarity; using AI more does not straightforwardly produce optimism about it, and demographic position (gender, education, age cohort) shapes both the symbolic imagination of AI and judgments of its risk. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] extends this political-contextual logic to disinformation specifically, showing that citizens' worry about disinformation is itself shaped by electoral context—specifically the performance of far-right parties in the 2024 European Parliament elections—suggesting that concern about AI-adjacent information threats is as much a product of political environment as of individual predisposition.

## Trust, disclosure, and the information ecosystem

A third cluster narrows from general societal attitudes to the specific question of trust in AI-mediated content, particularly news. [[Suk2026-ai]] supplies the theoretical scaffolding here, arguing that trust in generative AI should be studied as a multilevel phenomenon—individual, institutional, societal—building on decades of media-trust research rather than treating AI trust as sui generis. The empirical papers in this cluster test pieces of that framework. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that Swiss readers cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, and that *disclosing* AI involvement actually boosts short-term willingness to continue reading (curiosity rather than endorsement) without changing long-run openness to AI journalism. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates the disclosure story by disaggregating it across the news value chain: Dutch respondents penalize *every* task-specific AI disclosure, but severity varies sharply by task (fact-checking hit hardest, idea/image generation least), moderated by political position and AI literacy, and the authors uncover five distinct audience profiles rather than a single uniform "AI skeptic." [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] adds a comparative-context twist, showing that Italian audiences trust AI-generated *search results* more than AI-involved *news*, implying that trust depends heavily on institutional configuration—who is understood to be using AI, and for what purpose—echoing Suk's call for level-sensitive analysis. Read together, this cluster suggests publics are not reflexively hostile to AI-mediated information but apply fine-grained, task- and context-specific standards of legitimacy that blanket "disclosure" policies risk flattening.

## Worker-reported productivity, displacement, and the hidden labor behind AI

The final strand turns from attitudes-as-audience to attitudes-as-worker, asking how people experience AI inside their own labor. [[UnknownUnknown-db]]'s Anthropic survey of 81,000 Claude users offers the most directly economic account: displacement concern tracks observed occupational AI exposure, productivity gains concentrate among high- and low-wage workers (mostly via expanded task scope rather than raw speed), and early-career workers report both greater anxiety and smaller personal benefit than senior professionals—complicating simple narratives of uniformly AI-augmented labor. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] shifts attention to the labor *producing* AI safety itself, arguing that red-teaming—framed publicly as a technical safety procedure—is actually a sociotechnical labor system with embedded value judgments, outsourcing arrangements, and serious psychological costs, explicitly drawing the parallel to earlier content-moderation ghost work; its warning that automation narratives obscure rather than resolve these labor conditions functions as a pointed counterpoint to the productivity optimism reported in the Anthropic survey. [[Cotter2019-js]], examining tattoo artists' entanglement with Instagram's algorithmic visibility regime, generalizes this labor concern beyond AI firms narrowly construed: platform/algorithmic logics reorganize even professions with no intrinsic technological dependency, generating unpaid labor, workspace transformation, and precarization as workers "play the platform's game." Taken together, this final cluster insists that the "public" encountering AI is not only an audience forming opinions but also a labor force whose working conditions, incentives, and anxieties are being reshaped—often invisibly—by the same systems that the attitude surveys above ask people to evaluate from the outside.
