# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Persuasion at the frontier: mechanisms, limits, and contingencies

A tight cluster of large-scale experiments anchors this topic in the empirics of AI persuasion. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] establishes the baseline architecture of the question, decomposing "what makes conversational AI persuasive" into scale, post-training, prompting, personalization, and interactivity, and lands on information density — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims a system can produce — as the dominant mechanism, at the cost of a persuasion–accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] pushes this finding to its logical extreme by pitting AI against the best human persuaders available (elite debaters, professional canvassers) and showing that AI's edge collapses to zero once it is throttled to human typing speed and message length, confirming that throughput of claims, not rapport or humanness, is doing the work. Together these two papers convert "AI persuasion" from a diffuse worry into a mechanistically specified phenomenon: it is fundamentally an information-volume effect, not a charisma or manipulation effect, and it generalizes from stated attitudes to real donations.

[[Kotz2026-lk]] and [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] then interrogate the boundary conditions of this effect. Kotz shows the mechanism travels across issue domains (climate, vaccines, inequality) and is strongest precisely among skeptics — the audience segment traditional science communication struggles to reach — with trust in science as the key amplifier. DiGiuseppe complicates the picture by showing that the persuasive power documented in the other studies is not source-independent: a brief cue that the model is "biased against your party" cuts belief correction by a quarter, operating through increased argumentative pushback rather than disengagement. Read together, this quartet traces an arc from "how persuasive can AI be, mechanically" (Hackenburg2025-dj, Hackenburg2026-ud) to "for whom, and under what perceived-neutrality conditions" (Kotz2026-lk, DiGiuseppe2026-pu) — moving the discourse from capability demonstration toward the political-psychological contingencies that will govern real-world deployment.

## National publics and discourse formation

A second thread zooms out from individual persuasion episodes to how populations and public discourse form attitudes toward AI as a category. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] offer cross-nationally comparable survey snapshots — Italy and the US respectively — that converge on a similar structure of "critical ambivalence": rising adoption of chatbots and AI-enabled devices coexists with low self-assessed understanding, domain-specific rather than blanket acceptance (security applications welcomed, journalism/employment decisions distrusted), and demographic fault lines around age, gender, and education. Gottfried's Pew data extends this into an American political-trust register, documenting collapsing confidence in both government regulation and corporate stewardship of AI, while Fattorini frames the Italian pattern through a "social conversation" lens in which dissemination, dialogue, and participation coexist unevenly across social groups. Neither paper is naive technological determinism nor technophobia — both insist attitudes are contingent, contextual, and still forming.

[[Richter2026-bt]] supplies the missing discourse-formation layer behind these survey snapshots: who actually shaped the public conversation about AI before the generative-AI boom. Its longitudinal Twitter analysis of the US and Germany (2012–2021) shows starkly divergent institutional trajectories — German discourse institutionalizing around academia, advocacy, and government, American discourse individualizing around industry and tech influencers — and argues these early discourse regimes help explain the differing regulatory and public-opinion landscapes captured later in papers like Gottfried2026-ww. This paper thus functions as a genealogical complement to the attitude surveys: it asks not what people currently believe about AI, but how the informational and institutional environment that produces those beliefs was itself constructed.

## Trust, disclosure, and AI-generated content

A third cluster narrows from general AI discourse to the specific question of trust in AI-generated media, primarily in journalism. [[Suk2026-ai]] is the theoretical anchor here, proposing a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework for trust in generative AI drawn from media-trust scholarship, explicitly positioned to organize the empirical work surrounding it. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] supply two complementary empirical tests that sit somewhat in tension. Gilardi finds that undisclosed AI-generated and AI-assisted news is rated no differently from human-written news on quality dimensions, and that disclosure actually produces a short-term *bump* in willingness to continue reading — interpreted as curiosity rather than endorsement, since it does not translate into greater willingness to read AI news in the future. Mattis, by contrast, finds that task-specific AI disclosures *consistently reduce* trustworthiness, with the penalty scaling by journalistic task (harshest for fact-checking, mildest for idea/image generation) and moderated by political position and AI literacy rather than topic. The apparent tension — Gilardi's engagement bump versus Mattis's trust penalty — is instructive rather than contradictory: it suggests disclosure can simultaneously pique curiosity (behavioral engagement) and erode credibility (evaluative trust), and that the two outcomes must be measured separately, exactly the kind of multilevel, disaggregated approach Suk2026-ai calls for.

## Workers' experience: labor behind and displaced by AI

The final thread turns from publics-as-audience to publics-as-workers, examining AI's material effects on labor from two very different vantage points. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], Anthropic's survey of ~81,000 Claude users, quantifies self-reported productivity and displacement concerns and ties them back to observed occupational exposure: workers in high-exposure occupations report more displacement anxiety, gains concentrate among high- and low-wage earners via expanded task scope, and early-career workers are both more anxious and less likely to report personal benefit than senior peers. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] inverts the lens, examining not the workers *using* AI but the often-invisible workers *producing its safety* — red-teamers — arguing that this labor, like content moderation before it, is sociotechnically loaded: opaque, outsourced, psychologically taxing, and shaped by value judgments embedded by AI firms rather than by the diverse publics affected by AI harms. Read side by side, these two papers bookend the labor question raised implicitly throughout the topic: UnknownUnknown-db documents how ordinary workers experience AI's productivity and displacement effects from outside the lab, while Gillespie2026-aa documents how a specific, precarious workforce experiences AI from inside its production pipeline — together suggesting that "worker-reported effects of AI" cannot be reduced to task-automation statistics but must include the hidden human infrastructure (red-teaming, moderation-like labor) that makes today's AI systems deployable at all.
