# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Structure Note

This cluster brings together survey- and discourse-based work on how AI is perceived, discussed, and lived with — by publics, workers, journalists, and audiences. Read together, these papers trace a movement from *macro-discourse* (who talks about AI and how) to *micro-attitudes* (what individuals believe and feel) to *lived labor experience* (what workers actually report doing with AI). The throughline is that AI's social meaning is not fixed by the technology itself but is actively constructed, contested, and unevenly distributed across actors, institutions, and national contexts.

### Who Shapes the Discourse

Two papers ask who gets to narrate AI to the public. [[Richter2026-bt]] traces a decade of Twitter discourse (2012–2021) before the generative AI boom, finding that the US discourse consolidated around industry and, later, individual tech influencers, while Germany institutionalized around academia, advocacy, and government — a divergence the authors argue prefigures each country's distinct regulatory posture toward GenAI. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] picks up roughly where this leaves off, examining post-ChatGPT news coverage across 15 global outlets and finding a similar structural asymmetry: a dominant techno-capitalist master frame, heavily concentrated on OpenAI's products, with editorial and organizational style — rather than region — driving variation in whether LLMs are anthropomorphized as agentic minds. Together these papers suggest that the "who" of AI discourse (concentrated industry voices, influencer economies, outlet-specific editorial cultures) matters as much as the "what," shaping the sociotechnical imaginaries publics inherit before they ever interact with the technology themselves.

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] extends this discourse-and-power lens backstage, into the infrastructure of AI safety itself. Red-teaming, the authors argue, is being publicly narrated as a purely technical safeguard, obscuring the value judgments, outsourced labor arrangements, and psychological harms it entails — a trajectory they explicitly liken to the earlier normalization and concealment of commercial content moderation labor. This paper is a hinge in the topic: it shows that "AI discourse" is not only about how the technology is talked about, but about what remains *unsaid* regarding the human labor propping up its apparent safety and coherence.

### National and Public Attitudes

A second cluster surveys general-population attitudes directly. [[Gottfried2026-ww]]'s 2026 Pew survey of US adults finds rapid adoption of chatbots alongside deepening pessimism — more Americans expect harm than benefit, trust in both government regulation and corporate self-governance is eroding, and skepticism is notably concentrated among younger, heavier users, a seeming paradox the report treats as a central puzzle. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] finds a structurally similar pattern in Italy: rising AI tool use co-occurs with rising perception of AI as a societal threat, producing what the authors term "critical ambivalence" rather than technophobia — support is domain-contingent, high for security applications and low for journalism, art, and worker management. Both papers converge on a key finding that cuts against simple diffusion-of-innovation narratives: familiarity and use do not straightforwardly produce comfort or trust, and gender, age, and education continue to structure risk perception in patterned ways (women and less-educated respondents more cautious in both contexts).

[[Suk2026-ai]] offers a theoretical scaffold for this empirical terrain, proposing that "trust in generative AI" needs a multilevel framework analogous to decades of media-trust research — individual, institutional, and societal levels — rather than being treated as a single undifferentiated attitude. This paper functions as connective tissue for the topic: it explains why [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] find such context-dependent, seemingly contradictory attitudes (adoption up, trust down; use high, concern high) — because these are not contradictions within one construct but effects operating at different levels simultaneously.

### Persuasion, Trust, and the Limits of AI Credibility

A tightly linked pair of experiments interrogates AI's persuasive power and its conditions. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] demonstrates that frontier AI systems reliably out-persuade even elite, incentivized human persuaders (world debaters, professional canvassers) on political attitudes and real-money charitable behavior, locating the mechanism in sheer information throughput — fact density — rather than rapport or human-likeness. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complicates this picture: AI's persuasive edge is not unconditional but contingent on perceived neutrality. A brief cue that the model is politically biased against one's party cuts belief correction by roughly a quarter, operating not through disengagement but through increased argumentative pushback — a directional-motivated-reasoning account rather than a heuristic-discounting one. Read together, these two papers suggest that AI persuasion is powerful but *politically fragile*: its advantage rests on perceived credibility, and any signal — accurate or not — that threatens that perceived neutrality can neutralize much of the gain documented in idealized experimental settings.

[[Gilardi2026-hw]] extends the credibility question to a specific applied domain, AI journalism. Swiss readers rate AI-generated, AI-assisted, and human-written news as statistically equivalent in quality when authorship is undisclosed, and disclosure actually *boosts* short-term willingness to continue reading — but this curiosity effect does not translate into durable acceptance of AI-authored news going forward. This dovetails with [[Nguyen2026-vm]]'s finding that journalistic anthropomorphism of AI is not monolithically credulous; audiences and journalists alike seem to hold AI content and AI claims at an evaluative arm's length, curious but unconvinced, echoing the "critical ambivalence" documented in the national-attitudes surveys.

### Worker-Reported Experience

Finally, [[UnknownUnknown-db]] shifts from attitudes-in-the-abstract to lived labor experience, analyzing ~81,000 Claude.ai user survey responses alongside Anthropic's usage-based Economic Index. It finds that displacement concern tracks observed occupational AI exposure, that early-career workers are disproportionately anxious, and that productivity gains — mostly captured by users themselves rather than employers — accrue most strongly at the high and low ends of the wage distribution, largely through task-scope expansion rather than pure speed. This paper is the topic's clearest empirical bridge between discourse/attitude research and material stakes: it shows that the ambivalence documented in national surveys ([[Gottfried2026-ww]], [[Fattorini2026-bo]]) is not free-floating anxiety but is calibrated, among actual users, to real variation in exposure and career vulnerability — and it implicitly echoes [[Gillespie2026-aa]]'s concern that whoever defines and measures "impact" (here, Anthropic itself, via its own classifiers) shapes what story gets told about AI's costs and benefits to labor.

### Synthesis

Across this topic, three tensions recur: (1) between industry-driven, techno-capitalist framings of AI and the more critical, ambivalent, or fragmented ways publics and journalists actually receive them; (2) between AI's demonstrated technical capacities (persuasion, productivity) and the social conditions — perceived neutrality, disclosure, trust, labor visibility — that determine whether those capacities translate into acceptance or backlash; and (3) between what gets counted and publicized (usage statistics, national polls, news coverage) and what remains structurally hidden (red-teaming labor, the provenance of "productivity" self-reports, the influencer economies driving discourse). Read as a whole, the papers argue against any simple deficit model of public AI attitudes: skepticism, ambivalence, and selective trust are not failures of understanding to be corrected by more exposure, but reasoned responses to a discourse environment, labor system, and persuasive technology whose value commitments remain largely opaque and unevenly distributed.
