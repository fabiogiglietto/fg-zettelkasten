# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Public sentiment: ambivalence, exposure, and trust

The topic opens with a set of survey snapshots that triangulate how ordinary publics are making sense of AI as it becomes ambient. [[Fattorini2026-bo]]'s two-wave Italian survey captures what its authors call "critical ambivalence": rapid growth in chatbot and smart-device use runs alongside a persistent gap between exposure and self-assessed understanding, and acceptance is sharply domain-dependent (security applications welcomed, journalism and worker-management applications distrusted). [[Gottfried2026-ww]]'s Pew report on the US finds a structurally similar pattern—chatbot adoption now near half of adults, yet views tilting negative even among heavy-using younger cohorts, and confidence in government's capacity to regulate AI falling further. Together these two national surveys suggest that use and trust are decoupling: adoption is outpacing, not resolving, public unease, and demographic cleavages (gender, age, education) shape the texture of concern in both countries. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a conceptual scaffold for this empirical terrain, arguing that trust in generative AI needs a multilevel framework borrowed from—but not reducible to—decades of mass-media and social-media trust research; it is less an empirical finding than a lens for organizing the individual-, institutional-, and societal-level dynamics that [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] document empirically. [[Baym2026-tr]] supplies the critical macro-frame against which these survey findings should be read: a decade after warning about wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, and data extraction, Baym argues all four have intensified, with generative AI now folding user-generated content into new extraction pipelines. Read alongside the national surveys, her essay suggests that public ambivalence is not free-floating anxiety but a reasonably calibrated response to concrete shifts in power and labor conditions that the survey instruments only partially capture.

## Transparency, disclosure, and trust in AI-mediated information

A tighter cluster of experiments interrogates one specific lever thought to remedy public distrust—disclosure—and largely complicates its promise. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that Swiss readers cannot distinguish AI-assisted, AI-generated, and human-written news on quality grounds when authorship is concealed, but that revealing AI involvement produces a short-lived curiosity bump in willingness to continue reading rather than durable acceptance. [[Mattis2026-gu]] pushes further by disaggregating "AI disclosure" into seven task-specific journalistic functions, showing that trust penalties are not uniform: fact-checking incurs the steepest penalty, idea/image generation the mildest, and the effect is moderated by political position and AI literacy rather than topic—implying that generic AI labels, as used in most prior work including [[Gilardi2026-hw]], obscure meaningful variation. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends the disclosure question outward from newsrooms to the wider information ecosystem, finding that Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-involved news, which hints that the locus of AI mediation—search intermediary versus publisher—matters as much as the fact of AI involvement itself. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] then reframes what "meaningful transparency" should even mean in a directly persuasive (rather than informational) context: an EU-AI-Act-style identity label does essentially nothing to blunt a chatbot's persuasive power, but disclosing its persuasive *intent* roughly halves the effect—suggesting that the relevant regulatory target is not "what the system is" but "what it is trying to do." Across this cluster, a shared finding recurs: labeling something as AI-produced rarely changes perceived quality but reliably changes engagement or persuasion through second-order psychological mechanisms (curiosity, reactance, persuasion knowledge) rather than through any recalibration of the content's actual merits.

## The mechanics of AI persuasion—and how to blunt it

A parallel, more experimentally granular thread asks not how people react to AI-mediated content in the abstract, but how persuasive AI actually is and why. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] establishes the baseline mechanics across tens of thousands of conversations: post-training and prompting strategy, not model scale or personalization, drive persuasiveness, with information density—the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims—as the operative mechanism, alongside a troubling accuracy trade-off (the same levers that persuade also degrade factual accuracy). [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the comparison by pitting AI against expert human persuaders—elite debaters, professional canvassers—and finds AI reliably wins even against coached experts, with the gap collapsing only when AI is artificially constrained to human throughput, corroborating fact density (rather than rapport or perceived humanness) as the decisive lever, and showing the advantage extends to real-money charitable behavior, not just stated attitudes. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] then identify two ways this persuasive power can be constrained without touching the underlying information-density mechanism: perceived partisan bias in the model (DiGiuseppe) and disclosed persuasive intent (Rauchfleisch) both suppress persuasion—the former by provoking more argumentative pushback consistent with motivated reasoning, the latter by activating persuasion-knowledge defenses—while mere identity disclosure or the model's actual truthfulness do not. Read together, these four papers sketch an emerging causal model of AI persuasion: capability is real and exceeds elite human capability, its mechanism is informational throughput rather than charisma, and its two most effective governance levers—perceived neutrality and intent transparency—are social-psychological rather than purely factual, mirroring the trust-and-disclosure dynamics found in the journalism cluster above.

## The hidden labor of making AI safe and legible

A third strand shifts from AI's outputs to the human labor that shapes and constrains those outputs behind the scenes. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] both examine red-teaming as a sociotechnical practice rather than a purely technical safety procedure. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] draws an explicit historical parallel to content moderation, arguing that red-teaming inherits the same pattern of internally defined harm categories, outsourced and precarious labor, and psychological toll (secondary trauma, moral injury) on workers whose adversarial imagination is treated as infrastructure rather than labor requiring protection. [[Unknown2025-qj]]'s interview-based study of public red-teaming events complements this by showing empirically how institutional framing shapes what counts as a "harm" worth surfacing, suggesting that red-teaming's public-interest potential is real but contingent on context and participant diversity—concerns [[Gillespie2026-aa]] raises more programmatically about who gets to define AI safety values. [[Brown2025-jk]] sits adjacent to this labor question from a different angle: rather than studying red-teamers, it studies whether LLMs used as annotation labor (replacing or supplementing human crowdworkers on contentious classification tasks) introduce systematic demographic bias. Its finding that item difficulty (human disagreement) predicts LLM-human agreement far better than annotator demographics offers a partial reassurance about one specific automation of subjective judgment labor, even as [[Gillespie2026-aa]] cautions that claims about automating away red-teaming labor obscure rather than resolve the underlying labor and governance problems. Together this trio insists that "the AI" in public discourse about safety and fairness is inseparable from the often-invisible human workforce—crowdworkers, red-teamers, annotators—whose judgments and well-being are at stake.

## Worker-reported productivity and displacement

Finally, [[UnknownUnknown-db]] turns from the labor of building/safeguarding AI to the labor AI is deployed *upon*, reporting Anthropic's survey of roughly 81,000 Claude.ai users linked to the company's occupational usage data. Its central finding—that self-reported displacement anxiety tracks observed occupational AI exposure, while productivity gains are concentrated among both the highest- and lowest-paid workers and accrue mostly to the workers themselves rather than employers—offers a rare worker-side empirical counterpart to the more speculative displacement narratives found in the national attitude surveys ([[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]]) and to Baym's structural critique of precarious labor under platform capitalism. Its U-shaped relationship between speedup and perceived threat, and its finding that early-career workers report both more displacement anxiety and less personal benefit than senior professionals, echoes broader anxieties about generational inequality that run through this topic's public-opinion literature, while also giving those anxieties a more granular, occupation-specific empirical footing than survey-based attitude measures alone can provide. Read as a capstone, this paper closes the arc opened by [[Baym2026-tr]]: where Baym warns in general terms that AI's data and power dynamics are worsening precarity, [[UnknownUnknown-db]] supplies worker-reported micro-evidence of exactly where those gains and anxieties are landing—unevenly, and often self-reported as personally rather than institutionally captured.
