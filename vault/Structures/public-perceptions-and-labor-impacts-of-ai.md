# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Public Ambivalence, Not Technophobia

Two large-scale national surveys anchor the empirical picture of how ordinary people feel about AI. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] tracks Italian attitudes across 2023–2024 and finds a public caught between rising familiarity and persistent non-understanding: use of chatbots and smart devices roughly doubles year over year, yet self-reported comprehension barely improves, and perceptions of AI as a societal threat actually *rise* alongside adoption. The authors coin "critical ambivalence" to capture this pattern — support is high for safety applications but collapses for journalism, art, and worker management, suggesting Italians are discriminating between AI's institutional roles rather than reacting to AI as a monolith. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] finds a structurally similar story in the US a year later: chatbot use has become mainstream (about half of adults, roughly a quarter daily), yet net sentiment tilts negative even among the heaviest-using cohort (under-30s), and trust in both government regulation and corporate stewardship is falling. Read together, these two surveys undercut any simple "familiarity breeds acceptance" story — usage and unease are rising in tandem, and the gap between exposure and comprehension identified by Fattorini reappears in the US data as an "adoption/skepticism" paradox that cuts across age, gender, and partisan lines.

## Who Gets to Narrate AI, and Whom the Public Trusts

If public ambivalence is the outcome, [[Richter2026-bt]] offers a genealogy of the discourse that shaped it. Its longitudinal analysis of Twitter/X stakeholders (2012–2021) shows starkly diverging national trajectories predating the GenAI boom: German AI discourse institutionalized around academia, advocacy, and government, while US discourse became increasingly dominated first by industry and then by individual "tech influencers." This divergence plausibly conditions the very attitudes measured by Fattorini and Gottfried — a discourse mediated by accountable institutions versus one mediated by charismatic individuals invites different baseline levels of trust. [[Suk2026-ai]] picks up trust as an explicit theoretical object, proposing that trust in generative AI needs a multilevel framework (individual, institutional, societal) borrowed from decades of mass- and social-media trust research. This is a useful hinge for the topic cluster: it reframes the survey findings above not as isolated national snapshots but as data points for a nascent theory of AI as a new "information intermediary," inheriting — and complicating — the credibility problems long studied in media trust scholarship.

## Persuasion, Bias, and the Fragility of Trust in Interaction

A second thread examines what happens when publics don't just read about AI but talk *with* it. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] demonstrates that frontier conversational AI reliably out-persuades even elite human persuaders — professional canvassers, world-champion debaters — on political attitudes and real-money donation behavior, an effect traced to AI's superior information throughput (fact density) rather than rapport or perceived humanness. This raises the stakes for the trust questions above: if AI is more persuasive than trained humans, then the perceived neutrality of the messenger becomes consequential. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows exactly this mechanism in action — brief cues that a chatbot is politically biased against one's party reduce belief correction by roughly a quarter, not because users disengage but because they argue back more, consistent with motivated reasoning rather than simple heuristic discounting. Together these two papers suggest AI persuasion is powerful but contingent: it depends on maintained perceptions of neutrality, which are exactly what surveys like Gottfried's show eroding. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] extends this logic to AI-generated content more broadly: when readers don't know a news article is AI-authored, they rate it as equal in quality to human writing, but *disclosure* of AI involvement produces a short-term curiosity bump in engagement that fails to translate into durable willingness to consume AI journalism — a finding that dovetails with DiGiuseppe's point that source-label effects, not content effects, drive much of the public's reaction to AI-mediated communication.

## Worker-Reported Productivity, Displacement, and the Hidden Labor Behind the Systems

The final thread turns from publics-as-audiences to workers-as-users-and-laborers. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], Anthropic's survey of 81,000 Claude users, complements the topic's discourse-analytic and experimental work with self-reported labor-market data: displacement concern tracks observed occupational AI exposure, productivity gains cluster at both the highest- and lowest-paid occupations (largely via expanded task scope rather than raw speed), and early-career workers report both greater anxiety and smaller personal benefit than senior colleagues — a distributional asymmetry that echoes the demographic splits Gottfried and Fattorini find in attitudes (skepticism concentrated among the young and precarious). Two papers push further into the *hidden* labor sustaining these systems and their surrounding platform economy. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] argues that AI red-teaming — the human labor of adversarially probing models — replicates the outsourcing, opaque value judgments, and psychological toll of earlier content-moderation labor, and warns that framing this work as soon-to-be-automated obscures urgent worker-welfare and governance questions. [[Volpe2026-um]], while ostensibly about second-hand fashion influencers rather than AI per se, belongs here as a structurally parallel case of platform-mediated precarity: creators perform care and ethics as survival strategy under algorithmic production demands, using visibility as reputational capital toward more stable work — the same logic of algorithmically-conditioned self-presentation and precarious "stepping stone" labor that undergirds gig and crowd work feeding AI systems (data labeling, red-teaming) elsewhere in this cluster.

## The Arc

Across these ten papers, a coherent arc emerges: public discourse about AI is shaped early and unevenly by a narrow set of stakeholders ([[Richter2026-bt]]), producing national publics whose attitudes are ambivalent rather than uniformly fearful or credulous ([[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]]) and whose trust is theoretically under-specified but empirically fragile — persuasion and quality judgments hinge heavily on perceived neutrality and disclosure ([[Hackenburg2026-ud]], [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], [[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Suk2026-ai]]). Beneath these public-facing dynamics lies a labor substrate — displaced or augmented knowledge workers, precarious red-teamers, and platform-dependent content creators — whose lived experience of productivity, precarity, and psychological cost ([[UnknownUnknown-db]], [[Gillespie2026-aa]], [[Volpe2026-um]]) is the material reality that public attitudes are, ultimately, reacting to and rationalizing.
