# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## Mapping the Terrain: Attitudes, Trust, and Labor under AI

This cluster gathers survey- and discourse-based work on how ordinary people, workers, and institutional actors are experiencing the rapid diffusion of generative AI and algorithmic platforms. Read together, the papers trace three interlocking lines of inquiry: (1) what publics in different national contexts believe and feel about AI; (2) how AI and platform logics are reshaping work, productivity, and precarity; and (3) how trust, disclosure, and persuasion operate in AI-mediated communication. A fourth, more reflexive strand asks what all this means for the research and governance infrastructures meant to study and manage these systems.

### National Publics and the Shape of AI Attitudes

Several papers offer large-sample, nationally representative snapshots of public opinion. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] characterizes Italian attitudes as "critical ambivalence"—a persistent gap between rising AI exposure and self-assessed understanding, with support highly context-dependent (strong for security applications, weak for journalism, art, and worker management) and structured by age, gender, and education. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] finds a comparable ambivalence in the U.S.: chatbot adoption has surged to roughly half of adults, yet views of AI's societal and personal impact tilt negative across nearly all demographic groups, including heavy-using younger adults, alongside declining trust in both government regulation and corporate self-governance. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] complicates the picture by showing that trust is not a single undifferentiated attitude toward "AI" but varies by institutional configuration—Italians trust AI-infused search results more than AI-produced news, suggesting that search intermediaries are quietly accruing epistemic authority. [[Suk2026-ai]] supplies the conceptual scaffolding these empirical findings call for, proposing a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework adapted from mass- and social-media trust research to make sense of why trust in generative AI behaves differently from—yet is continuous with—trust in earlier information intermediaries.

### Worker-Reported Productivity, Displacement, and Platform Precarity

A second thread moves from general publics to workers directly exposed to AI and platform systems. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], Anthropic's large-scale Claude.ai user survey, provides the most direct worker self-report on productivity and displacement: concern about job loss tracks occupational AI exposure, productivity gains cluster at the high and low ends of the wage distribution (mostly via expanded task scope rather than pure speed), and early-career workers report both less benefit and more anxiety than senior colleagues—evidence that AI's labor effects are unevenly distributed well before formal policy catches up. This economic snapshot resonates with earlier platform-labor ethnographies that, while predating the generative-AI moment, anticipate its dynamics structurally. [[Cotter2019-js]] shows how Instagram's algorithmic performativity "platformizes" even a pre-digital craft (tattooing), generating dependence, unpaid algorithmic labor, and a blurring of work and self-promotion. [[Volpe2026-um]] extends this logic to micro-influencers, who perform care and ethical commitment as reputational labor that doubles as survival strategy against algorithmic and economic precarity. Read against [[UnknownUnknown-db]]'s productivity optimism, these papers supply the texture of displacement and adaptation that aggregate survey statistics can only gesture toward—turning "productivity gain" into lived, often precarious, self-branding work.

### Persuasion, Disclosure, and the Limits of AI Trust

A tightly linked set of preregistered experiments investigates how perceived neutrality, source identity, and disclosed intent shape AI's persuasive and credibility effects—effectively asking what "meaningful transparency" means in practice. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that merely priming users to believe an LLM is politically biased against their party cuts belief-correction by roughly a quarter, operating through increased counterarguing rather than disengagement. [[Kotz2026-lk]] demonstrates the flip side: evidence-based AI dialogues can shift beliefs and even contested policy support across ideologically fraught domains (climate, vaccination, inequality), with effects concentrated among skeptics and amplified by trust in science—suggesting persuasion is not simply blocked by skepticism but is channel-dependent. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] turn to disclosure design specifically: identity labels (e.g., EU AI Act–style "this is AI" notices) do little to blunt persuasion or trust penalties, whereas disclosing persuasive *intent* roughly halves attitude change, and task-specific journalistic AI disclosures (fact-checking especially) incur the steepest trust costs. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] adds a temporal nuance: disclosure of AI authorship produces a short-term curiosity bump in willingness to keep reading without durable endorsement, implying that audience reactions to AI-authored content are more about novelty-driven engagement than settled acceptance. Together these studies converge on a shared finding across very different publics (German-Swiss, Dutch, UK, US): transparency about *what* a system is does less work than transparency about *what it is trying to do*, and audience trust is segmented, task-specific, and politically contingent rather than a single dial.

### The Reflexive Undercurrent: Infrastructures of Critique and Care

Finally, three essays step back to interrogate the institutions and scholarly practices surrounding AI and platforms themselves. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits a decade-old diagnosis of social media's harms—wealth concentration, opaque algorithms, precarious creative labor, data extraction—and argues generative AI has intensified rather than resolved them, urging researchers toward collaborative intervention over critique alone. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] extends this ethos to AI red-teaming, reframing it as a sociotechnical labor practice structurally reminiscent of early content moderation, complete with outsourced, psychologically taxing "ghost work" and opaque value judgments about harm. [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the empirical fallout of exactly the retrenchment Baym anticipates: the professional field of trust and safety is destabilizing under political and economic pressure, with direct consequences for the online safety infrastructures that both AI companies and publics depend on. Read as a coda to the attitudes-and-labor literature above, these pieces insist that public perception and worker experience of AI cannot be separated from the (increasingly precarious) human labor and institutional arrangements producing the systems being perceived and used.
