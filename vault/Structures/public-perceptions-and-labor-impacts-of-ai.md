# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster of papers traces a single underlying question from several angles: as AI systems move from laboratory curiosities to everyday infrastructure, how do publics come to perceive, trust, and labor within them — and how do those perceptions diverge from what is actually happening inside the systems? The papers split naturally into three movements: (1) large-scale surveys of national publics and workers that map *what people believe and experience*, (2) experimental studies of *how trust and persuasion are produced* in AI-mediated communication, and (3) sociological accounts of the *hidden human labor* that makes AI systems function at all — work that rarely appears in the attitude surveys but that shapes the systems those surveys are about.

## National attitudes: ambivalence, not technophobia

[[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] form a natural comparative pair, applying similar survey instruments (Pew in the US, Observa in Italy) to track rapidly shifting public sentiment. Both find adoption outpacing understanding: chatbot use has become mainstream (roughly half of US adults, sharply rising in Italy), yet most respondents report feeling only vaguely informed, and both populations have grown *more* pessimistic about AI's societal impact over successive survey waves rather than habituating to it. Fattorini's framing of "critical ambivalence" — usefulness recognized in narrow, safety-adjacent domains but resisted in domains touching labor, journalism, and creativity — resonates with Gottfried's finding that Americans under 30, despite being heavy users, are also the most pessimistic age cohort. Together these studies undercut a simple exposure-reduces-fear model and instead suggest that familiarity with AI tools and anxiety about AI's institutional and economic consequences are developing in parallel, not as substitutes.

## Worker-reported productivity and displacement

[[UnknownUnknown-db]] shifts the lens from general publics to actual users-as-workers, pairing Anthropic's Claude usage data with 81,000 open-ended self-reports. It operationalizes exactly the ambivalence the national surveys gesture toward: workers in high-AI-exposure occupations report more displacement anxiety, yet the same high-wage, high-exposure workers (alongside the lowest-wage ones) report the largest productivity gains, mostly from *expanded task scope* rather than raw speed. The U-shaped relationship between perceived speedup and job-threat concern is a notable finding — threat is reported by those AI barely helps *and* by those it transforms most. This paper is the topic's closest approximation of a "ground truth" labor study, and it productively complicates the more impressionistic national attitude data by showing that concern is not simply a function of ignorance (as Fattorini's "exposure gap" might imply) but tracks fairly precisely with real occupational exposure.

## Trust, disclosure, and persuasion in AI-mediated information

A cluster of papers examines how trust in AI is constructed discursively and experimentally, especially in the journalism and political-communication context. [[Suk2026-ai]] supplies the connective theoretical scaffolding here, proposing that trust in generative AI should be studied the way communication scholars studied trust in mass and social media — across individual, institutional, and societal levels — explicitly positioning generative AI as a new "information intermediary" rather than a wholly novel object. The empirical papers test pieces of that framework. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] both interrogate the effect of *disclosure* on perceived trustworthiness of AI-assisted journalism, but reach apparently discordant results that are actually complementary once task-specificity is taken seriously: Gilardi finds no quality penalty pre-disclosure and even a short-term "curiosity bump" in willingness to continue reading post-disclosure, whereas Mattis's conjoint design — which varies disclosure by specific journalistic task (idea generation vs. fact-checking vs. full drafting) — finds a *consistent* trust penalty whose magnitude scales with how consequential the disclosed task is. Read together, they suggest that blunt AI-vs-human framings (as in Gilardi's conditions) understate the penalty that more granular, task-aware disclosures (as in Mattis) reveal, and that any post-disclosure "engagement" is more likely novelty-seeking than genuine endorsement. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends this logic laterally, showing that trust is not a single scalar attached to "AI" in general but varies by *configuration* — Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-assisted news from media organizations, implying that institutional framing (search intermediary vs. newsroom) matters independently of the underlying technology.

## Persuasion as a political and relational phenomenon

[[Hackenburg2026-ud]] and [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] push the trust-and-communication thread toward a harder question: not just whether people trust AI output, but whether AI can change minds, and under what conditions that capacity is blunted. Hackenburg's finding that frontier LLMs out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers — through sheer information throughput rather than rapport — establishes a strong baseline claim about AI's persuasive power that is politically consequential on its own terms. DiGiuseppe's study complicates it usefully: that same persuasive capacity is not fixed but socially and politically contingent, since a brief cue suggesting the model is "biased against your party" measurably dampens belief correction, operating through increased partisan argumentation rather than disengagement. Read against each other, these two papers suggest AI persuasion operates near the ceiling of human capability in a depoliticized context, but that perceived neutrality is a lever nearly as important as fact density — meaning the discourse *around* an AI system (is it biased? whose interests does it serve?) can materially alter what the system can do to its users, a finding that dovetails with the trust-in-intermediaries work above.

## The hidden labor sustaining AI systems

A final set of papers turns from AI's audience to its workforce, documenting the human labor obscured behind supposedly automated or algorithmically governed systems. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes the broadest conceptual move, arguing that AI red-teaming — nominally a technical safety procedure — is better understood as a sociotechnical labor practice structurally similar to commercial content moderation: outsourced, psychologically taxing, and shaped by value judgments made opaquely inside AI companies rather than by affected publics. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] supplies an empirical complement from the adjacent field of content moderation proper, using DSA transparency data to show that the labor of moderation is itself unevenly and inequitably distributed across languages, with Global South languages chronically under-resourced relative to English — a finding that substantiates Gillespie's worry that internally defined, insufficiently diverse labor forces cannot adequately represent global harms. [[Cotter2019-js]], though focused on a pre-generative-AI platform (Instagram) and an unrelated occupation (tattoo artistry), extends the same logic of platformized, largely unpaid, algorithmically disciplined labor to independent workers outside the tech industry, reinforcing a throughline across this sub-cluster: platforms and AI systems alike reorganize work in ways that are substantially invisible to the public-facing discourse (and surveys) that dominate the rest of this topic's literature.

## Synthesis

Taken as a whole, the topic maps a loop: publics form attitudes about AI (Gottfried, Fattorini) that are shaped by discourse and disclosure practices (Suk, Gilardi, Mattis, Sbaraini-Fontes) and by AI's own persuasive capacities (Hackenburg, DiGiuseppe), while workers both inside AI companies (Gillespie, Tonneau) and in AI-adjacent gig/platform economies (Cotter) absorb costs and labor that rarely surface in those same public-facing attitude surveys — except obliquely, in self-reports of productivity and displacement (Anthropic/UnknownUnknown-db). The open tension running through the set is between a public discourse that frames AI's effects in terms of trust, bias, and quality perception, and a labor reality — red-teaming trauma, underpaid content moderation, platform-driven unpaid work, occupation-specific displacement anxiety — that is structural rather than perceptual, and that current survey instruments and disclosure experiments only partially capture.
