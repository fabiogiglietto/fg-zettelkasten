# Public Attitudes and Labor Experiences with AI

## The Arc of Inquiry

This collection traces a widening circle of concern: from the hidden labor that makes AI systems possible, through the political economy that organizes their deployment, to the lived experience and attitudes of the publics and workers who encounter them daily. Read together, the papers suggest that "public attitudes toward AI" cannot be understood apart from the material and discursive infrastructures that produce those attitudes in the first place.

### Labor Behind the Interface

Two papers insist that any account of AI's social effects must begin with the invisible human labor that sustains it. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] argues that red-teaming—the adversarial probing that underwrites claims of AI "safety"—is a sociotechnical practice freighted with value judgments, outsourced labor arrangements, and genuine psychological harm, closely echoing the earlier history of commercial content moderation. The essay's call for an empirical, interdisciplinary research agenda on red-teaming labor resonates with [[UnknownUnknown-db]], which turns to workers themselves—81,000 Claude.ai users—to ask how AI exposure translates into self-reported productivity gains and displacement anxiety. Where Gillespie et al. foreground the hidden costs borne by those *building* AI's guardrails, the Anthropic report documents the more diffuse anxieties and gains experienced by those *using* AI in their jobs, finding a U-shaped relationship between productivity speedup and perceived job threat, and sharper displacement concern among early-career and highly-exposed workers. Together these two papers frame "AI labor" as a continuum running from the ghost workers who police AI's outputs to the white-collar professionals whose task scope AI quietly expands or threatens.

### Naming the Political Economy

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] supplies the structural frame that the labor-focused papers gesture toward but do not fully theorize. By arguing that AI is best understood as a capital-driven "Project" rather than a technology, and by naming five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that redirect critical attention away from network power, this paper offers a lens for reinterpreting the other entries in the topic. The "safety decoy," for instance, casts new light on [[Gillespie2026-aa]]'s account of red-teaming: proprietary, opaque value-setting by AI companies can be read not just as a labor-governance problem but as part of the broader mechanism by which safety discourse legitimizes corporate self-regulation. Similarly, the "disruption decoy"—which frames workforce automation as the central story while obscuring corporate restructuring—complicates how we should read self-reported productivity and displacement data like that in [[UnknownUnknown-db]]: survey respondents' framings of "who benefits" from AI gains (themselves, employers, AI companies) are precisely the kind of individualized accounting that Vertesi et al. warn can mask concentration of power upstream.

### National Publics and the Texture of Ambivalence

A second cluster of papers moves from labor and political economy to the survey-based study of general public attitudes. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] offer complementary national portraits—Italy and the United States, respectively—that converge on a common finding: rising adoption of AI tools (chatbots, voice assistants, smart devices) coexists with deepening skepticism and a widening sense of AI as societal threat. Fattorini et al. characterize Italian sentiment as "critical ambivalence," showing that acceptance is highly context-dependent (high for security applications, low for journalism, hiring, and artistic production) and stratified by age, gender, and education. Gottfried et al. find a structurally similar pattern in the US: chatbot use has become mainstream, yet views "tilt negative" across virtually every age group, and confidence in both government regulation and corporate stewardship is low and falling. Both papers push back against a simple "exposure breeds comfort" narrative; in Italy, increased media visibility of AI has not closed the gap between exposure and understanding, while in the US, the heaviest adopters (adults under 30) are also among the most pessimistic. Read against [[Vertesi2026-lv]], these ambivalence findings might themselves be seen as evidence that the "inevitability decoy" is only partially successful—publics are adopting AI tools while refusing the narrative of frictionless, beneficial inevitability pushed by industry.

### Persuasion, Trust, and the Problem of the Intermediary

The remaining papers narrow the lens further, from general societal attitudes to the specific question of how AI shapes trust, persuasion, and information-seeking. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] demonstrates empirically that frontier AI systems can out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, an advantage rooted in information throughput rather than rapport—a finding that gives teeth to public anxieties (visible in [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]]) about AI's power to manipulate political attitudes and information ecosystems. [[Suk2026-ai]] supplies a theoretical scaffold for this concern, proposing a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework for studying trust in generative AI that explicitly builds on legacies of mass-media and social-media trust research; it functions as connective tissue linking the empirical national-survey papers to the more granular journalism-trust studies that follow.

Three closely related empirical papers—[[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]]—apply this trust lens specifically to AI-mediated news and search, and together paint a nuanced picture that complicates blanket claims of "AI skepticism." Gilardi et al. find that Swiss readers cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, and that disclosure of AI involvement actually boosts short-term willingness to continue reading—though this curiosity effect does not translate into durable acceptance of AI journalism. Mattis et al., using a more granular task-specific conjoint design with Dutch respondents, show that this scalar "trust penalty" is not uniform: fact-checking disclosures incur much larger penalties than idea or image generation, and audience responses cluster into at least five distinct preference profiles shaped by political position and AI literacy, challenging the simpler all-or-nothing disclosure effects found elsewhere. Sbaraini-Fontes et al. add a cross-cutting distinction, showing that Italian audiences trust AI-generated *search* results more than AI-produced *news*, suggesting that it is not AI involvement per se but the institutional configuration in which it is embedded—search intermediary versus newsroom—that calibrates trust. Taken together, these three studies argue for disaggregating "AI in journalism" into task-, platform-, and audience-specific components rather than treating it as a monolithic object of public sentiment, a methodological corrective that echoes Mattis et al.'s explicit critique of "generic AI labels."

### Synthesis

Across this topic, a layered argument emerges. The political-economy and labor papers ([[Vertesi2026-lv]], [[Gillespie2026-aa]], [[UnknownUnknown-db]]) insist that public attitudes do not form in a vacuum: they are conditioned by corporate narrative control, outsourced and often traumatic labor, and occupational exposure that is unevenly distributed. The national-survey papers ([[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]]) show that, whatever its sources, public sentiment increasingly resolves into durable ambivalence rather than naive enthusiasm or outright rejection. And the persuasion/trust cluster ([[Hackenburg2026-ud]], [[Suk2026-ai]], [[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]]) demonstrates that this ambivalence is itself finely structured—sensitive to disclosure framing, task type, political identity, and institutional context—even as AI systems are simultaneously becoming demonstrably more capable of shaping belief and behavior than the skeptical publics studying them might assume.
