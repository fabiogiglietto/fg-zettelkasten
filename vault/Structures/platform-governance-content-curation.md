# Platform Governance & Algorithmic Content Curation

## The arc of inquiry

This corpus traces a single, tightening argument: that visibility on platforms is neither neutral nor natural but actively engineered, that the infrastructure researchers once used to study this engineering has been systematically dismantled, and that new regulatory tools (chiefly the EU's Digital Services Act) are only partially and unevenly restoring the capacity to see what platforms do. The notes move from empirical demonstrations of algorithmic curation, through a political economy of data access ("Wild West" to "walled garden"), into a reflexive literature on how the resulting evidence base is itself compromised by industry entanglement.

## From open web to walled garden: the data-access arc

A dominant strand documents platforms' retreat from open data access as a historical process with clear stages. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodize this trajectory explicitly—from a laissez-faire API era, through a brief "Golden Age" of academic access, to today's paywalled or "clean room" regimes—while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the human cost in a bibliometric eulogy showing Twitter-based scholarship declining 13% in 2024 after the $42,000/month Enterprise API pricing. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this into the present moment, showing how Meta's Content Library "clean room" replaced CrowdTangle mid-2024-election and structurally privileges well-resourced, code-fluent, Global-North quantitative researchers over qualitative and cross-platform work—a concern echoed by [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]], whose audits of TikTok's and YouTube's research APIs find persistent, undocumented limitations that undermine their stated DSA-compliance function. [[Helmond2026-ll]] situates this retreat within a broader "platformization" thesis: Meta's 2026 retirement of the Like button signals the end of the open-web social-graph model in favor of in-app AI inference, extending platform logics into new domains (space, defense) via new intermediaries.

The regulatory response—Article 40 of the DSA—receives sustained, ambivalent scrutiny. [[Pierri2025-hm]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], and [[Bechmann2026-dr]] treat implementation turbulence not as regulatory failure but as evidence the law is finally constraining entrenched platform power, a "moving targets, moving politics" reading that counsels patience over abandonment. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows empirically how "data quality" itself became a contested, politically negotiated category during DSA consultations, with Meta strategically inverting researchers' own arguments. [[Crosset2026-mq]] zooms out further, reading the DSA alongside US state laws as one of three competing "circulation regimes" (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation) that converge on a shared logic of security bureaucratisation rather than direct content control.

## Auditing the feed: what algorithms actually do

A parallel empirical tradition audits specific algorithmic systems to test whether they cause the harms attributed to them. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X finds the "For You" feed shifts users measurably rightward via a persistence mechanism (algorithm-induced following), reconciling earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complicates simple partisan-bias readings of Musk-era X, showing apparent right-leaning advantage is better explained by "agitating content" and proximity to Musk himself. Bouchaud2026-um (Bouchaud2026-lr) demonstrates X's recommender inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear ideology dimension in its embedding space, raising GDPR-adjacent profiling concerns, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows Community Notes structurally undermoderates precisely the polarizing content most relevant to electoral integrity, and [[Renault2025-uh]] finds Republican posts flagged 2.3x more often than Democratic ones even under this "bias-resistant" crowdsourced system. On YouTube, [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects and finds strong "rabbit holes" but no radicalization pathway, alongside a platform-wide conservative tilt; Jurg2025-ju-adjacent [[Rieder2026-pp]] documents how deplatforming Andrew Tate failed to reduce his ambient visibility, outpaced by recommendation-driven replatforming. [[McNally2025-dn]] insists the "black box" metaphor is overstated, showing Facebook's News Feed changes produce detectable, lagged, section-specific effects on Guardian engagement—directly motivating systemic algorithmic auditing under DSA Article 40(4). [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment offers a constructive counterpoint: engagement-based feeds do amplify moralized/toxic content and distort social-norm perceptions, but a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment—demonstrating governance is a design choice, not an inevitability.

## Meta's political content reduction and the URL Shares Dataset

A tightly linked cluster interrogates Meta's specific policy interventions and the datasets used to study them. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] identifies a critical artifact: the 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in the URL Shares Dataset produces near-identical trends across 46 disparate countries, revealing a platform-wide algorithmic force (likely Feed/MSI changes) that can overwhelm the social signal researchers seek to measure—a foundational methodological warning for the entire empirical literature that follows. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] applies this dataset comparatively across Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares in election years without proportional rises in views, and confirms older cohorts dominate exposure and sharing. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] pushes furthest, using the successor Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset to show partisan alignment dampens and journalistic quality amplifies reach independent of raw shares—with these effects fluctuating sharply around known governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures), directly evidencing active curation rather than neutral conduit. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] then examines Meta's political content reduction policy itself in Italy, finding a 72% reach reduction for re-elected MPs that began roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout, a policy undisclosed in DSA transparency reports, and—crucially—an asymmetric effect whereby extremist accounts offset per-post losses through sharply increased posting volume. Together these four papers form a coherent methodological-empirical spine: dataset artifact → comparative baseline → causal mechanism → policy-specific consequence.

## Platform self-narration and governance discourse

Several papers treat platform communications themselves as objects of discourse analysis. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows Meta's Newsroom frames "problematic content" as originating from outside actors while eliding its own algorithmic and Groups-based amplification, with terms like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" functioning as strategically ambiguous floating signifiers that diffuse into regulation itself. [[De2026-ld]] generalizes this into "changecraft"—a cross-platform (Meta, TikTok, YouTube, X) analysis of how platforms discursively normalize policy pivots as continuity rather than rupture. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] extends the same lens to Google's AI Overviews, finding Google frames itself as neutral infrastructure while journalists voice epistemic risk and SEO marketers voice commercial anxiety—with the user's own voice largely absent across all three discourses. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically tests Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors against actual outcome data, finding removal occurs in only ~30% of cases, directly undercutting the rhetorical justification for ending Meta's Third-Party Fact-Checking Program; [[Farkas2026-lr]] complements this by showing how fact-checkers themselves rhetorically defend platform dependency through apologia strategies of differentiation and transcendence.

## Industry capture of the evidence base

A reflexive cluster argues that the research literature evaluating platforms is itself structurally compromised. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] draw explicit parallels to tobacco, pharma, and fossil-fuel science, cataloguing suppression of internal research, design bias, selective funding, and "performative collaboration." [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers a meta-scientific critique of the flagship Meta2020 partnership—including [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s null-effect study of political ad removal and [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s earlier ideological-diversity study—arguing one-off experiments on rapidly mutating platforms lack "temporal validity," and that empirical social science is structurally incapable of keeping pace, making proactive disclosure mandates necessary. [[Budak2024-ef]] pushes back from a different angle, arguing public discourse has overstated algorithmic and misinformation harms relative to the actual evidence base—a corrective in tension with, rather than simple agreement with, the "capture" literature.

## Adjacent currents: theorizing new platform logics, extremism, and moderation collapse

Several papers reconceptualize what "social" media even means under algorithmic curation. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes TikTok's shift from "networked" to "clustered" publics; [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media"; [[Swartz2026-zb]] argues scams are now central, not peripheral, to platform economics. Extremism and moderation-evasion studies—[[Karo2026-dn]] on jihadist TikTok tactics, [[Nangle2026-yo]] on far-right Instagram affect, [[Rieder2026-pp]] on Tate—converge on a shared finding: recommendation-driven circulation structurally outpaces removal-based moderation. Meanwhile [[Moran2025-qn]] and [[Donovan2025-ws]] document the institutional retreat of trust-and-safety infrastructure itself, and [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study synthesizes expert consensus that platform governance and journalism-ecosystem interventions now rank above individual-level literacy fixes—closing the loop back to this note's central claim: visibility is governed, that governance is opaque, and the tools to make it legible are themselves under constant renegotiation.
