# Information Operations & Disinformation Narratives

## Overview: A Field Mapping Its Own Object

The papers gathered here trace a discipline in the process of redefining its central object of study. What began as a relatively bounded problem — "fake news," discrete false claims, bot accounts — has expanded into a sprawling investigation of *how deception is organized*: as narrative, as infrastructure, as labor, as platform affordance, and increasingly as a capability of generative AI. Read together, these papers chart three interlocking arcs: (1) a conceptual turn from information to narrative and from content to structure; (2) an empirical turn toward measurement, detection, and cross-platform/cross-national comparison; and (3) an emergent turn toward generative AI as both subject and instrument of information operations.

## From False Facts to Narrative: The Conceptual Turn

A striking convergence across this corpus is dissatisfaction with fact-centric models of disinformation. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the strongest programmatic case: disinformation succeeds not because it is false but because it functions as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *story*, signaled through catchphrases and images that travel across genres immune to domain-level detection. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes further into narrative theory itself, arguing the field's use of "narrative" has been analytically naïve, and proposing a hermeneutic-realist framework (via Ricoeur and Phelan) that can judge content as referentially and ethically suspect without reducing to true/false binaries. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] anticipates this shift from a cybernetic angle, relocating analytical attention from creators' intent to the judgments of *propagators* who reshape meaning at each retelling. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] operationalize this narrative turn empirically, showing via election-integrity rumors how "deep stories" are collaboratively performed by influencers and audiences, and how evidence-frame interactions (rather than false facts per se) drive misleading rumor cycles. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Goel2025-iq]] extend the logic to the US hybrid media ecosystem, the latter showing that *true* content from mainstream outlets is routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives — a finding that indicts domain-blocklist methodologies. [[Waight2025-al]] responds methodologically, building an LLM-based pipeline to measure "narrative similarity" as distinct from lexical or topical overlap, demonstrating on the Ukraine "biolabs" case that cruder methods miss most of the phenomenon. Even critiques of "fake news" as a term belong here: [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] show how "fake news" itself has become a rhetorical weapon of delegitimization rather than a descriptive category, while [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorizes "strategic lying" as an evolution of political spin whose potency lies precisely in provoking amplifying rebuttals.

## Naming the Phenomenon: Deceptive Networks, Zombie Internets, and the Limits of CIB

A second cluster interrogates the adequacy of platforms' own operational categories — especially Meta's "coordinated inauthentic behavior" (CIB). [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]], central to this corpus, proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a broader analytic that unifies state propaganda, gambling promotion, and poorly moderated adult-content networks under a shared structural logic — the "Zombie Internet" — arguing that content-inauthenticity framings miss operations whose harm lies in exploiting platform affordances rather than fabricating false claims. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] complements this from the platform-discourse side, showing how Meta's own Newsroom uses "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" as a floating signifier that externalizes blame while eliding the role of hyperpartisan verified accounts and Facebook Groups. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers the most systematic theoretical corrective, arguing CIB conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent and proposing a rational-choice typology keyed to principals' resources and stakes. [[noauthor_undated-bm]] extends the ontology further by showing that financially-motivated networks (scam/clickbait operations) are empirically indistinguishable in political impact from classic CIB — a finding echoed in spirit by [[Swartz2026-zb]]'s essay arguing that scams are not peripheral to platform economies but their organizing logic. [[Pante2025-pq]] applies this skepticism reflexively, re-testing claims of inter-state CIB collaboration with modern detection methods and control datasets. Detection methodology itself is advancing on multiple fronts: [[Minici2024-tf]] (graph foundation models), [[Luceri2025-tr]] (adapting coordination detection to TikTok's video-first ecosystem), [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] (real-time bot detection on Bluesky), Bastos2025-ol (visual/image-based attribution), and [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE framework, which reconstructs cross-platform "bridge users" who seed narrative migration between Truth Social and X — a structural rather than interactional account of influence.

## State Propaganda, Troll Labor, and Authoritarian Adaptation

A substantial subset examines state-linked operations empirically and organizationally. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT analysis of IRA employee CVs overturns received wisdom about the "troll factory," showing it as an ordinary media-labor-market institution whose recruitment spiked with Crimea, not the 2016 US election. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] examine coordination in more closed authoritarian contexts (pro-government Telegram in Russia/Belarus; multilingual Twitter activity around China's COVID protests), while [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents Serbia's SNS party developing decentralized, mimetic CIB that evades bot-farm-style detection — part of a broader argument that digital authoritarianism is evolving toward subtler forms, a theme also taken up comparatively in [[Beacken2026-zb]]'s six-country study of generative-AI propaganda under democratic weakening. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes a "dual objective" model of wartime propaganda addressed differently to domestic versus foreign audiences, complementing [[Kim2026-br]]'s two-decade study of Korean troll comments, which finds morally condemning rhetoric — not praise of foreign actors — is the dominant and most-amplified strategy. [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Sadler2025-vu]] both examine Ukraine-war propaganda (#IStandWithPutin; NATO-blaming influencer narratives) as exploiting rather than evading platforms' "infrastructure of truth." [[Bosch2024-hj]] adds a sensory dimension, identifying sound as an under-examined vector of TikTok-based Ukraine disinformation, while [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats deploying platform-specific visual securitization — argumentative on Facebook/X, memetic and often geographically fabricated on TikTok — illustrating how radical-right campaigns tailor disinformation-adjacent tactics to affordances and younger audiences.

## Political Elites, Parties, and the Structural Drivers of Misinformation Sharing

Several papers shift attention from covert operations to elite political behavior. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country analysis of parliamentarians finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing — only their conjunction, radical-right populism, does — a structural claim echoed by [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s account of how "CRT Republicans" gain factional advantage through connective ties to hyper-partisan media, and by [[Bennett2025-xs]]'s call to integrate technocentric and institutionalist accounts of backsliding via "digital surrogate organizations." [[Tai2026-qk]] and [[Rodarte2026-dk]] examine elected officials more directly: the former asking what institutional/ideological factors check misinformation sharing, the latter showing Brazilian parliamentarians contesting journalistic epistemic authority through distinct rhetorical postures (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, brokerage) during the Manaus crisis. [[Copland2025-em]] traces how Sky News Australia became a node in transnational "network propaganda," while [[Iris2026-pg]] documents cross-national evidence that radical-right actors receive disproportionate media visibility regardless of electoral performance — a finding in tension with claims of right-wing media marginalization, and one that dovetails with [[Gattermann2025-yx]]'s finding that far-right electoral performance itself shapes voters' disinformation concerns.

## Consequences: Trust, Polarization, and Democratic Erosion

A cluster of survey and panel studies asks what disinformation *does* to citizens. Brazil is a recurring site: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]], using the same panel infrastructure, show that belief in electoral misinformation both increases political intolerance and decreases trust in election results, while mainstream news use bolsters democratic resilience even among Bolsonaro supporters — and that messaging-app use has only indirect, misinformation-belief-mediated effects. [[Ventura2025-sw]] pushes this further by using a WhatsApp deactivation field experiment to isolate messaging platforms' distinct causal role. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] complicates the causal story from another angle: media narratives *about* election interference themselves produce asymmetric polarization, independent of whether the interference worked — suggesting alarmist coverage may serve the disinformation campaign's goals. [[Kim2026-wg]] provides some of the most direct causal evidence in the corpus, using real-time ad-tracking to show that 2016 geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads measurably depressed turnout among their targets, challenging a run of prior null results. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s Kenya-based experiment reframes deepfake impact as socially negotiated: audience comments, not the artifact alone, determine persuasive effect, moderated by partisanship — a "collective sensemaking" argument that resonates with [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]].

## Platforms, Fact-Checkers, and the Politics of Moderation

Several papers examine the institutional response apparatus. [[Vincent_undated-re]] offers longitudinal measurement showing TikTok's mis/disinformation prevalence rising and persistent engagement advantages for low-credibility accounts. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] turn to fact-checkers themselves: the former shows that Meta's post-fact-check content removal is rare and inconsistent, undercutting Zuckerberg's censorship framing, while the latter documents fact-checkers' rhetorical "apologia" in justifying platform funding dependencies — both papers converging on fact-checking as an epistemic institution whose legitimacy is precarious and negotiated. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written from within a fact-checking organization (Maldita.es), proposes moving beyond claim-level correction toward "narrative monitoring and argument-checking," directly operationalizing the narrative-turn insights of [[Marwick2026-qd]] and [[Sadler2025-vu]] in a crisis-response setting (Valencia floods). [[Rogers2026-cy]] contributes a "post-truth space" mapping method for locating influential unverifiable sources, demonstrated on Moldovan Facebook war discourse. Zooming out, [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes platform moderation as structurally tethered to engagement-based business models, while [[Freelon2024-sc]] documents the deteriorating "post-API age" infrastructure that constrains all this research, and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] frames platform accountability as a precondition for halting democratic backsliding.

## The Generative AI Inflection

The most forward-looking cluster registers generative AI as reshaping both the production and epistemology of disinformation. [[Emilio2026-ik]] proposes the paradigmatic frame: "synthetic realities" that fabricate content, identity, interaction, and institutional trust jointly, producing a paradox whereby ubiquitous fakery causes rational discounting of *all* digital evidence. [[Schroeder2026-im]] extends this to agentic "malicious AI swarms" capable of manufacturing consensus and poisoning training data ("LLM grooming"). [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] supplies unsettling experimental grounding: AI systems reliably out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, an advantage traced to sheer information throughput rather than rapport. [[Waight2026-ts]] demonstrates a more insidious pathway — state media control is already embedded in LLM training data, causing systematically more pro-regime outputs in lower-media-freedom languages, effectively "laundering" propaganda into ostensibly neutral AI answers. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] tempers alarm about AI-generated visuals specifically, finding decontextualized *real* video often outperforms AI images in credibility — a reminder that low-tech deception remains potent. [[Paci2025-ag]] shows current LLMs still struggle to interpret manipulative political implicature, suggesting AI's role as *analytic* tool for detecting manipulation remains limited even as its role as *producer* of manipulation grows. [[Wang2025-zy]] rounds out this cluster by mapping how national presses themselves narrate generative AI through utopian/dystopian imaginaries, showing the discourse *about* AI risk is itself nationally contested terrain.

## Cross-Cutting Threads and Open Questions

Running beneath these clusters are recurring meta-concerns: the WEIRD-centrism critique voiced explicitly by [[Humprecht2025-ml]] and empirically addressed by [[Nenno2025-xa]]'s 24-country news-values study and [[Beacken2026-zb]]'s comparative GenAI propaganda work; the tension between content-level and structural/behavioral definitions of harm, playing out across [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]], [[Thiele2025-ol]], and [[Hurcombe2025-cs]]; and a persistent methodological anxiety — visible in [[Freelon2024-sc]], [[Waight2025-al]], and [[Pante2025-pq]] — about whether existing tools are adequate to phenomena that are increasingly cross-platform, cross-lingual, AI-mediated, and narratively rather than factually organized. Taken together, this body of work suggests the field's next task is less to catalog new manipulative artifacts than to build frameworks — conceptual, computational, and regulatory — capable of tracking meaning, coordination, and trust as they migrate across an information ecosystem that platforms, states, and now generative AI systems are jointly and often adversarially reshaping.
