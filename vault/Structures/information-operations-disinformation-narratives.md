# Information Operations & Disinformation Narratives

## The empirical turn in disinformation research: from content to structure

The corpus filed here traces a field in transition — from treating disinformation as discrete false claims to be detected and corrected, toward understanding it as narrative, infrastructure, and organized labor. Foundational statements like [[Vosoughi2018-at]], [[Lazer2018-mm]], and [[Benkler2018-lw]] established the baseline concern (falsehood spreads faster than truth; propaganda is structural, not just technological), while [[Bennett2018-ys]] and later [[Bennett2025-xs]]/[[Gillespie2010-as]] reframe disinformation as a symptom of institutional legitimacy crisis rather than an independent variable. This structural turn crystallizes across the collection's most theoretically ambitious papers: [[Marwick2026-qd]] and [[Sadler2025-vu]] both insist that "narrative," not the true/false binary, is the right unit of analysis, while [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Waight2025-al]] operationalize this insight empirically — the latter demonstrating that lexical or topical similarity measures (text-reuse, topic models, standalone embeddings) systematically undercount how narratives actually travel, particularly when true content from mainstream outlets ([[Goel2025-iq]]) or true claims filtered through partisan frames ([[Starbird2025-jj]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]]) is what does the ideological work. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]]'s concept of "Deceptive Information Operations" and the "Zombie Internet" pulls together gambling promotion, pro-Putin propaganda, and poorly moderated adult-content networks under one structural logic, explicitly arguing that platform enforcement frameworks (CIB, CSH) are too narrow because they fixate on content falsity and account inauthenticity rather than the exploitation of affordances.

## Coordination, attribution, and the labor of manipulation

A cluster of papers pushes past detection-as-endpoint toward questions of who coordinates, how, and why. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers the field's most systematic attempt to theorize the "attribution problem," critiquing looser concepts like coordinated inauthentic behavior for conflating inauthenticity with manipulative intent — a critique echoed empirically in [[Hurcombe2025-cs]]'s discourse analysis of how Meta's own Newsroom exploits the ambiguity of terms like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" as a floating signifier. Detection methodology itself is advancing along platform-specific lines: [[Luceri2025-tr]] and [[Bosch2024-hj]] tackle TikTok's video- and sound-first affordances; [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] moves to Bluesky; [[Minici2024-tf]] proposes a graph foundation model (IOHunter) for cross-campaign generalization; [[Gerard2025-br]] reconceives cross-platform networks via shared narrative-cluster embedding rather than interaction traces, revealing a small set of "bridge users" who seed roughly 70% of migrating narratives between Truth Social and X. Meanwhile [[Pante2025-pq]] applies rigor retroactively, questioning whether earlier claims of inter-state coordination survive more careful benchmarking — a useful corrective note within a field prone to pattern-matching. The organizational/labor dimension is anchored by [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT reconstruction of the IRA's actual workforce (young, inexperienced, embedded in Russia's ordinary media labor market, recruited around Crimea rather than the 2016 election) and extended geographically by [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] (pro-government Telegram in Russia/Belarus), [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] (Serbia's SNS mimicking grassroots authenticity), [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] (dual-use coordination during China's COVID protests), and [[Kim2026-br]]'s two-decade, 112-million-comment analysis of Korean trolling, which finds that morally condemning rhetoric — not pro-regime praise — is what actually earns engagement-based amplification.

## Platforms as governance actors and the fate of fact-checking

A second thread interrogates platforms' own accountability rhetoric. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes "misinformation-at-scale" as inherent to engagement-driven business models, tracing platforms' oscillation from non-intervention to behavioral moderation and back. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] turn to fact-checkers themselves, documenting both the actual (limited) consequences of verification post-Zuckerberg's anti-fact-checking pivot and the rhetorical "apologia" fact-checkers deploy to justify platform dependency. [[Freelon2024-sc]] supplies the infrastructural backstory common to nearly all computational work here: the "post-API age" in which data access is perpetually contingent on platform goodwill. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Schroeder2026-im]] gesture toward the frontier concern — that platform accountability failures compound with emerging agentic-AI threats — while [[Rogers2026-cy]] offers a practically oriented alternative to platform-centric governance, mapping "post-truth spaces" for use by verification specialists directly.

## Generative AI as a new vector

A fast-growing subset addresses how generative AI reshapes propaganda's economics and epistemics rather than simply automating old tactics. [[Emilio2026-ik]] frames the core risk as "synthetic realities" that induce a society-wide "epistemic tax," while [[Topinka2026-hb]] offers a sharper theoretical wedge: "slopaganda" works not through deception but affective attunement, obviously fake yet emotionally resonant, produced by models optimized via RLHF to feel out ambient ideological moods. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] supplies alarming behavioral evidence that AI already out-persuades elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with fact-density (not rapport) as the mechanism — a finding that recontextualizes [[Waight2026-ts]]'s discovery that state media control already biases LLM training data and output valence by query language, and [[Wack2026-bt]]'s Kenya-based experiment showing deepfake impact is socially negotiated through surrounding audience commentary rather than fixed at production. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] tempers the panic, finding visual/AI disinformation does not universally outperform text — only decontextualized video of real footage does, and only for high-salience issues like Ukraine. [[Beacken2026-zb]] and [[Paci2025-ag]] add comparative and linguistic-competence caveats: GenAI propaganda adoption is structurally uneven across weakened democracies, and current LLMs still struggle to interpret the implicit, pragmatically loaded content that makes political discourse manipulative in the first place.

## Elites, parties, and the politics of the label

A substantial cluster examines disinformation as elite political strategy rather than bottom-up pathology. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds, across 26 countries and 32 million tweets, that neither populism nor right ideology alone predicts elite misinformation-sharing — only their interaction (radical-right populism) does — complementing [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s account of how "connective faction" ties (e.g., the sudden elevation of Critical Race Theory) reward ideological extremity with amplification. [[Gaber2022-bk]] traces "strategic lying" through Brexit and the 2019 UK election as an evolution, not rupture, of political spin, while [[Osmundsen2021-et]] and [[Tai2026-qk]] pursue the psychological and institutional correlates of elite sharing behavior. The instrumentalization of the "fake news" label itself is a recurring object of study: [[Yoo2026-ev]], [[Mosca2026-yh]], and [[Pennycook2021-jq]]/[[Lazer2018-mm]] (which appear to share the Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model applied to Italian media) all converge on the finding that "fake news" functions as a weaponized, boundary-policing rhetorical device rather than a description of content accuracy — with populist parties disproportionately positioned as targets rather than accusers. [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Heft2021-ky]] map the transnational right-wing media architecture (Sky News Australia, hyperlinked European/US right networks) that supplies raw material for these contests, while [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows how a single party (Sweden Democrats) tailors securitizing visual rhetoric differently across Facebook, X, and TikTok, prioritizing memetic content for younger audiences on the platform where its following is smallest.

## Elections, trust, and downstream democratic effects

The final and most policy-relevant thread asks what all this actually does to democratic practice. Brazil furnishes rich comparative ground: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] track how belief in electoral misinformation predicts rising intolerance and falling trust across the 2022 election, with messaging-app use working indirectly through misinformation belief; [[Ventura2025-sw]] isolates WhatsApp's distinct causal role via a deactivation experiment; and [[Rodarte2026-dk]] examines how Brazilian parliamentarians competed for epistemic authority during the Manaus COVID collapse. US-focused causal work is anchored by [[Kim2026-wg]]'s striking finding that geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads produced turnout declines the field's earlier null results ([[Eady2023-xg]]) missed by relying on average treatment effects rather than direct exposure measurement among targeted subgroups. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] and [[Karlsson2026-hd]] extend this to the corrosive and asymmetrically polarizing effects of elite fraud claims and even of media narratives about interference itself — the latter suggesting that alarmist coverage of election interference can itself deepen perceived societal division. [[Scalco2026-bd]] and [[Vincent_undated-re]] supply infrastructure-level measurement tools (information voids, cross-platform prevalence tracking) for monitoring these dynamics longitudinally, while [[Ramos2026-qo]] and [[Wang2025-zy]] extend the narrative-first framework into practitioner terrain (argument-checking beyond fact-checking) and comparative media-imaginary analysis of AI discourse, respectively — both symptomatic of a field increasingly oriented toward synthesis frameworks that can travel across national contexts, platforms, and the widening aperture from text to image, video, sound, and now synthetic interaction itself. Efstistratiou2026-ij (on COVID science communication pathways), [[Marino2023-9137f448]] (Alternative Influence Networks in Italy), [[Tsfati2020-uo]], [[Yang2023-cg]], [[Gattermann2025-yx]], [[Iris2026-pg]], [[Humprecht2020-gd]], [[Humprecht2025-ml]], [[Nenno2025-xa]], [[Zhao2025-ny]], [[Graham2025-gp]], [[Makeev2026-ma]], [[Keller2019-nk]], [[Starbird2019-qv]], [[FitzGerald2025-nv]], Bastos2025-ol, [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]], [[Swartz2026-zb]], [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]], [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]], and [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] round out this arc, collectively insisting that whether the object is COVID science, gambling scams, culture-war epithets, or the mimicry of ordinary citizen speech, the through-line is the same: information operations are best understood not as external contaminants of an otherwise healthy public sphere but as symptoms and accelerants of the sphere's own structural conditions — attention economics, platform architecture, institutional legitimacy, and now, the training data and alignment procedures of generative models themselves.
