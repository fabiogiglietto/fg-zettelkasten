# Information Operations & Disinformation Narratives

## The turn toward narrative

A significant portion of this literature is programmatic: it argues that disinformation studies has outgrown its founding vocabulary. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the case most forcefully, reconceiving disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative* rather than discrete false claims—a move echoed in [[Sadler2025-vu]]'s call for a "hermeneutic realist" narratology able to judge content as referentially and ethically suspect without needing it to be strictly false, and in [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]]'s survey evidence that cumulative exposure to recurring narratives, not isolated claims, drives belief. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] operationalize this shift empirically, showing via CSCW-style sensemaking frameworks (deep stories, evidence-frame analysis) how election-fraud rumors are built collaboratively across quote-tweets, with influencers supplying ambiguous "evidence" that partisan audiences frame into narrative. [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[Waight2025-al]] push the point methodologically: domain-blocklist and text-reuse approaches systematically miss how *true* mainstream content gets repurposed into misleading narratives, or how narratives migrate across languages and outlets despite zero lexical overlap. Together these papers argue that the classic misinformation/disinformation binary—and its associated fact-checking apparatus—targets the wrong unit of analysis. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]]'s "Zombie Internet" study of gambling, adult-content, and pro-Putin operations on Facebook makes a structurally parallel argument for coordination research: motivation-based taxonomies (CIB, deception theory) each capture one dimension, but operations converge on a shared logic of deceptive content, affordance exploitation, and coordinated participation regardless of whether the underlying goal is epistemic, economic, or normative harm.

## Coordination, attribution, and detection across platforms

A dense empirical cluster addresses the mechanics of coordinated inauthentic behavior. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers the theoretical scaffolding, critiquing "CIB" as conflating inauthenticity with manipulative intent and proposing a rational-choice typology linking campaign scale, elaborateness, and disguise to a principal's resources and stakes. This attribution problem plays out empirically across contexts: [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT reconstruction of the St Petersburg IRA's workforce and hierarchy recasts the "troll factory" as an ordinary media-labor-market actor rather than a covert cell; [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] traces coordinated amplification among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus; [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents Serbia's SNS mimicking authentic grassroots support in ways that evade bot-farm-style detection; and [[Kim2026-br]]'s two-decade study of Korean troll comments finds that *condemning* rhetoric, not praise of foreign actors, is both dominant and disproportionately amplified by engagement-based ranking. Methodologically, the frontier is moving toward platforms and modalities beyond text-on-Twitter: [[Luceri2025-tr]] adapts coordination detection to TikTok's video-first ecosystem, Bastos2025-ol mines the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium dataset for visual signatures of troll farms, [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] pursues real-time bot detection on Bluesky, and [[Minici2024-tf]]'s IOHunter proposes a graph-foundation-model approach generalizing across campaigns. Yet several papers counsel caution about over-claiming coordination: [[Pante2025-pq]] reassesses prior claims of inter-state collaboration and finds them unsupported once modern methods and control datasets are applied, while Efstdoyle...—more precisely [[Efstratiou2026-ij]]—shows that a coordinated contrarian network amplifying credentialed anti-consensus experts is not bot-driven at all. [[Zhao2025-ny]] extends the coordination lens to "hashtag hijacking" as a strategic move in public-opinion contests. Cutting across all of this, [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE framework reframes cross-platform influence structurally—via shared narrative-cluster participation rather than direct interaction—identifying a tiny set of "bridge users" who seed most narrative migration between Truth Social and X.

## State and party propaganda: Russia, China, and the radical right

A geopolitically oriented strand examines propaganda as state or party strategy. On the war in Ukraine: [[Waight2025-al]]'s biolabs case, [[Sadler2025-vu]]'s NATO-blame narrative analysis, [[Graham2025-gp]]'s #IStandWithPutin study of propaganda exploiting "the infrastructure of truth," [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s account of TikTok sound as a propaganda vector, and [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s dual-audience model of Russian domestic-versus-foreign wartime messaging collectively trace how a single conflict generates differentiated narrative ecosystems by platform, language, and audience. China-focused work extends this: [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] examines dual-use Twitter coordination around COVID-19 protests, while [[Waight2026-ts]] offers a striking pivot to infrastructure, showing state-coordinated Chinese media is memorized by LLM training pipelines and measurably shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence—an "indirect" propaganda pathway operating through AI rather than through audiences directly. On the radical right, [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary Twitter analysis finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing, but their conjunction does; [[Knupfer2025-vt]] traces how the Critical Race Theory issue activated a "logic of connective faction" rewarding ideologically extreme Republicans with engagement; [[Bennett2025-xs]] synthesizes these dynamics into a "digital surrogate organizations" account of MAGA-era illiberalization; [[Copland2025-em]], [[Heft2021-ky]], and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] map transnational right-wing media infrastructure (Sky News Australia, hyperlinked alt-media networks, Sweden Democrats' platform-tailored securitizing visuals); and [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate news-media visibility for radical-right parties across the 2024 European elections, independent of electoral performance. [[Beacken2026-zb]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] round out this cluster by asking how GenAI propaganda and disinformation concern themselves vary contextually across weakened democracies and electoral outcomes respectively.

## Foundational frameworks and historical throughlines

Much of this newer work is explicitly in dialogue with an earlier foundational generation still central to the topic. [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] established the "asymmetric," institutionally-rooted account of the American and transnational "disinformation order"; [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Lazer2018-mm]] set the field's early definitional terms (disinformation vs. misinformation vs. fake news) and its evidentiary gaps; [[Vosoughi2018-at]] supplied the canonical diffusion finding that falsehood outpaces truth; [[Starbird2019-qv]] reframed information operations as "collaborative work" performed by crowds rather than bots alone—a move that anticipates [[Marwick2026-qd]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] a half-decade later; [[Keller2019-nk]]'s South Korean NIS study established coordination-pattern detection and the principal-agent lens now formalized by [[Thiele2025-ol]]; [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] offered an early cybernetic critique of creator-centric "fake news" typologies that anticipates the narrative and propagator-centered turn; and [[Humprecht2020-gd]] established the cross-national resilience framework that [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue introduction and [[Nenno2025-xa]]'s 24-country news-values study explicitly build on to push the field past its WEIRD-country default. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] anchors the recurring finding, revisited by [[Yang2023-cg]] for images and [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] for platform self-narration, that mainstream media and platforms themselves are often the primary amplifiers of the very content they claim to police.

## Effects: elections, trust, and persuasion

A substantial empirical wing asks what disinformation actually *does* to political attitudes and behavior, often with more equivocal findings than popular alarm suggests. On voter suppression and fraud claims: [[Kim2026-wg]] finds real, geo-racially targeted turnout effects from 2016 IRA-linked ads; [[Berlinski2023-rj]] and [[Gaber2022-bk]] show elite fraud claims and "strategic lying" erode confidence in specific elections without denting support for democracy as such; [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] extend this to Brazil, showing misinformation belief predicts both political intolerance and diminished trust in election results, while mainstream news use bolsters resilience even among Bolsonaro voters. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] complicates the picture further: merely reporting on election-interference narratives can itself polarize audiences asymmetrically by partisan framing, suggesting media discourse *about* disinformation is a harm pathway in its own right. [[Ventura2025-sw]] pushes the object of study toward encrypted messaging, an infrastructure increasingly implicated in Global South information ecosystems. At the frontier of AI-driven persuasion, [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] demonstrates that frontier LLMs already out-persuade elite human debaters and professional canvassers via sheer information throughput, while [[Wack2026-bt]] shows deepfake impact in Kenya is socially negotiated through visible peer commentary rather than fixed at the point of viewing, and [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds visual disinformation's advantage over text is issue-contingent rather than universal.

## Institutions, platforms, and governance

Finally, a cluster addresses the governance layer: how platforms, fact-checkers, and researchers themselves navigate this landscape. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes "misinformation-at-scale" as structural to engagement-driven business models; [[Freelon2024-sc]] catalogues the deteriorating "post-API age" of researcher data access; [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects how Meta's Newsroom discursively externalizes blame for platform harms; [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] examine fact-checkers' precarious rhetorical and material dependence on the same platforms they audit, especially after Meta's 2025 retreat from third-party fact-checking; [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Scalco2026-bd]] contribute ongoing measurement infrastructure (platform prevalence tracking, "information voids"); and [[Rogers2026-cy]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]] offer applied, verification-oriented tools ("post-truth spaces," argument-checking) explicitly designed to give fact-checkers actionable purchase on narrative-level phenomena. At the normative horizon, [[Schroeder2026-im]], [[Emilio2026-ik]], [[Topinka2026-hb]], and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] anticipate where this is heading: agentic "AI swarms," synthetic-reality "epistemic security," affectively-resonant "slopaganda," and platform accountability for democratic backsliding—suggesting the field's next decade will be organized less around discrete false claims than around the governance of synthetic, narrative-saturated information environments this whole body of work has been documenting.
