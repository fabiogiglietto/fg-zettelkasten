# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## Affective polarization as the organizing construct

The foundational claim running through this collection is that polarization is as much about *feeling* as about *believing*. [[Iyengar2019-jj]] sets the terms: partisanship functions as a social identity that divides the world into liked in-groups and disliked out-groups, and this affective dimension is empirically separable from issue-based (ideological) polarization. Several later papers operationalize and extend this distinction empirically. [[Malhotra2026-vl]] shows affective polarization materializing as *tie severing* — Facebook users disproportionately unfriend cross-cutting political contacts, especially around flashpoints like January 6th, demonstrating that network homophily is not just a static artifact of selection but an ongoing behavioral process. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] locates the same animus at the root of fake-news sharing: not ignorance or trolling but out-party hostility, which turns misinformation into an extension of ordinary partisan goal-directed behavior rather than a distinct cognitive failure. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] extends the logic further by showing that partisans perceive *misinformation itself* asymmetrically — a "hostile misinformation effect" in which people believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by falsehoods, intensified by identity strength and ideological extremity. Together these papers suggest affect, not information deficit, is the throughline connecting polarization to its downstream pathologies: intolerance, unfriending, rumor-sharing, and epistemic grievance.

## The echo chamber debate: contested ground, contested method

A second cluster interrogates the central metaphor of the field — the echo chamber — and largely finds it fragile, context-dependent, or methodologically manufactured. The early computational baseline is set by [[Barbera2015-je]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]], which develop ideal-point estimation from Twitter network structure and find that political topics generate strong ideological segregation while nonpolitical topics do not, with a notable asymmetry — conservatives cluster more tightly than liberals. [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] corroborates homogeneous, polarized diffusion patterns on Facebook using percolation modeling, reinforcing the echo-chamber picture for conspiracy versus science content. But subsequent work complicates this consensus sharply. [[Nyhan2023-gb]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]], both drawing on population-scale Facebook data from 2020, arrive at seemingly opposite conclusions — the former finding like-minded exposure prevalent but causally inert for attitudes, the latter finding segregation substantial, asymmetric, and concentrated with misinformation on the political right — illustrating how measurement choices (domain- versus URL-level, potential versus engaged exposure) shape the story. [[Green2025-ap]] diagnoses exactly this problem: domain-level partisanship scores mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, meaning "curation bubbles" are real but are built story-by-story through networked sharing rather than source-by-source. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review formalizes this meta-critique at scale, arguing that most disagreement in 129 echo-chamber studies stems from inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than divergent realities. Dehghan2026-xg... wait key is Dehghan2026-sy extends the skepticism to Reddit, finding that even a nominal cross-ideological "town square" like r/politics shows negligible cross-ideological overlap, arguing polarization is "sedimented" into platform-discourse-moderation entanglements rather than emergent from discourse content alone. At the experimental end, [[Bail2018-fk]] delivers the sharpest challenge to intervention optimism: exposing partisans to opposing views on Twitter backfires, increasing polarization (asymmetrically, among Republicans) rather than reducing it. Capping this thread, [[Schulz2026-ts]] reframes the entire debate as partly illusory: much apparent online polarization is "false polarization" produced by the self-selection of ideologically extreme users into posting, not by a genuinely more divided underlying public — a finding that retroactively helps explain why echo-chamber measurements vary so much depending on whether they tap expressed discourse or population opinion.

## Platforms, algorithms, and governance as polarization infrastructure

A related set of papers shifts attention from users' choices to platform design and governance as independent forces shaping polarization. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows that X's Community Notes system, by design, privileges cross-partisan agreement and therefore systematically undermoderates the most polarizing (and electorally risky) content — a structural rather than incidental flaw. [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents a starker intervention effect: Brazil's judicially imposed ban on X produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet," as conservative users disproportionately circumvented the ban while liberal users went silent, with partisan asymmetries in the platform's news environment persisting well after the ban lifted. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] poses the broader question of whether alternative/alt-tech platforms themselves drive discursive polarization, situating platform choice as a structural variable alongside algorithmic curation. Read together with [[Knupfer2025-vt]] — which shows how Republican elites who adopt hyper-partisan-media-friendly talking points (using Critical Race Theory as a "ping") are rewarded with outsized engagement and attention, forging a "logic of connective faction" between party factions and digital surrogate networks — this cluster suggests that both top-down moderation choices and elite incentive structures on networked platforms actively manufacture, rather than merely reflect, partisan extremity.

## Elites, media, and the discursive construction of division

Where the previous cluster treats polarization as a structural/algorithmic outcome, another group of papers treats it as a *discursive accomplishment* — something elites and media actors narrate into being. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents Brazilian parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during a COVID crisis, with opposition and pro-government blocs adopting opposing strategies (derivative journalism-mimicry versus direct anti-press authority claims) that harden into near-disconnected network clusters. [[Yoo2026-ev]] finds a parallel dynamic in U.S. hyperpartisan outlets, where "fake news" functions not as a descriptor but as a weaponized boundary term that both left (Occupy Democrats) and right (Newsmax) outlets use nearly identically to recast opponents as epistemically deviant, reshaping Hallin's spheres of consensus and legitimate controversy. [[Elfes2026-jb]] pushes this further methodologically, introducing "narrative polarisation" — measuring how partisan groups assign opposing actantial roles (hero/villain/victim) to the same actors — and finding, counterintuitively, that user comments can be *less* narratively polarized than the videos they respond to, even as deeper narrative motifs preserve partisan difference. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] traces a longer discursive arc, showing how "woke" migrated from Black vernacular anti-racist discourse into a cross-platform culture-war epithet, hijacked synergistically by partisan opinion leaders across Twitter and YouTube. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offer adjacent case studies — the former on how UK newspapers' political lean predicts which COVID scientists they cite, the latter on the counter-hashtag #JeNeSuisPasCharlie as a site of dissent against dominant post-tragedy solidarity narratives — both illustrating how polarized framing colonizes even ostensibly factual or unifying discourse.

## Misinformation as accelerant, not root cause

A substantial subgroup treats misinformation not as polarization's cause but as its amplifier, operating through pre-existing partisan identities. [[Rossini2026-jn]] shows belief in Brazilian electoral fraud claims predicting rising political intolerance over a panel study, with messaging-app news use working indirectly through misinformation belief — misinformation "amplifies" rather than originates cleavages like antipetismo. [[Lieu2025-nl]] finds that among climate misinformation types, attacks on climate solutions and scientists (rather than logical-fallacy structure) are both most believed and most polarizing, especially for conservatives. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] identifies influencer status itself, rather than content, as a driver of toxic echo-chamber-like responses to brand misinformation, converting credibility and parasocial bonds into collective antagonism. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] complicates simple alarm-based narratives: exposure to stories about foreign election interference does not raise aggregate polarization, but asymmetric "my side was the target" framings do heighten perceived division — meaning discourse *about* misinformation and interference can itself be polarizing, independent of whether the interference worked. [[Marino2026-he]]'s three-year longitudinal study of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook communities undercuts the assumption that hyperpartisan communities are stable fortresses, showing affective engagement is highly event-sensitive and even vulnerable to adversarial "brigading" — a finding that resonates with [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s claim that community boundaries are actively, not passively, maintained.

## Identity, narrative, and meaning-making beyond content

[[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes the volume's discursive thread into cultural sociology, showing that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere less through shared ideological content than through symbolic boundary work — reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor, converting personal hardship into collective "deep stories," and uniting ideologically disparate (secular-libertarian and religious) factions against an imagined "mainstream other." This paper, alongside [[Elfes2026-jb]], argues for moving polarization research past interaction-count metrics toward identity and narrative construction — a methodological provocation echoed by [[Sarmiento2025-as]], which proposes unsupervised, non-predefined frame-detection pipelines precisely because polarized framing is often too emergent and subtle for fixed coding schemes to capture.

## AI as both diagnostic tool and new actor

Two papers position large language models as a double-edged addition to this landscape. [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates that LLMs can infer users' political alignment with striking accuracy even from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse, exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary — a capability that is analytically powerful for studying the politicization of culture but also a serious micro-targeting and privacy risk. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] inverts the lens, showing that perceived political bias in an LLM itself attenuates its persuasive power on polarized topics, with users arguing back rather than disengaging — suggesting that as conversational AI becomes a new discourse participant, it inherits rather than escapes the dynamics of partisan motivated reasoning documented throughout this literature.

## Meta-level reckonings: method, norms, and geographic scope

The collection closes on self-reflexive notes. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] finds that depolarization recommendations in the scholarly literature are typically disconnected from the evidence those same studies produce, resting instead on unexamined deliberative-democratic ideals — a critique that resonates with [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s finding that definitional inconsistency, not empirical reality, drives much disagreement about echo chambers. [[Esau2025-tf]] responds constructively by proposing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as normative antidotes to specifically *destructive* polarization, implicitly accepting van Eck and Hartmann's call for transparency about normative commitments. Finally, [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] names the field's structural blind spot directly: polarization scholarship is fragmented across disciplines and disproportionately concentrated on the United States (and, in this collection, increasingly Brazil as a second major site via [[Rodarte2026-dk]], [[Ventura2026-yc]], [[Rossini2026-jn]], and [[Marino2026-he]]), calling for integrative, globally equitable frameworks — a fitting capstone for a literature that, across its forty years of thermometer scores, network scrapes, and narrative codebooks, is still negotiating what polarization even is.
