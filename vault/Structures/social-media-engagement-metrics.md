# Social Media Engagement & Reactions

## The affective infrastructure of engagement

At the centre of this cluster sits a deceptively simple platform feature: the Facebook Reaction button. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] provides the connective tissue for the whole topic, synthesising 64 studies to show that Reactions function as "paralinguistic digital affordances" — low-effort signals that nonetheless carry rich emotional meaning, with political content in particular skewing toward negative, high-arousal reactions like anger. This review essentially sets the research agenda that several other papers in this folder pursue empirically. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] operationalises exactly the dynamic Anwar's review flags: across Norwegian, Swedish, and Danish elections, populist parties elicit disproportionate "Angry" reactions to confrontational posts, and anger — unlike "Love" — reliably predicts sharing. Read together, these two papers establish a durable empirical regularity: negative affect is not incidental noise on political Facebook but a structural driver of virality, and it advantages actors (especially populists) whose rhetorical style is confrontational rather than conciliatory.

## From cross-sectional snapshots to longitudinal instability

Where Kalsnes and Anwar largely capture engagement at a moment or across discrete campaigns, [[Marino2026-he]] pushes the topic toward a longitudinal, dynamic view. Its three-year study of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook networks complicates the echo-chamber assumption implicit in much reaction research: rather than stable, predictable emotional regimes, the community's affective engagement is shown to be highly volatile, reorganising sharply around events like Lula's inauguration and the January coup attempt. This is an important corrective to the more static logic of "angry post → angry reactions → more shares" found in [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]: Marino shows that the *strength* and even *direction* of such affective mappings shift with political context, and that hyperpartisan boundaries are porous, penetrated by adversarial "brigading" rather than sealed by homophily. Together, Kalsnes and Marino trace an arc from "reactions as a stable signature of ideological style" to "reactions as an unstable, event-contingent process" — a shift that mirrors broader debates in polarization research about whether partisan communities are best modeled as fixed structures or dynamic systems.

[[Green2025-ap]] contributes a methodological argument that resonates with this arc from a different angle: it shows that source-level (domain) measures of partisanship — the kind of aggregate categorization that underlies much engagement research — systematically mask story-level heterogeneity in audience reactions. Its concept of "partisan curation bubbles" suggests that what looks like a stable partisan audience at the domain level dissolves into much more heterogeneous, story-specific reaction patterns once one looks closer, an epistemological caution that complements Marino's substantive finding that apparent community stability breaks down under longitudinal, fine-grained analysis.

## Emotion as strategy, not just symptom

A second thread treats affective engagement not merely as an outcome to be measured but as a resource strategically leveraged by communicators. [[Xue2025-bp]] shows that even fact-checkers — whose genre claims normative objectivity — deploy emotional language to shape engagement and audience sentiment, troubling the fact/feeling binary that underwrites much misinformation research. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] extends this strategic-emotion logic to toxicity: influencer-sourced misinformation generates more, and qualitatively different, toxic reactions than the same content from ordinary users, because influencer credibility and parasocial bonds convert audience trust into infrastructure for collective antagonism. This paper effectively identifies a *source* mechanism to complement the *content* mechanisms foregrounded by Kalsnes and Anwar — toxicity and virality are not just about what is said (anger-inducing content) but who says it and what relational capital they carry.

[[Nangle2026-yo]] pursues a related but inverted strategy: rather than anger or toxicity, far-right actors on Instagram use *positive* affect, humour, and memetic softening to normalise extremist content, aided by algorithmic proximity that blurs boundaries between mainstream and extremist material. Read against Kalsnes's anger-drives-shares finding, Nangle's case suggests that the emotional register most effective for mobilization is platform- and context-dependent: confrontational anger works for electoral Facebook posts, while ambient positive affect and humour work for ideological normalisation on Instagram Reels. This divergence usefully complicates any single-explanation account of "which emotion drives engagement," pointing instead toward an affective-discursive ecology model, in which platform affordances, genre, and political goal jointly shape which emotional strategy is optimal.

## Engagement beyond the political: intimacy, health, and labour

Not all papers in this set concern polarization outright, but each helps triangulate what "engagement signals" mean sociologically. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] offers an earlier, non-political account of Facebook's affective infrastructure, showing how "Liking," tagging, and implicit sharing constitute a collaborative, networked intimacy among friends — a reminder that the same lightweight affective signals mobilised for political anger in [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] originated in, and still coexist with, everyday relational maintenance. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] extends the engagement-as-signal framework to health communication, finding that emotionally resonant or "popular" topics (celebrity health scandals, malpractice) generate disproportionate comment activity relative to their prevalence in the news agenda — an echo of Anwar's and Kalsnes's finding that emotionally charged content outperforms neutral content regardless of domain.

Finally, [[Volpe2026-um]] shifts the lens from reactions-as-data to the labour conditions that produce shareable, affectively resonant content in the first place: micro-influencers' performances of care and community are shown to be survival strategies shaped by platform demands for constant, emotionally legible output. This paper usefully denaturalizes the entire topic's object of study — the "engagement signal" is not simply an authentic expression of public sentiment but, upstream, an artifact of precarious content-production incentives that reward exactly the kind of niche, emotionally consistent material that platforms are optimised to circulate.

## Synthesis

Across this cluster, a coherent arc emerges: from establishing that emotional reactions are a meaningful, structured signal of political and social discourse ([[Anwar2024-34dba628]], [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]), through complications of that signal's stability and interpretability ([[Green2025-ap]], [[Marino2026-he]]), to accounts of how various actors — fact-checkers, influencers, far-right communicators, ordinary users — strategically produce or are structurally incentivized to produce the very emotional content that these engagement metrics register ([[Xue2025-bp]], [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]], [[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Volpe2026-um]]), grounded throughout by earlier and adjacent accounts of what affective platform engagement means outside strictly political contexts ([[Farci2017-2a88c00e]], [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]]). The overall trajectory suggests a field moving from treating reactions as transparent indicators of public sentiment toward treating them as co-produced outcomes of platform affordances, strategic communicative labour, and unstable community dynamics — a shift with real consequences for how virality and affective polarization should be measured and theorized going forward.
