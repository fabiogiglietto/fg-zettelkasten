# Social Media Engagement & Reactions

## The Emotional Architecture of Platformed Politics

The papers gathered here converge on a single wager: that engagement signals—reactions, shares, comments, toxicity scores—are not epiphenomenal traces of political discourse but constitutive of it. Where earlier internet-fragmentation debates asked whether exposure to diverse content would homogenize or polarize opinion, this cluster asks a more granular question: which affective and structural mechanisms determine what circulates, and what political work does that circulation do?

### Reactions as affective affordances

[[Anwar2024-34dba628]] provides the synthetic anchor for this line of inquiry, systematizing 64 studies on Facebook's Reactions feature since its 2016 launch. Its central finding—that negative, especially "angry," reactions drive engagement in political content while positive reactions dominate lifestyle content—is empirically extended by [[Kalsnes2025-zb]], whose comparative study of Scandinavian elections shows that populist parties are disproportionately effective at eliciting "Angry" reactions, and that anger (unlike "Love") reliably predicts sharing. Together these papers establish reactions as "paralinguistic digital affordances" (per Hayes et al., cited in Anwar) that function simultaneously as emotional expression and algorithmic currency: what gets felt strongly gets seen widely. [[Xue2025-bp]] complicates this affective economy by showing that even fact-checking—a genre with a normative claim to dispassionate objectivity—strategically deploys emotionality to secure engagement, suggesting that the "emotional turn" identified in populist communication has become a general strategic logic across genres, not a pathology specific to demagogic actors.

### From reactions to toxicity and mobilization

[[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] pushes this affective analysis toward its darker consequences, demonstrating that the *source* of misinformation—influencer versus ordinary user—shapes not just the volume but the qualitative texture of toxic response, with parasocial bonds redirecting follower hostility into homogeneous "flame-bait firestorms." This source-amplification logic dovetails with [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s account of far-right Instagram as an "affective-discursive ecology," where positive affect and humour, rather than overt hostility, do the work of normalizing extremist content—an important complication of the anger-centric model, showing that softened, memetic positivity can be just as mobilizing as outrage when algorithmic proximity does the amplifying. Read alongside [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]], these papers suggest a bifurcated affective playbook available to political actors: anger for visibility and mobilization, humour and warmth for mainstreaming and legitimation.

### Longitudinal and structural correctives to echo-chamber assumptions

A second thread interrogates whether affectively-charged partisan communities behave as stable echo chambers at all. [[Marino2026-he]]'s three-year study of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook networks is the pivotal longitudinal contribution: against assumptions of impermeable hyperpartisan stability, it finds engagement patterns—love, anger, argumentative comment-dominance—shifting dramatically around political inflection points (Lula's inauguration, the January coup attempt), with boundary permeability increasing rather than decreasing under stress. This finding resonates with [[Green2025-ap]]'s methodological argument that source-level partisanship measures obscure enormous story-level heterogeneity, meaning that what looks like a bounded partisan bubble at the domain level may in fact be a shifting, story-by-story "curation bubble" reconstituted through networked sharing rather than fixed audience identity. Both papers push back against static echo-chamber models, though from different angles—one temporal, one structural.

This revisionist stance has deeper roots in the two classic studies anchoring the topic historically: [[Messing2014-jc]] shows that social endorsement, not partisan source identity, drives news selection, undermining strong fragmentation narratives; [[Bail2018-fk]] delivers the more unsettling counterpoint that direct exposure to opposing views—the very corrective imagined to counteract echo chambers—can backfire and increase polarization, particularly among Republicans. Read together with [[Marino2026-he]] and [[Green2025-ap]], these four papers trace an arc from naïve fragmentation theses, through experimental disconfirmation of the "more exposure heals" hypothesis, to contemporary computational demonstrations that partisan community boundaries are volatile, contested, and event-sensitive rather than fixed structural features of platforms.

### Engagement as sociality, labour, and gatekeeping

A final set of papers reframes engagement metrics away from strictly political discourse toward the everyday social and economic logics that produce them. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] shows that even something as minimal as "Liking" operates as a relational signal sustaining "networked intimacy," reminding us that the same affordances driving political virality also scaffold friendship and community maintenance—a useful counterweight to the toxicity- and polarization-focused papers, showing engagement's constructive, phatic register. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] extends the gatekeeping lens to health communication, finding that emotionally resonant or scandalous topics (celebrity health, malpractice) generate disproportionate engagement relative to their prevalence in aggregated news, echoing the anger/negativity findings from the explicitly political studies but in a non-partisan domain. Finally, [[Volpe2026-um]] reframes engagement production itself as labour: micro-influencers' performances of care and community are shown to be survival strategies negotiating platform algorithms and precarity, a reminder that behind every affective signal analyzed in the other papers sits a content producer whose emotional authenticity is also professional necessity. Taken together, this final cluster insists that "engagement" is never a neutral metric of public sentiment—it is always simultaneously a social bond, an economic resource, and a gatekeeping mechanism, entangled with the very platform infrastructures ([[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]]) that make political and non-political virality alike possible.
