# Disinformation Narratives & Information Operations

## From false facts to narrative: a conceptual turn

The thread that ties this collection together is a shared discontent with the "true/false" binary that dominated first-generation disinformation research. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] opens this argument early, showing via second-order cybernetics that intent-centric definitions of "fake news" cannot capture how information actually cycles through a hybrid media system — attention shifts instead to propagators and the meso-level judgments that turn a claim into misinformation, disinformation, or something composite. [[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], and [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] push this insight further by arguing that disinformation is best studied as *narrative*: cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic stories (Marwick), hermeneutically "disclosive" rather than simply false accounts (Sadler), or cumulative exposure effects that build belief over repeated tellings (Suau Martinez). [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] operationalize this narrative turn empirically, showing that U.S. election-fraud rumors are less about discrete false facts than about "evidence-frame" dynamics and "deep story" performances co-produced by influencers, elites, and audiences across quote-tweets and comment threads. [[Goel2025-iq]] extends the argument in an unexpected direction: even scrupulously accurate mainstream articles are disproportionately co-shared to prop up misleading narratives (on vaccines, on voter fraud), meaning that source-level "fake news" metrics dramatically undercount the phenomenon. This cluster converges on a methodological consequence taken up directly by [[Waight2025-al]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]]: if narrative rather than falsity is the operative unit, then detection and correction tools need to measure narrative similarity and argumentative structure (premises, fallacies) rather than only fact-check individual claims — Waight's LLM-based narrative-similarity pipeline and Maldita.es's "argument-checking" workflow (built around the Valencia DANA flood conspiracy) are direct methodological responses to this reframing.

## Elite strategy and the politics of misinformation

A second cluster treats misinformation and "fake news" rhetoric as elite political strategy rather than epistemic accident. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians contesting journalistic gatekeeping during the Manaus COVID collapse through distinct epistemic-authority repertoires; [[Rossini2026-mj]] extends the Brazilian case to Bolsonaro's systematic assault on electoral legitimacy, finding mainstream news use bolsters trust even among his own voters while belief in fraud narratives corrodes it. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] generalizes this across 26 countries: neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation sharing, but their conjunction — radical-right populism — does, tying misinformation to a specific ideological-institutional posture rather than a generic information disorder. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] examine the discursive weaponization of the *label* itself, showing how "fake news" accusations function rhetorically to redraw Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance (Newsmax vs. Occupy Democrats) or to structure negative campaigning and selective media amplification in Italy. [[Tai2026-qk]] rounds out this cluster by asking which institutional and ideological positions act as checks on elected officials' own misinformation-sharing — a question that dovetails with Tornberg's party-level findings.

## The Ukraine war as narrative battleground

Several papers converge on the Russo-Ukrainian war as an especially rich site for observing narrative propagation and propaganda design. [[Sadler2025-vu]] and its narratological toolkit dissect a Venezuelan influencer's NATO-blame narrative to show how content can be referentially and ethically suspect without being straightforwardly false. [[Waight2025-al]] traces the "Ukrainian biolabs" claim's diffusion from Russian state media into low-quality and even mainstream U.S. outlets, demonstrating empirically that lexical-overlap methods badly undercount narrative migration. [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Bosch2024-hj]] examine platform-specific propaganda mechanics — hashtag/verification infrastructure exploitation in #IStandWithPutin, and sonic affordances in TikTok's #Ukraine ecosystem. [[Rogers2026-cy]], conducted under the EU vera.ai project, maps Moldovan Facebook's Russia-Ukraine discourse through "post-truth spaces," finding that a "borderline" cluster bridging fringe and mainstream content — rather than fringe sources themselves — drives the war conversation. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] includes a pro-Putin coordinated network as one of its three "Deceptive Information Operations" case studies, and [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes the dual domestic/foreign propaganda logic underlying Russian state messaging about the war more broadly.

## Coordinated inauthentic behavior, attribution, and platform governance

A methodologically dense cluster, closely aligned with the PROMPT and vera.ai monitoring agenda, investigates the organizational and infrastructural mechanics of coordinated manipulation. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] offers a rare organizational-labor account of the IRA "troll factory," showing it functioned as a conventional media/PR employer embedded in Russia's labor market rather than a shadowy intelligence unit. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Zhao2025-ny]] examine coordination mechanics on Telegram (Russia/Belarus regime amplification) and hashtag hijacking dynamics involving bots and narrative substitution, while [[Kim2026-br]] applies explainable ML to two decades of Korean news comments, finding that suspected trolls favor morally condemning rhetoric over direct praise — a subtler and more algorithmically rewarded strategy than naive boosting models predict. [[Thiele2025-ol]] steps back to theorize the field's core "attribution problem," proposing a rational-choice typology linking campaign scale, elaborateness, and disguise to principals' resources and stakes — explicitly critiquing Meta's "coordinated inauthentic behavior" category as too narrow. That critique is empirically vindicated by [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] and [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]], both emerging directly from PROMPT/vera.ai monitoring infrastructure: the former's "Zombie Internet" concept and three Facebook case studies (pro-Putin propaganda, gambling promotion, unmoderated adult-content groups) show that CIB/CSH enforcement frameworks miss manipulation whose harm lies in the convergence of deceptive content, affordance exploitation, and coordination rather than in any single dimension; the latter documents an exponential, ChatGPT-triggered explosion in AI-generated gambling imagery exploiting Meta's paid/organic regulatory gap. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] and [[Gaw2025-ru]] extend the infrastructural argument temporally and institutionally — manipulation campaigns persist by appropriating new events, and Philippine influence operations function as durable political-economic brokerage rather than ad hoc trolling.

## Platform ecosystems, mainstreaming, and audience culture

A related set of papers studies how platform affordances and audience cultures channel extremist or securitizing content into the mainstream. [[Rothut2026-or]] develops "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" from the German Querdenken movement's Telegram entanglement with far-right networks; [[Copland2025-em]] traces Sky News Australia's transformation into a node of transnational right-wing "network propaganda" via a dual Facebook/YouTube strategy; [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] compare platform-tailored visual securitization (Sweden Democrats' EU campaign) against surprisingly pro-immigration TikTok vernacular in Canada, both stressing that platform affordances are used symmetrically across ideological positions. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates stereotypes further, showing ConspiracyTok's "generous epistemology" is driven by young, non-white, and female creators performing "populist knowledge production." [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows influencer-sourced misinformation generates qualitatively distinct, more homogeneous toxicity than identical claims from regular users, implicating credibility and parasocial bonds as amplification mechanisms in their own right.

## Generative AI: persuasion, propaganda, and epistemic security

The newest and fastest-growing cluster addresses generative AI's role in both interpersonal persuasion and industrial-scale information operations. On persuasion, [[Hackenburg2025-dj]], [[Hackenburg2026-ud]], and [[Lin2025-xp]] form a tight empirical program: conversational AI outpersuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with information density (fact-checkable claims per exchange) — not personalization or model scale — identified as the operative mechanism, alongside a troubling persuasion-accuracy trade-off and a systematic rightward-skewing inaccuracy asymmetry. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows this fact-density story has a geopolitical shadow: state-coordinated media memorized in training corpora make LLMs more pro-regime in languages associated with lower media freedom, a subtle "laundering" pathway distinct from direct model manipulation. At the operational end, [[Schroeder2026-im]] and [[Emilio2026-ik]] theorize the frontier risk — malicious multi-agent "AI swarms" manufacturing consensus, and a four-layer "synthetic reality" stack producing an "epistemic tax" that may lead societies to rationally discount all digital evidence. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] grounds this in concrete harm and policy response, mapping the deepfake financial-fraud ecosystem and arguing for gatekeeper-liability regulation over victim-blaming frameworks. [[Wack2026-bt]] provides crucial audience-reception evidence from Kenya, showing deepfake impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated through surrounding comments and partisan motivated reasoning — directly relevant to Global South contexts underserved by moderation and labeling infrastructure. [[Beacken2026-zb]] insists this uptake is not uniform, comparing GenAI propaganda across six democratically weakened states to argue against technologically deterministic narratives. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] offers an important corrective note to the generative-AI alarm: decontextualized real video, not AI-generated stills, proved most credible and engaging disinformation for the salient Ukraine-war issue, suggesting low-tech recontextualization remains at least as potent as synthetic media. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] tie this cluster back to the coordinated-behavior literature empirically, documenting how generative AI intensifies (rather than replaces) established persuasive visual drivers in industrial-scale gambling promotion.

## Synthesizing the field

Sitting above all these strands, [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study with 47 international experts offers a field-level anticipatory assessment that anticipates much of this note's own architecture: it calls for integrative, multi-level, multi-stakeholder interventions across platform governance and journalism ecosystems rather than siloed, individual-focused fixes — and notes, tellingly, that generative AI's disruptive potential was barely anticipated as recently as 2022. Read together, these forty-five papers trace an arc from information-centric, fact/falsity paradigms toward narrative-, infrastructure-, and now generation-centric understandings of disinformation — a trajectory the PROMPT project's own monitoring tools ([[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]], [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]], [[Rogers2026-cy]], [[Waight2025-al]]) are actively built to keep pace with.
