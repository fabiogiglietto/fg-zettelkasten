# Disinformation Narratives and Information Operations

## The narrative turn: why "false claims" is the wrong unit of analysis

A striking convergence runs through this literature: disinformation is increasingly theorized not as discrete false statements but as *narratives* — recurring, cross-platform, emotionally resonant stories that outlive any single fact-check. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the strongest programmatic case, arguing that successful disinformation has characters, settings, plots, and catchphrases that provide identity affirmation and catharsis; domain-level or claim-level detection simply cannot see it. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes this further into narrative theory proper, showing via a Venezuelan influencer's NATO-blame tweets about Ukraine that content can be referentially and ethically suspect without containing any outright falsehood — the true/false binary that underwrites most disinformation research is inadequate to the phenomenon. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes this empirically, showing that cumulative exposure to a narrative across outlets — not exposure to any one false claim — predicts belief. [[Goel2025-iq]] demonstrates a related mechanism from the opposite direction: mainstream, factually accurate journalism is routinely repurposed to carry misleading narrative structures, meaning narrative-level and source-level misinformation measures diverge sharply. [[Waight2025-al]] supplies the methodological infrastructure this reframing needs — an LLM-based pipeline for measuring narrative similarity across languages — and uses it to trace the Russian "biolabs" narrative into mainstream U.S. outlets, a diffusion pattern invisible to text-reuse methods.

This narrative-first stance converges with sensemaking approaches from CSCW: [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] both show that election-fraud rumors emerge from an interaction between (often true) evidence and partisan interpretive frames supplied collaboratively by influencers and audiences — the "how" of telling constitutes meaning as much as the "what." [[Ramos2026-qo]], reporting from Maldita.es's frontline work on the Valencia floods conspiracy, arrives at the same conclusion from practice: narratives evolve and absorb new claims faster than fact-checkers can debunk them, motivating a shift toward "argument-checking" that targets fallacious reasoning rather than individual falsehoods.

## Anatomy of operations: coordination, brokerage, and the "Zombie Internet"

A second cluster interrogates the *structure* of deceptive operations rather than their content. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] is the anchor text here, proposing "Deceptive Information Operations" (DIOs) as a category defined by the convergence of deceptive intent, exploitation of platform affordances, and multi-account coordination — deliberately decoupled from the presence of falsehood. Its three Facebook case studies (pro-Putin propaganda, gambling promotion, unmoderated adult-content groups) reveal a shared operational logic across ostensibly unrelated harms, giving empirical flesh to the "Zombie Internet" metaphor: engagement-driven ranking and inconsistent moderation blur the line between authentic and manipulated activity regardless of motive. This matches [[Thiele2025-ol]]'s theoretical critique that "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent, and its proposal of a rational-choice typology for attributing campaigns to principals based on scale, elaborateness, and disguise. [[Pante2025-pq]] applies similar methodological rigor to inter-state coordination claims on X, finding that prior claims of state collaboration don't survive proper control comparisons — a caution against overreading coordination signals.

Organizational and political-economy perspectives deepen this picture. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT reconstruction of the St Petersburg IRA reveals it as a labor-market institution — young, inexperienced, integrated into Russia's ordinary media economy — that predates and outlives its association with the 2016 election, complicating the "foreign hacker" mythology. [[Gaw2025-ru]] extends this organizational lens to the Global South, reframing Philippine election influence operations as a form of political brokerage mediating between clients, platforms, and voters. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] and [[Zhao2025-ny]] gesture toward the adaptive, iterative character of these campaigns — their capacity to appropriate emerging events and hijack trending hashtags to sustain relevance — themes that anticipate the AI-driven "swarm" literature below.

## Scale, reach, and the limits of engagement metrics

A methodologically sophisticated strand asks how much any of this actually reaches people, using platform-level exposure data rather than engagement proxies. [[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion [[noauthor_undated-bm]] analyze Meta's 49 deceptive networks around the 2020 US election, finding reach highly concentrated (three networks drove ~70-80%), overwhelmingly propagated through reshares by unaffiliated accounts rather than direct network posting, and — crucially — largely non-causal: associations with factual discernment or trust evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] complements this with billion-post diffusion-tree data on Facebook, confirming that virality is concentrated in a small share of highly-reached content. Against this note of caution, [[Kim2026-wg]] and [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] make causal claims stick by exploiting exposure heterogeneity: geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads plausibly depressed turnout by up to 17% among the specifically targeted subpopulation, and antivaccine tweets are linked via epidemiological modeling to a measurable, if modest, toll in vaccine refusals and deaths. Together these papers model a maturing empirical standard: aggregate null effects can coexist with meaningful, concentrated harms in targeted subpopulations — a lesson [[Vincent_undated-re]]'s ongoing platform monitoring (TikTok's rising ~25% mis/disinformation share, persistent low-credibility engagement advantage) operationalizes as continuous audit.

## Platform-specific vectors: TikTok, visuals, sound, and AI

Several papers isolate how particular affordances shape disinformation's form. [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an underexamined, TikTok-specific propaganda technique in Ukraine-war content reaching tens of billions of views. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] both study immigration discourse across platforms, finding — counterintuitively for the former — that Canadian TikTok skews pro-immigration, while for the latter Swedish Democrats deploy TikTok specifically for memetic, decontextualized securitizing content aimed at younger voters, exploiting footage's ambiguous provenance. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues that Instagram's image-first architecture demands visual-first methodologies for studying climate conspiracy. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] offers a useful corrective to assumptions about visual/AI supremacy: video disinformation only outperforms text when abundant authentic footage exists to plausibly recontextualize (as with Ukraine), while AI-generated stills show no consistent advantage — low-tech decontextualization may matter more than generative AI hype suggests.

That said, several 2025-2026 papers argue AI does introduce qualitative shifts. [[Emilio2026-ik]] frames the risk as "synthetic realities" — coherent, interactive, fabricated environments across content, identity, interaction, and institutional layers — producing a paradox whereby ubiquitous synthetic media leads societies to rationally discount all digital evidence. [[Schroeder2026-im]] extends this to agentic "malicious AI swarms" capable of persistent, adaptive, cross-platform coordination beyond anything botnets achieved, including "LLM grooming" of training data. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s Kenyan deepfake experiment grounds these concerns empirically, showing that a deepfake's political impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated through surrounding comments — credulous framing amplifies damage, incredulous framing restores support, moderated by partisanship. [[Beacken2026-zb]] tempers technological determinism by showing GenAI propaganda adoption varies substantially with local political-economic structure across six weakened democracies.

## Elite strategy, partisan asymmetry, and the politics of the "fake news" label

A distinct thread examines how political elites, not just anonymous networks, produce and weaponize disinformation. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary Twitter analysis finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing — their interaction, radical-right populism specifically, does. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" shows how the Critical Race Theory issue was adopted disproportionately by ideologically extreme Republicans whose ties to hyper-partisan media were rewarded with engagement, radicalizing intra-party dynamics. [[Bennett2025-xs]] scales this into a broader theory of "digital surrogate organizations" reshaping the Republican Party toward illiberalism, arguing that technocentric and institutionalist accounts of backsliding must be integrated. [[Tai2026-qk]] and [[Rodarte2026-dk]] examine elected officials more directly: the former asks what institutional and ideological factors check misinformation-sharing, while the latter's Brazilian Twitter study shows parliamentarians pursuing rival modes of "epistemic authority" (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, proximity-based brokerage) during a health crisis — governing elites as media producers in their own right, not just sources.

Adjacent to this is the discursive battle over the "fake news"/"disinformation" label itself. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows both left- and right-wing US alternative outlets use "fake news" almost entirely as an empty delegitimizing weapon rather than a descriptive term. [[Mosca2026-yh]] formalizes this as "Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification," modeling fake-news accusations as negative-campaigning tools differentially amplified by aligned media. [[Graham2025-gp]]'s #IStandWithPutin case study argues propaganda increasingly exploits, rather than evades, the platform infrastructures built to signal truthfulness (hashtags, verification, algorithmic amplification), and [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s "post-truth spaces" concept, developed for the vera.ai project, offers fact-checkers a mapping technique to locate influence even when fringe clusters lack direct centrality — as in Moldovan Russia-Ukraine discourse, where a "borderline" cluster proved more influential than either overtly disinformative or mainstream nodes.

## State propaganda and wartime narratives

The Russo-Ukrainian war recurs as the paradigm case for state-level information operations. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes a "dual-objective" model in which authoritarian regimes construct divergent domestic (low-cost, high-stakes) and foreign-facing propaganda agendas simultaneously. [[Copland2025-em]] documents an adjacent transnational propaganda pathway: Sky News Australia's dual strategy of parochial Facebook content for domestic audiences versus viral YouTube amplification feeding the US right-wing pipeline, spreading COVID, election-fraud, and "Great Reset" conspiracies internationally. Both connect to [[Karlsson2026-hd]]'s finding that even *media narratives about* election interference — independent of whether interference succeeds — can generate asymmetric affective polarization when audiences perceive the opposing side as beneficiary, suggesting discourse about disinformation is itself a distinct harm pathway.

## Consequences: trust, tolerance, and democratic erosion

Downstream, several Brazil-focused studies (part of a shared panel-survey program) trace concrete democratic costs: [[Rossini2026-jn]] shows belief in electoral fraud misinformation increases political intolerance over time, partly channeled through messaging-app news use; [[Rossini2026-mj]] shows the mirror-image effect on institutional trust, with mainstream news use bolstering electoral trust even among Bolsonaro voters while misinformation belief and messaging-app use erode it. [[Ventura2025-sw]]'s WhatsApp deactivation experiment causally isolates messaging apps' distinct contribution to these dynamics, beyond feed-based platforms. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s cross-national "hostile misinformation effect" study complicates any simple diagnosis of who bears the harm: partisans systematically believe their own side is more targeted by misinformation than their rivals, an identity-driven bias linked to lower trust in both media and elections. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] adds that voter concern about disinformation itself tracks far-right electoral performance, suggesting perception and reality of the threat are politically entangled.

## Institutional response: fact-checking under siege

A final cluster examines the fragile institutional apparatus meant to counter all this. [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform-funded partnerships against accusations of compromised independence, while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] shows empirically that Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors is largely unfounded — content removal is rare, and most verified falsehoods are merely labeled or left untouched — even as Meta's shift away from its Third-Party Fact-Checking Program signals a broader move toward oligarchic, US-style content governance with weaker versions of accountability in Latin America. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] supplies the discourse-analytic complement, showing how Meta's own Newsroom communications strategically frame problematic content as originating from external "inauthentic" actors, deflecting attention from platform design and business-model incentives — a framing critique [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes at greater length, arguing that "misinformation-at-scale" is a structural feature, not a bug, of engagement-driven platforms, and that platforms' retreat from trust-and-safety investment after 2021 has left users without reliable access to "timely, accurate, local knowledge."

## Synthesis

Read together, these sixty papers trace an arc from *content* to *structure* to *system*: early disinformation research asked whether a claim was true; this corpus asks instead how narratives circulate and mutate across platforms ([[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], [[Waight2025-al]]), how operations are organized and financed regardless of stated motive ([[Giada2026-fc9a3833]], [[Thiele2025-ol]], [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]), how much of this actually reaches and moves real audiences ([[Appel2026-qr]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Kim2026-wg]]), and how weakly-governed platform infrastructures and now agentic AI threaten to make iterative, "zombie"-like persistence the default condition of the information environment ([[Schroeder2026-im]], [[Emilio2026-ik]], [[Frischlich2025-vn]]). The throughline connecting the PROMPT-adjacent case studies — Ukraine, gambling, immigration, elections, LGBTQI+ rights — is that none of these are best modeled as bounded "campaigns" with discrete falsehoods to debunk, but as recurring, adaptive, cross-platform narrative-and-infrastructure complexes that outlast any single intervention, demanding correspondingly layered, structural, and narrative-aware countermeasures.
