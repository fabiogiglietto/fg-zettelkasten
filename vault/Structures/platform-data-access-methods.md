# Platform Data Access & Research Methods

## The arc of a collapsing infrastructure

The papers gathered here trace a single, cumulative story: the empirical foundation of computational social science built on open platform APIs has eroded, and researchers, regulators, and platforms are now improvising a replacement architecture in real time. [[Yang2026-tq]] supplies the macro-diagnostic evidence for this claim, showing across 59 journals that the share of social-media-based social science research tripled from 2010 to the early 2020s before plateauing and *declining* in 2022–2024 — the first bibliometric confirmation that API closures are reshaping the discipline's empirical base, not just its methods sections. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya narrate the paradigmatic case: Twitter's transformation from the "Golden Age" open API that underwrote a whole subfield of "Twitter studies" to a $42,000/month paywall, with [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifying a 13% publication decline in 2024 alone. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodizes this same trajectory — Wild West, Golden Age, walled garden — and generalizes it beyond Twitter/X to Meta, Reddit, and TikTok, while [[Freelon2024-sc]] offers the most systematic taxonomy, mapping two decades of access policy across six platforms into five historical periods and four (plus one unofficial) present-day access regimes. Together these pieces establish the field's now-dominant framing concept: the "post-API age," in which access is permanently contingent on platform discretion rather than a stable research commons.

## Regulation as the new terrain: the DSA and Article 40

A second cluster treats the EU Digital Services Act, and specifically Article 40's vetted-researcher provision, as the emerging (if fraught) alternative infrastructure. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frame Article 40 as a genuine qualitative shift — from platform philanthropy to a binding legal right — while insisting that current turbulence (platform stalling, ambiguous procedures) reflects growing pains rather than regulatory failure. [[Pierri2025-hm]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] substantiate this optimism-with-caveats empirically: drawing on an ERC pilot with DG-CONNECT, [[Pierri2025-hm]] documents resource asymmetries, a "circular resource problem" disadvantaging early-career researchers, and platform reluctance verging on legal threats, while [[Philipp2026-tl]] shows how DSA-mandated APIs and portals vary widely across platforms in scope and eligibility, unevenly enabling election research. [[Peters2026-mo]] adds a granular political-economy account of the Delegated Regulation's drafting process itself, showing that "data quality" — ostensibly a technical construct — was a contested object that academic and NGO stakeholders had to fight to get written into the rules at all, against platforms (notably Meta) that strategically inverted the argument. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] converge on the same verdict: implementation is uneven, EU-only in reach, and risks creating a two-tier "data abyss" that excludes Global South and under-resourced researchers — a concern echoed structurally in [[Lukito2026-nb]]'s taxonomy of 72 election-data-access tools, which identifies "price, proficiency, or permission" as the three barriers gating who can actually do this research, and finds consumption-side data almost entirely absent (6 of 72 tools).

## Auditing the promises: does mandated access actually work?

A distinct empirical strand tests whether DSA-era access mechanisms deliver on their promise, and the verdict is largely skeptical. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained inconsistencies; [[Rieder2025-ju]] conducts the most granular such audit, showing YouTube's Data API search endpoint is "forgetful by design" — exhibiting a three-phase temporal decay that makes retrospective research on even still-public videos unreliable, directly undermining its fitness for DSA systemic-risk research. [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this critique substantively, auditing YouTube's election-period content moderation and finding inconsistent application of transparency labels and vague removal communications, arguing for reconceiving "transparency" as "observability." [[Bruns2026-pn]] generalizes the critique to Meta's clean-room model (the Meta Content Library), arguing that clean rooms, whatever their privacy rationale, structurally privilege quantitative code-based work over qualitative and cross-platform methods and widen the Global-North/Majority-World resource gap — a diagnosis anticipated ethnographically by [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "Seeing like an API" study of CrowdTangle, which shows how an API's technical design and governance rules actively constrain what researchers can even conceive of asking. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] applies DSA transparency data itself to expose disparities the regulation was meant to illuminate — showing systematic underinvestment in moderating Global South languages — demonstrating that DSA transparency reporting can be repurposed as a research object even while remaining, per the authors, insufficient for full independent assessment.

## Platform power over the research process itself

Several papers push past access mechanics to argue that platforms shape not just *what* can be studied but *what gets concluded*. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] form a tight trio documenting industry influence on the published record: the latter's bibliometric audit of Science/Nature/PNAS finds roughly half of high-profile social media papers have undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small elite of repeatedly-engaged authors and editors, with an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" once reviewers are included — empirically vindicating the conceptual warnings in [[Heiss2026-qv]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] about platform-academic collaborations reproducing tobacco- and pharma-style capture. This concern connects directly to methods papers showing that platform-provided datasets themselves encode hidden algorithmic artifacts: [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] demonstrates that Meta's 100-share anonymization threshold in the URL Shares Dataset produces spurious cross-country convergence driven by Feed-ranking changes rather than genuine social signal, while [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[McNally2025-dn]] both show, using CrowdTangle-adjacent or Privacy-Protected data, that Facebook's algorithmic governance interventions (e.g., 2020 "break the glass" measures) produce detectable, time-varying effects on what content is amplified — arguing against treating the News Feed as an unknowable "black box" and for treating algorithmic auditing as a DSA-enabled research program in its own right.

## Building around the wreckage: independent tools and methods

Faced with unreliable or gate-kept official channels, a final cluster documents the field's methodological adaptation. [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats and TokStats exemplify the emerging open-source response: unpermissioned random sampling that exploits YouTube and TikTok's ID structures to solve the "denominator problem" that opportunistic, keyword-based samples cannot address, explicitly positioned as public-interest infrastructure for the post-API age. [[Allen2025-ot]] discusses a complementary strategy — Piccardi et al.'s browser-extension-and-LLM reranking design as a genuinely "platform-independent" experimental method — while [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that even browser-based *in-situ* capture matters enormously, since ex-situ scraping introduces far more distortion (33.8%) than time delay alone, with systematic, non-random bias across content categories. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] represents an earlier-generation adaptation (Facebook ad-based survey recruitment) that anticipated today's necessity of working around, rather than through, platform APIs. [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Fan2026-af]] show how these adaptations scale up into infrastructural and analytical challenges: the former treats multi-platform election observability itself as an unsolved "research infrastructure problem," while the latter proposes new computational frameworks (sequence analysis, HMMs, process mining) for exploiting donated, timestamped digital trace data now that APIs no longer supply it. Detection methods for coordinated inauthentic behavior — [[Luceri2025-tr]] on TikTok's video-first ecosystem, [[Yang2025-iv]] on evasion-resistant Facebook signals, [[Pante2025-pq]]'s methodological reassessment of inter-state coordination claims — likewise show the field re-engineering core analytic techniques platform-by-platform as each new data environment imposes its own constraints. [[Scalco2026-bd]] and [[Oswald2025-km]] round out the picture by reminding readers what is epistemically at stake in all this infrastructure-building: social media data, however accessed, remains a skewed, production-biased proxy for public discourse and opinion, and methods for detecting "information voids" or correcting for the production-consumption gap are as vital as the access mechanisms themselves.

## Where the line of argument is heading

Read together, these papers converge on a shared diagnosis and a shared open question. The diagnosis, most explicitly stated by [[Bechmann2026-dr]] and [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]], is that platform research needs a conceptual reorientation — from isolated causal-effect studies toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure, and from static exposure measures toward the networked, hybrid, and temporally dynamic nature of political communication. The open question, raised most sharply by [[Bruns2026-pn]], [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] (the DSA-era APIs-and-tools survey), and [[Lukito2026-nb]], is whether legally mandated access — DSA Article 40, clean rooms, research APIs — will actually be *usable*, equitable, and durable, or whether it merely formalizes a new, more bureaucratically legitimated version of platform gatekeeping. The consensus emerging across this literature is that mandates alone are not enough: the manner, infrastructure, and politics of access must themselves become objects of sustained empirical scrutiny, alongside whatever substantive questions about platforms and society researchers hope to answer.
