# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## The Shape of the Field

This body of work sits at the convergence of two long-standing but increasingly entangled projects: building computational machinery to detect structure in networks (embeddings, community detection, coordination-detection pipelines) and using that machinery to explain why online publics fragment, polarize, or cohere the way they do. The papers range from pure methods contributions — new architectures and formalisms for finding clusters or quantifying influence — to empirical studies that apply graph-analytic tools to specific political, extremist, or platform-governance questions. Read together, they trace an arc from *how do we measure structure* to *what does structure tell us about power, authenticity, and publics*.

## Scaling and Reimagining Community Detection

At the technical core is a push to make community/ cluster detection tractable and meaningful at platform scale. [[Ferrara2026-io]] directly engineers around the "Semantic Wall" (over-smoothing) and "Systems Wall" (O(N²) memory) that have limited self-supervised GNNs, using a topology-aware router and chunked similarity extraction to reach million-node attributed graphs like Pokec. [[Bruns2025-fz]] approaches the same representational problem from a different angle, proposing "practice mapping" via vector embeddings of network actions as an alternative to hairball visualizations, explicitly aiming to integrate multimodal interaction types into a single analytic space — a concern echoed architecturally in [[Mannocci2025-ig]], which systematically benchmarks five ways of operationalizing *multimodal* coordination (monomodal, flattened, multiplex) and finds multiplex community detection the most balanced compromise between integration and inclusiveness. [[Gerard2025-br]] pushes further, replacing interaction-based ties altogether with latent narrative-cluster affiliation (CANE/t-CANE), reconstructing cross-platform discourse networks from shared topical alignment rather than reposts or follows — a move that recovers "bridge users" invisible to conventional graph construction. Together these four papers stake out a methodological frontier: structure detection must handle heterogeneity (topology, modality, time, platform) that classical community detection assumed away.

## Coordination, Causality, and the Limits of Detection

A dense cluster of papers treats *coordinated behavior* as a special, adversarial case of community structure, and increasingly asks not just "can we detect it" but "does it matter." [[Mannocci2026-kc]] offers the synthesizing survey here, reconciling fragmented industry/academic definitions into a framework of actors, actions, and intent, and mapping the methodological landscape (co-action networks, similarity functions, time windows, Louvain/Leiden clustering) that the other coordination papers instantiate. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Yang2025-iv]] advance detection itself — respectively through temporal-multiplex collaboration models resistant to modality-dilution tactics, and through statistical regularities in sharing speed/frequency that resist trivial timing manipulation — while [[Minici2024-tf]] reframes detection as a graph-foundation-model problem, fusing language and structural signals for cross-campaign generalization. [[Luceri2025-tr]] extends this detection agenda into TikTok's video-first ecosystem, arguing that text-centric methods don't transfer cleanly. But a second, more skeptical strand — [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and its companion [[Di_Marco2026-xu]] — pivots from detection to *causal/counterfactual quantification* of influence, formalizing coordinated-account placement in cascade trees and finding that real-world coordinated accounts are far less influential than optimal or even random placement would predict. This is a significant corrective within the topic: it suggests the field's detection successes may have outpaced its understanding of actual causal impact, a tension that [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]'s billion-post diffusion-tree analysis of misinformation on Facebook implicitly tests at even larger scale.

## Platforms as Structuring Agents

Several papers treat the platform itself — its algorithm, interface, or moderation regime — as the causal engine shaping network structure, rather than treating structure as an emergent property of user choice alone. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] provides the field's theoretical keystone claim: TikTok and TikTok-like systems replace person-centric "networked publics" with algorithmically inferred "clustered publics," a shift toward item-centric, opaque, similarity-based grouping. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] operationalizes a version of this empirically, showing via paired algorithmic/chronological feeds that Twitter's recommender centralizes visibility around Elon Musk and rewards agitating content, with political-leaning effects better explained by behavior than ideology per se — a causal-network audit of algorithmic curation. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] demonstrates that the same actor set (German far-right accounts) produces structurally distinct networks across five platforms, arguing that affordances are relational outcomes of design and practice rather than fixed properties — directly extending Gerbaudo's and Efstratiou's platform-as-structuring-force thesis into a comparative, multi-platform register. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] extend the governance angle into moderation infrastructure itself, showing how moderator allocation and ranking/removal practices (unevenly distributed across languages, inconsistently labeled) materially shape which structures and voices become visible at all — a precondition for any downstream community-detection analysis.

## Polarization, Fragmentation, and the Failure of Bridging Spaces

A substantial empirical cluster uses network overlap and audience-sharing methods to interrogate whether cross-ideological "town squares" exist. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] is paradigmatic: across 11 political subreddits, overlap in users, sources, and cross-posting is minimal even between discursively similar communities, leading to the "sedimentation of polarisation" thesis — polarization as entangled precondition rather than discursive outcome. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] extend this overlap-network logic to alternative/mainstream media ecosystems, finding that structural isolation is context-dependent (shaped by media systems and populist parties' institutional access) and that entity-level overlap can coexist with relational divergence in how actors are networked. The foundational methodological lineage behind all of this is [[Barbera2015-fw]] and [[Barbera2015-je]], whose Twitter-based ideal-point and homophily methods established that network structure (following, retweeting) can recover valid ideological positions and that echo-chamber intensity is topic- and actor-dependent — a baseline against which [[Eady2025-vm]]'s URL-sharing ideology model and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]]'s multi-level "political cartography" of news circulation both position themselves as methodological refinements addressing oversimplified, outlet-only measures of political leaning.

## Transnational and Extremist Network Formation

A final thread applies these network-mapping tools to trace how extremist or hyperpartisan actors build cohesion across borders and platforms. [[Heft2021-ky]] and [[Groebner2026-pc]] both use hyperlink/commenter-overlap networks to show right-wing media forming transnational hubs (the US as centralizing node in both cases), while [[Askanius2026-de]] and [[Tuters2026-vo]] move from interlinking structure to practice: the former mapping guest-appearance/hyperlink/mention networks among Swedish far-right YouTubers to show hybridization of activism and influencer commerce, the latter using co-hashtag networks to argue that conspiratorial alignment around the "Great Reset" is produced by patterned platform circulation preceding narrative coherence — extending the "structure before meaning" logic that runs throughout this topic's more theoretical papers.

## Migration, Time, and the Durability of Structure

Finally, two papers interrogate the temporal fragility of social-network structure itself. [[Wang2026-ub]] shows that even densely interconnected, motivated communities (academic Twitter migrants) fail to sustain structure on a new platform absent diverse cross-server engagement — network cohesion alone does not guarantee persistence. [[Fan2026-af]] generalizes this temporal concern methodologically, arguing that communication research has under-used the fine-grained timestamps in digital trace data, and demonstrating via six computational approaches (sequence analysis, HMMs, network analysis, process mining, embeddings) that cross-platform behavioral "routines" are themselves a structural phenomenon worth modeling directly — a fitting closing move for a topic whose unifying thread is that structure in online social networks is never simply given by ties, but is actively produced, contested, and made visible (or invisible) by the computational methods used to find it.
