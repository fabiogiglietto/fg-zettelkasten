# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## Scaling embeddings to detect emergent structure

At the methodological core of this topic sits a family of papers asking how to detect communities and practice-clusters in networks that are too large, too dense, or too multimodal for classical modularity methods. [[Ferrara2026-io]] is the most explicit about the engineering problem: self-supervised GNNs for attributed community detection hit a "Semantic Wall" (over-smoothing on dense/heterophilic graphs) and a "Systems Wall" (O(N²) pairwise similarity costs), and ECHO's routing architecture and chunked InfoNCE objective are designed specifically to push detection to million-node attributed graphs like Pokec. This same impulse—to represent heterogeneous social action in a single tractable structure—animates [[Bruns2025-fz]], which proposes "practice mapping," using vector embeddings of multimodal interactions to escape the "hairball" problem of conventional network visualization, and [[Gerard2025-br]], whose CANE/t-CANE framework abandons platform-specific interaction signals altogether in favor of embedding users as distributions over latent narrative clusters, enabling cross-platform discourse network reconstruction even where direct ties (reposts, follows) are unobservable. Together these three papers trace an arc from infrastructure-level scaling (ECHO) to representational reframing of what a "tie" even is (practice mapping, CANE), each treating community structure as something to be learned from behavioral or semantic similarity rather than hand-coded from interaction counts.

## From monomodal signals to multiplex coordination

A second cluster asks how structural methods can detect *deliberate* coordination rather than organic community formation. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] extends Newman's collaboration model with a temporal decay kernel and a multiplex (multi-layer) representation across hashtags, retweets, mentions, and URLs, arguing that neither temporal nor modal information alone suffices to separate coordination from coincidence. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] interrogates this move more systematically, benchmarking five ways of operationalizing multimodal coordination (monomodal, independent layers, union flattening, multiplex detection, intersection flattening) and showing a fundamental trade-off between integration strength and inclusiveness—with multiplex community detection (echoing Iannucci's architecture) emerging as the best-balanced compromise, since naive flattening, the field's dominant practice, discards centrally important nodes. Both papers converge on a shared methodological lesson: treating modality as a *layer* rather than collapsing it preserves structural nuance that aggregation destroys, a lesson with obvious kinship to the embedding-based approaches above, which likewise resist premature aggregation of heterogeneous signals into a single edge type.

## Does coordination matter? Causal evaluation of influence

Having established how to detect coordinated structure, a tightly linked pair of papers asks a more skeptical, causal question: once detected, how much does coordination actually matter for diffusion? [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] develops a graph-theoretic framework for bounding the influence a labeled subset of nodes can exert on an information cascade, comparing real coordinated accounts in UK election retweet trees against optimal and greedy placements. The finding—that real CIB accounts achieve influence far below strategic benchmarks and statistically resemble random placement—pushes back against narratives of coordinated accounts as decisive diffusion engines. [[Di_Marco2026-xu]] generalizes this post-hoc evaluation apparatus to both cascade and retweet-network formulations, explicitly reframing the field's priorities: detection has been overstudied relative to quantifying downstream causal impact. Read together with the multiplex-detection cluster, these two papers complete a natural pipeline—detect structure, then ask whether that structure is causally consequential—that is largely absent from the rest of the literature reviewed here, which tends to treat detected structure as self-evidently meaningful.

## Mapping political and alternative-media ecosystems

A large empirical cluster applies network and hyperlink methods to map how far-right, alternative, and partisan media ecosystems cohere across platforms and borders. [[Askanius2026-de]] traces a Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube through guest appearances, hyperlinks, and in-video mentions, finding high internal connectivity and transnational linkage to Anglophone and German far-right actors. [[Heft2021-ky]] examines the same connective logic at larger scale and earlier in time, showing that right-wing news sites across six countries build interlinked domestic and transnational ecologies with the U.S. as a structural hub—while, counterintuitively, legacy media remain the primary *shared* external reference point, undercutting the insulated-echo-chamber narrative. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] complicates the picture further by showing that the *same* set of far-right actors produces markedly different network topologies across five platforms, arguing that affordances (connectivity, replicability, scalability) are relational outcomes of platform design meeting situated practice rather than fixed properties—an argument that resonates with Askanius's finding that YouTube functions as a comparatively "sanitised" gateway relative to alt-tech platforms. Audience- and content-level analogues extend this mapping logic beyond actor networks: [[Zhu2026-tn]] builds chance-corrected audience-overlap networks across six European countries to show that alternative media's structural marginality is contingent on media-system type and populist parties' institutional access, while [[Balluff2026-ev]] constructs named-entity co-occurrence networks to show that alternative and legacy German outlets reference overlapping actors but organize them into more cohesive, less modular relational structures. Across this cluster, the recurring finding is that apparent ideological separateness is a structural *variable*, not a constant—shaped by platform affordances, national media systems, and political access, rather than being an intrinsic property of "alternative" media as a category.

## Extending network inference to ideology, time, and artificial agents

Several papers push structural network methods into adjacent inferential territory. [[Lai2024-to]] shows that latent ideology estimation, long applied to legislators and users, can be extended to video-level content by using Reddit cross-posting behavior as a correspondence-analysis substrate—effectively inferring ideological position from the community structure of who shares what. [[Fan2026-af]] moves orthogonally, arguing that cross-sectional network snapshots miss the temporal order of interaction; its survey of sequence analysis, event-history models, HMMs, and process mining (alongside network analysis proper, which it uses to show how platforms interconnect via transition modularity) makes the case that structural methods need a temporal companion to capture path-dependent practice, a concern that echoes the decay-kernel weighting in [[Iannucci2025-eg]]. Finally, [[Ng2026-og]] generalizes the entire topic's logic one step further, arguing that the same structural regularities studied here—network-constrained dependence, co-evolving node/edge dynamics, distributional instability—should be treated as *priors* for agentic AI systems, and demonstrates all four empirically in a 2.1M-post AI social network. This paper functions as a coda to the set: if community structure, coordinated practice, and transitive influence are measurable regularities of human social networks, the same computational toolkit—and the same causal caution urged by the CIB-influence papers—will be needed as non-human agents begin populating comparable network structures themselves.
