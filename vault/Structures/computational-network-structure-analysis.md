# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## Overview

The papers filed here converge on a single methodological wager: that the "shape" of online sociality — who clusters with whom, which ties transmit influence, and how coordination or polarization congeal into structure — can be recovered computationally from trace data, and that doing so changes what we think social media *is*. Read together, they trace an arc from infrastructure-level theorizing about what kind of "social" platforms now produce, through concrete graph-learning and network-science methods for detecting structure, to applied studies that use these methods to map ideological fragmentation, coordinated manipulation, and transnational media ecosystems.

## From networked publics to clustered and algorithmic publics

[[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] sets the conceptual frame for much of this topic: it argues that TikTok-style platforms have replaced explicit, people-centric "networked publics" with algorithmically inferred, item-centric "clustered publics," reorienting the very object that network analysis is meant to capture — no longer interpersonal ties but statistically constructed interest neighborhoods. This reframing resonates with [[Ng2026-og]], which goes further and asks whether *agentic AI* populations (not just human users) require social-theoretic structural priors — network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability — to be understood at all, using an AI social network as an empirical proof of concept. Both papers insist that structure is not incidental to platforms but constitutive of their sociality, a claim empirically tested in [[Wang2026-ub]]'s account of academic Twitter's failed migration to Mastodon, where dense internal connectivity among coordinated adopters proved insufficient to sustain a community once the platform's affordances (discoverability, server structure, federation) changed.

## Methods for detecting structure at scale

A cluster of technically oriented papers develops the computational toolkit itself. [[Ferrara2026-io]] (ECHO) and [[Minici2024-tf]] (IOHunter) push self-supervised graph representation learning toward million-node, attributed, and cross-campaign generalization, addressing scalability and semantic bottlenecks that have limited GNN-based community detection. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes a complementary embedding-based alternative to conventional network visualization — "practice mapping" — explicitly to escape the "hairball" problem that dense multimodal interaction graphs produce, a concern also central to [[Mannocci2025-ig]] and [[Iannucci2025-eg]], which systematically compare monoplex, flattened, and multiplex representations of coordinated action and show that preserving modality-specific structure (rather than collapsing it) better recovers genuine coordination. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] synthesizes this entire subfield into a survey, offering a general definition of coordinated online behavior (actors, synergic actions, intent) and taxonomizing detection pipelines — co-action networks, similarity functions, community discovery via Louvain/Leiden — that the other coordination papers instantiate. [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE/t-CANE framework extends this logic across platforms, modeling users not by interaction but by shared participation in latent narrative clusters, which lets it identify small sets of "bridge users" who structurally carry narratives between fragmented platforms like Truth Social and X — a structural analogue to classical brokerage theory.

## Applying structural methods to polarization, coordination, and the far right

A second cluster applies these graph-analytic tools to substantive questions of political fragmentation. [[Barbera2015-fw]] is the foundational precedent here, using follower-network embeddings to show that ideological segregation on Twitter is topic- and time-contingent rather than fixed — a finding extended and complicated by [[Dehghan2026-sy]], whose multi-indicator analysis of political subreddits finds structurally "sedimented" polarization that is irreducible to discourse alone and resists any "town square" reading of platforms like r/politics. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] bring comparative and entity-network methods to alternative media, showing that audience overlap and referential overlap with the mainstream are context-dependent rather than universally low — alternative media are structurally entangled with legacy media in ways that depend on national media systems and populist parties' institutional access. [[Heft2021-ky]] extends this to hyperlink networks among right-wing news sites, finding transnational integration concentrated around U.S. hubs, while [[Groebner2026-pc]] and [[Askanius2026-de]] use co-commenting and guest-appearance/hyperlink networks respectively to map transnational and cross-actor far-right ecosystems on YouTube, both finding dense, strategically cultivated connective structures rather than isolated national silos. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] shows that the same far-right actor set produces starkly different network topologies depending on platform affordances, reinforcing the methodological point — shared across [[Gaisbauer2025-by]]'s multi-level "political cartography" proposal — that structure must be measured at multiple levels (story, outlet, content, platform) rather than assumed to transfer.

## Infrastructure, data access, and temporal structure

Underlying all this empirical work is a methodological dependency on what platforms allow researchers to see. [[Cullen2026-cb]] makes this reflexive turn explicit, showing how CrowdTangle's API shaped not just data access but the very questions and communities of practice that network researchers could form — an epistemic-infrastructure argument that complements [[Fan2026-af]]'s push to recover temporal, sequence-level structure (via HMMs, process mining, and sequence embeddings) from digital trace data, arguing that cross-sectional network snapshots miss the path-dependent, rhythmic structure of cross-platform behavior. Finally, [[Tuters2026-vo]] closes the arc by showing how patterned network adjacency itself — co-hashtag circulation and modular reuse — can produce conspiratorial meaning *before* any coherent narrative exists, suggesting that the structural methods surveyed throughout this topic are not just analytic tools imposed from outside but increasingly part of the vernacular interpretive apparatus users themselves bring to reading platform-produced structure.
