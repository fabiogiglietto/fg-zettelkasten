# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

This cluster traces an arc from method development—new architectures for detecting community and coordination structure at scale—toward increasingly causal and mechanistic accounts of *why* those structures emerge, and finally toward applications that use structural mapping to diagnose the health and fragmentation of specific platform ecosystems.

### Scaling structure discovery

At the technical core sits [[Ferrara2026-io]], which tackles the two bottlenecks that have kept self-supervised graph neural approaches from matching classical topological algorithms (Louvain, Infomap) at scale: semantic over-smoothing on heterophilic graphs and the O(N²) memory wall of pairwise similarity clustering. Its routed encoder architecture and chunked InfoNCE objective let attributed community detection run on million-node graphs like Pokec, explicitly reviving the multi-scale philosophy of generalized Louvain methods but replacing static heuristics with learned diffusion. [[Bruns2025-fz]] approaches the same representational problem—how to make sense of large, multimodal interaction graphs—from the opposite end: rather than optimizing an embedding objective for a benchmark, it proposes "practice mapping," using vector embeddings of user actions to escape the "hairball" problem of conventional network visualization and to let multiple interaction modalities (likes, replies, shares) be integrated into a single interpretable map of user *practices* rather than raw connections. Together these two papers frame the paper's central methodological wager: that embedding-based, self-supervised representations can recover structure that edge-counting and modularity maximization alone cannot.

### Multimodality and coordination as network problems

A second methodological thread asks what "community" even means once interaction is multimodal and adversarial. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] systematically compares five ways of operationalizing multimodal coordination (monomodal, independent layers, union flattening, multiplex detection, intersection flattening) and shows that the dominant practice—flattening layers into a single network—silently discards central nodes and communities; multiplex community detection via Generalized Louvain instead preserves monomodal signal while surfacing genuinely cross-modal coordination. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] extends this multiplex logic temporally, weighting co-actions by an exponential decay kernel atop Newman's collaboration model, arguing that neither frequency nor timing alone suffices to separate deliberate coordination from coincidental synchrony. [[Gerard2025-br]] pushes multimodality further still, replacing platform-specific interaction signals (retweets, follows) with shared participation in latent narrative clusters, yielding a "Cluster Affiliation Network Embedding" that reconstructs discourse networks across platforms where interaction data is unavailable or meaningless (e.g., across Truth Social and X). Its identification of a tiny population of narrative "bridge users" who seed most cross-platform diffusion echoes the brokerage themes running through the whole topic, and shows embedding-based network reconstruction paying off in a substantive finding about information ecosystems rather than a benchmark score.

### From structure to cause

A distinct but connected strand asks whether observed structural patterns—transitive triads, influential placements—are causally consequential or largely epiphenomenal. [[Smith2025-kc]] offers the topic's clearest causal-inference contribution: using a difference-in-differences design around a novel exploit of API follower-event data, it shows that amplification by high-degree "attention brokers" (the *tertius amplificans*) causally accelerates the very triad-closure process that community-detection methods later observe as an emergent statistical regularity—linking micro-level exposure mechanisms to the macro-level tendency toward transitivity that pervades directed social graphs. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] interrogates a related question from a graph-theoretic optimization angle: given labeled coordinated accounts in real retweet cascades, how much influence *could* they have exerted under optimal or greedy placement, versus how much they actually exert? Finding real coordinated placements statistically indistinguishable from random assignment, and far from optimal, the paper offers a skeptical counterpoint to alarmed narratives about coordinated inauthentic behavior, using the same formal cascade/tree structures that Iannucci and Mannocci mine for detection to instead bound the *causal ceiling* of coordination's effect.

### Structure meets substance: platforms, polarization, and publics

The remaining papers apply structural or relational methods to diagnose specific ecosystems, and collectively trace how network-analytic findings reshape understanding of online fragmentation. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] provides the topic's broadest theoretical frame, arguing that TikTok-style algorithmic curation replaces explicit "networked publics" with implicit "clustered publics"—a shift that reframes what community detection is even detecting once follower ties become secondary to statistically inferred interest neighborhoods. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]] both use overlap-network methods to puncture optimistic readings of cross-ideological contact: Dehghan et al. find minimal user, source, and discourse overlap across political subreddits even between ostensibly aligned communities, arguing polarization is "sedimented" as a structural precondition rather than emergent from discourse alone; Zhu et al. show, via chance-corrected audience-overlap backbones across six countries, that alternative-mainstream media overlap is generally low but strongly conditioned by media-system type and populist parties' institutional access—demonstrating that network position is contextually contingent rather than an intrinsic property of "alternativeness." [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates the alternative/mainstream divide further by showing, through named-entity co-occurrence networks, that the two media types reference largely the same actors but organize them into differently shaped (more cohesive vs. more modular) relational structures—recasting the community-detection question as one of relational topology rather than membership.

[[Green2025-ap]] and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] push structural analysis down to the level of individual stories rather than sources or users, showing that networked curation (who follows whom, who shares what) produces partisan "curation bubbles" invisible to domain-level partisanship scores—a finding that resonates with this topic's broader argument that coarse-grained network summaries (single-modality graphs, source-level scores) systematically mask the finer relational structure that multiplex, embedding-based, or story-level methods can recover. [[Askanius2026-de]] offers a qualitatively-grounded network case study in the same spirit, mapping a far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube through guest appearances, hyperlinks, and mentions to show how practice-level connective tissue (rather than simple following ties) sustains a cohesive extremist ecosystem—directly paralleling Bruns's practice-mapping logic and Gerard's discourse-cluster logic in a different empirical register. [[Fan2026-af]] rounds out the empirical set by shifting the object of analysis from cross-sectional network snapshots to time-ordered user-sequences, reviewing sequence analysis, HMMs, process mining, and embedding models as complementary lenses on the same underlying problem this topic poses throughout: how to represent hyper-longitudinal, multimodal digital-trace data without collapsing away the temporal and relational structure that drives emergent behavior.

Finally, [[Ng2026-og]] gestures toward where this line of inquiry may be heading: formalizing multi-agent social systems (among AI agents rather than humans) as a triple of information-exchange, influence-dynamics, and interaction-network functions, and empirically grounding structural priors like network-constrained dependence and co-evolution in a large AI social network. It suggests that the graph-theoretic and causal tools developed across this topic—for detecting communities, tracing coordination, and identifying causal brokerage—are not specific to human social media but describe a more general class of networked social systems, human or artificial, whose emergent structure this whole body of work is trying to characterize.
