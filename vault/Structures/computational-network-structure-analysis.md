# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## Methodological core: scaling community detection and representation learning

At the technical center of this topic sits a cluster of papers pushing self-supervised and graph-theoretic methods toward the scale and heterogeneity of real platforms. [[Ferrara2026-io]] is paradigmatic: ECHO explicitly names the two bottlenecks that have limited GNN-based community detection—over-smoothing in dense/heterophilic graphs (the "Semantic Wall") and the O(N²) memory cost of pairwise similarity clustering (the "Systems Wall")—and resolves them with a topology-aware router, diffusion, and chunked similarity extraction, pushing attributed community detection to million-node graphs like Pokec. This sits in direct lineage with classical modularity-based community detection (Louvain, Leiden, Infomap) while absorbing the self-supervised contrastive-learning toolkit (DGI, MVGRL, GraphCL). [[Bruns2025-fz]] approaches the same representational problem from a more interpretive angle, proposing "practice mapping"—vector embeddings of multimodal network actions—as a way past the "hairball" problem of conventional SNA visualization, anticipating the move from discrete tie-based graphs to continuous embedding spaces that recurs throughout this literature.

## Coordinated behavior: from detection to causal evaluation of influence

A dense sub-cluster addresses coordinated inauthentic behavior (CIB) as a network-structural problem. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] provides the survey scaffolding: it reconciles fragmented industry/academic definitions into a framework of actors, synergic actions, and intent, and catalogs the dominant technique—building latent "coordination networks" from co-actions (co-retweet, co-hashtag, co-URL, etc.) and applying community discovery (Louvain/Leiden) to flattened or multiplex representations. The papers around it operationalize and extend this framework. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] systematically compares five ways of handling multimodality (monomodal, independent layers, union flattening, multiplex detection, intersection flattening) on UK election data, finding a basic trade-off between integration strength and inclusiveness, with multiplex Generalized Louvain emerging as the most balanced choice. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] adds a temporal dimension, weighting co-actions with an exponential decay kernel atop a multiplex architecture, showing that neither modality nor time alone suffices to separate deliberate coordination from coincidence. [[Minici2024-tf]] moves toward foundation-model generalization, fusing language-model and graph-neural signals (IOHunter) to detect IO drivers across heterogeneous state-sponsored campaigns, including out-of-distribution settings—an important bridge between network-structural and content-based detection. [[Luceri2025-tr]] extends this detection apparatus to a genuinely different substrate, arguing that TikTok's video-first ecosystem breaks assumptions baked into text-centric coordination network methods and requires adapted user-similarity constructions.

Crucially, a second pair of papers interrogates what detected coordination actually *does*. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and its companion [[Di_Marco2026-xu]] reframe the question from detection to causal/counterfactual influence, modeling cascades as trees and asking how much sway a labeled set of coordinated nodes exerts relative to optimal and random placements. Their finding—that real coordinated accounts in UK election retweet cascades behave statistically more like random placements than strategic ones, and exert far less influence than assumed—constitutes an important corrective note within the broader coordination-detection literature, suggesting that structural presence of coordination (which the detection methods above are built to find) does not automatically imply outsized causal effect on diffusion.

## Platforms as covert community-makers: embeddings, recommenders, and clustered publics

A second axis examines how platform-internal computational structures—rather than researcher-built graphs—themselves produce emergent communities. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] reverse-engineers X's recommender embedding space from donated "Who to Follow" data and shows that a single linear direction encodes users' Left-Right ideology with striking fidelity, independent of demographics, and demonstrates that this encoding can be surgically removed via orthogonal projection—an empirical confirmation of the linear representation hypothesis inside a real-world large-scale system, and a direct, practical counterpart to ECHO's model-building concerns about what embeddings actually capture. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes this shift at the level of platform logic: TikTok's "For You" algorithm reorganizes "networked publics" built on explicit ties into "clustered publics" built on inferred behavioral similarity, with follower count becoming a weak predictor of reach—effectively describing, in sociological terms, the same embedding-driven clustering that Bouchaud et al. document empirically for X. [[Gerard2025-br]] develops a method (CANE/t-CANE) that operationalizes this insight methodologically: rather than relying on platform-specific interaction signals, it reconstructs cross-platform discourse networks from shared latent narrative-cluster membership, identifying a tiny set of structurally pivotal "bridge users" who seed cross-platform narrative migration between Truth Social and X—showing that discursive/topical embedding spaces can reveal connective structures invisible to classical tie-based graphs. [[Brown2026-br]] complements this by auditing YouTube's recommender directly, disentangling algorithmic from user-driven contributions to echo chambers, rabbit holes, and radicalization pathways, finding strong content-adjacency effects (rabbit holes) but no evidence that the algorithm independently manufactures ideological echo chambers.

## Applied mappings of ideological and alternative media networks

A substantial empirical wing applies these network methods to map concrete communities of practice and belief, especially on the political right. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] examines political subreddits and finds persistently low cross-ideological overlap in users, sources, and cross-posting—"sedimented" polarization that is irreducible to discourse or platform design alone. [[Askanius2026-de]], [[Kakavand2026-kt]], and [[Groebner2026-pc]] each map far-right networks through different connective practices: guest appearances, hyperlinks, and mentions for Swedish YouTube influencers; platform-specific affordance-shaped ties (retweets, forwards, subscriptions) across five platforms in Germany, showing that the same actor set produces structurally distinct networks on each platform; and sustained transnational commenter overlap welding together English-language hyperpartisan channels across four countries, with the US positioned centrally. [[Heft2021-ky]] extends the transnational-hyperlink approach to a six-country comparison of right-wing news sites, finding domestically dense but thinly transnational linking, with the US acting as hub and—counterintuitively—legacy media as the chief shared external reference point. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] push into audience- and entity-network territory: the former builds chance-corrected audience-overlap networks across six European countries to show that alternative media's structural isolation is contingent on media-system type and populist parties' institutional access; the latter builds named-entity co-occurrence networks around Nord Stream 2 coverage, finding that alternative and legacy media reference largely the same actors but organize them into differently shaped (more cohesive vs. more modular) relational structures. [[Tuters2026-vo]] offers a more infrastructural-theoretical take, using co-hashtag network analysis of the "Great Reset" meme complex to argue that conspiratorial alignment is produced by patterned platform circulation itself, prior to any coherent shared narrative—an account that resonates with Gerbaudo's "clustered publics" thesis but applied to the formation of conspiracist assemblages rather than taste communities.

## Infrastructure and generalization

Two outlying papers address, respectively, the empirical and theoretical infrastructure underpinning this entire research program. [[Zheng2026-bi]] tackles the basic denominator problem behind most studies above: without representative samples of YouTube or TikTok, network and content analyses risk systematically oversampling popular, algorithmically amplified material, and TubeStats/TokStats are offered as corrective, reproducible sampling infrastructure. [[Ng2026-og]] generalizes the topic's concerns beyond human networks altogether, arguing that agentic AI populations (exemplified by the MoltBook AI social network) exhibit the same structural regularities documented throughout this topic—heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability—and that social-theoretic and network-analytic priors, rather than single-agent alignment paradigms, should govern how we model emergent community structure in artificial as well as human social systems. Together with ECHO's architecture-level innovations, this suggests the topic's methods (graph embeddings, coordination detection, structural audits) are becoming a general toolkit applicable wherever large populations of interacting agents—human or synthetic—generate emergent structure.
