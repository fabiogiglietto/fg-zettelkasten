# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## Reframing the object: publics as clusters, not networks

The starting provocation for this cluster of papers is [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]'s claim that the very object of social network analysis has shifted. Where first-generation platforms organized "networked publics" around explicit, visible interpersonal ties (following, friending), TikTok-style recommender systems produce "clustered publics": statistically inferred neighborhoods of behavioral similarity that are opaque, item-centric, and algorithmically assigned rather than chosen. This reframing sets the agenda for much of what follows: if community structure is increasingly latent and behaviorally inferred rather than declared through follow-graphs, then computational methods must be able to recover structure from indirect, multimodal, and often adversarially obscured signals — which is exactly the methodological problem the rest of this topic addresses.

## Scaling structural discovery: embeddings and practice maps

Two papers tackle the base infrastructural problem of *how* to detect community structure at scale. [[Ferrara2026-io]] (ECHO) targets the technical bottlenecks of self-supervised graph learning for attributed networks — over-smoothing in dense/heterophilic graphs and the O(N²) memory wall of pairwise similarity clustering — solving them with a topology-aware router that switches between isolating and densifying encoders, attention-guided diffusion, and chunked similarity extraction, achieving million-node throughput on real social graphs like Pokec. This is a direct technical answer to the scale and heterogeneity problem that Gerbaudo's "clustered publics" implies: recovering community structure that is not organized around explicit ties. [[Bruns2025-fz]] approaches the same representational problem from a different angle, proposing "practice mapping" — vector embeddings of user actions across multiple interaction modes — as a way past the classic "hairball" problem of network visualization, integrating multimodal interaction into a single map rather than a tangle of edges. Together, ECHO and practice mapping represent the two poles of current computational strategy: deep self-supervised architectures optimized for scale versus embedding-based practice representations optimized for interpretability and multimodal integration.

## Detecting coordination: multiplex, temporal, and causal approaches

A dense cluster of papers extends these representational problems to the specific case of coordinated inauthentic behavior, where the community structure to be detected is deliberately obscured. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] extends Newman's collaboration model with a time-aware exponential decay kernel and multiplex layering across hashtags, retweets, mentions, and URLs, arguing that both temporal proximity and modality-specific structure are necessary to separate deliberate coordination from coincidental synchrony. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] systematizes this insight into a comparative framework of five multimodal operationalizations, showing a fundamental trade-off between integration strength and inclusiveness, and identifying multiplex community detection (rather than the field's default practice of flattening layers) as the most balanced solution — a methodological argument that directly reinforces Iannucci et al.'s multiplex design choice. [[Luceri2025-tr]] pushes this line into a new modality entirely, adapting network-based similarity detection to TikTok's video-first ecosystem and arguing that text-centric CIB methods do not transfer cleanly to platforms built on non-textual content.

Against this detection-focused literature, [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] asks a complementary causal question: even where coordinated accounts are identified, how much influence do they actually exert on cascade structure? Using optimal and greedy influence-placement benchmarks on retweet trees, the paper finds that real coordinated accounts' placement resembles random assignment far more than strategic optimality, suggesting coordination detection and coordination *efficacy* are separate empirical questions — a useful corrective to assumptions embedded in the detection-focused papers above.

## From association to causal mechanism: triad transitivity

[[Smith2025-kc]] shifts the topic from detecting existing structure to explaining *why* particular structural patterns (specifically, triad transitivity in directed networks) emerge in the first place. Introducing the "attention broker" (tertius amplificans) as an extension of Obstfeld's tertius iungens, the paper uses a difference-in-differences design on Twitter/X follower events to causally show that amplification by high-degree accounts accelerates followers' tie formation to the amplified account, beyond background virality. This is the topic's clearest instance of causal network analysis proper — complementing the largely correlational or descriptive community-detection work elsewhere in the set by offering a micro-mechanism for a well-documented macro-structural regularity, and gesturing toward generalizability across any many-to-many attribution system (citation networks, duets, corporate forwarding).

## Cross-platform and ideological community structure

A second major thread applies these structural methods to map how communities cohere — or fail to cohere — around political and ideological content, often across fragmented platform ecosystems. [[Gerard2025-br]] develops CANE/t-CANE, modeling users as distributions over latent narrative clusters rather than direct interaction ties, enabling platform-agnostic discourse network reconstruction; applied to Truth Social and X, it identifies a tiny population of "bridge users" responsible for most cross-platform narrative migration, formalizing brokerage as a structural, discourse-based rather than interaction-based phenomenon — a natural complement to Smith2025-kc's brokerage mechanism at the level of narratives rather than dyadic ties.

Several papers use overlap and co-occurrence networks to interrogate the boundary between "alternative" and "mainstream" or cross-ideological spaces. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] finds minimal cross-ideological overlap across users, sources, and discourse in political subreddits, arguing polarization is "sedimented" as a structural precondition rather than an emergent outcome, and explicitly critiquing platform-centric, discourse-only, and design-only explanations in favor of an entangled account. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] extend this comparative logic to alternative media: Zhu2026-tn's cross-country audience overlap networks show that alternative media's structural marginality is shaped by media-system type and populist parties' institutional access, while Balluff2026-ev's named-entity co-occurrence networks around Nord Stream 2 coverage find substantial entity overlap between alternative and legacy German media, with the real divergence lying in relational structure (transitivity, modularity) rather than referenced actors — both papers converge on the finding that "alternativeness" is a relational, context-dependent network property rather than a fixed content attribute.

Far-right digital ecosystems receive sustained network-analytic treatment across platforms and countries. [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a cohesive Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube through guest appearances, hyperlinks, and mentions, showing hybridization of activist and commercial-influencer logics. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] compares German far-right networks across five platforms, arguing that the same actors produce structurally distinct networks depending on platform affordances (connectivity, replicability, scalability), which complicates any single-platform community-detection exercise. [[Groebner2026-pc]] extends this to a transnational register, using commenter co-occurrence networks across four English-language far-right YouTube channels to demonstrate a pre-existing, US-centered transnational audience structure. [[Lai2024-to]] supplies a complementary measurement tool for this literature, using Reddit cross-posting behavior to estimate latent ideological position for YouTube videos via correspondence analysis — a technique that could in principle underpin the ideological classification steps used implicitly in several of the papers above. Relatedly, [[Brown2026-br]] audits YouTube's recommendation algorithm directly, using randomized seed videos to separate algorithmic from user-driven contributions to apparent "echo chamber" and "rabbit hole" structures, finding the latter but not the former — a useful causal counterpoint to purely observational network-overlap studies of ideological clustering.

## Temporal dynamics and generative extensions

[[Fan2026-af]] broadens the topic's temporal ambitions beyond coordination detection, proposing a general framework of computational methods (sequence analysis, HMMs, network analysis, process mining, embeddings) for mining time-stamped "user-sequences" across platforms, and empirically showing platform-specific behavioral routines and cross-platform transition dynamics — a methodological toolkit that resonates with the temporal kernels used in [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and the diffusion timing central to [[Gerard2025-br]] and [[Smith2025-kc]].

Finally, [[Ng2026-og]] gestures at where this line of work may be heading: applying social-theoretic structural priors (heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) to multi-agent AI systems, empirically validated on a large AI social network. It suggests that the graph-based, causal, and embedding methods developed across this topic — originally built to understand human community formation — are already being repurposed to model and govern emergent structure among artificial agents, closing the loop between computational social network analysis and its next object of study.
