# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## From Networked to Clustered Publics: Reframing the Object of Study

The structure note opens conceptually with [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]], which argues that algorithmic platforms like TikTok have replaced the explicit, people-centric "networked publics" of first-generation social media with implicit, item-centric "clustered publics" assembled from behavioral similarity rather than declared ties. This reframing matters methodologically: if communities are now statistically inferred neighborhoods rather than visible graphs of friendship, then community-detection techniques must themselves become probes into platform logic, not just descriptive tools. [[Bruns2025-fz]] responds to a cognate problem from the opposite direction, arguing that conventional network visualization collapses into an uninterpretable "hairball" when interactions are multimodal, and proposing "practice mapping" via vector embeddings of user actions as a way to recover commonalities in practice that graph-drawing alone obscures. Together these two papers set the stakes for the rest of the topic: structure in online social networks is no longer simply there to be drawn, but must be inferred, embedded, and modeled against principled alternatives.

## Scaling Embedding-Based Community Detection

A cluster of papers develops the computational machinery to do this inference at scale. [[Ferrara2026-io]] is the most purely methodological contribution: ECHO addresses the "Semantic Wall" (over-smoothing in GNN-based community detection) and the "Systems Wall" (O(N²) memory costs) via a topology-aware router, attention-guided diffusion, and memory-sharded contrastive learning, pushing self-supervised attributed community detection to million-node graphs like Pokec. This is the technical substrate that the topic's more applied papers implicitly need. [[Gerard2025-br]] operationalizes a related idea—users as distributions over latent clusters rather than nodes in an interaction graph—in its CANE/t-CANE framework, reconstructing cross-platform discourse networks from shared narrative participation instead of reposts or follows; applied to Truth Social and X, it recovers a small set of structurally pivotal "bridge users" who seed cross-platform narrative diffusion, echoing sociological notions of brokerage and weak ties. [[Minici2024-tf]] extends the embedding paradigm toward foundation models, combining language and graph representations in IOHunter to detect coordinated information-operation drivers across heterogeneous campaigns, with an explicit ambition toward out-of-distribution generalization. [[Lai2024-to]] shows a simpler but conceptually related move: correspondence analysis over Reddit cross-posting patterns can place YouTube videos themselves (not just users) on a latent ideological manifold, extending ideal-point-style scaling from actors to content objects.

## Detecting Coordination: Multiplex and Temporal Structure

A tightly linked pair of papers treats coordinated inauthentic behavior as a problem of multiplex and temporal network construction rather than single-layer graph analysis. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] builds a time-aware, multiplex extension of Newman's collaboration model, weighting co-actions by exponential temporal decay across separate hashtag/retweet/mention/URL layers, and shows this combination outperforms both time-agnostic and single-modality baselines, particularly against dilution tactics. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] systematically compares five ways of operationalizing multimodal coordination—including naive flattening versus genuine multiplex community detection—and identifies a fundamental trade-off between integration strength and detection inclusiveness, concluding that multiplex approaches (via Generalized Louvain) best preserve both monomodal signal and central nodes. Read alongside [[Minici2024-tf]], these three papers converge on a shared diagnosis: coordination and manipulation leave traces that are structurally heterogeneous and time-sensitive, and flattening or static graph methods systematically under- or over-detect them.

## Mapping Political Fragmentation and Polarization

A second empirical cluster uses these structural tools to interrogate claims about echo chambers and cross-ideological bridging. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] combines multiple network and overlap measures across 11 political subreddits to show that even discursively similar communities rarely overlap in users, sources, or cross-posting, challenging the idea of r/politics as a cross-ideological town square and proposing a "sedimentation of polarisation" model in which platform design, moderation, and discourse are entangled co-producers of fragmentation. [[Zhu2026-tn]] extends this question comparatively, building chance-corrected audience-overlap networks across six European news ecosystems to show that the structural isolation of alternative media is not fixed but contingent on media-system type and the institutional access of populist parties—with Sweden's isolated alternative enclave offered as a limit case. Both papers insist that network position is not a direct readout of ideological distance but is co-produced by platform affordances, moderation regimes, and political context—a point that chimes with [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]'s broader claim that "clustering" is always already shaped by platform architecture.

## Transnational and Cross-Platform Far-Right Networks

A substantial empirical arc applies community-detection and network-overlap methods specifically to far-right digital ecosystems, tracing how practice-level ties (hyperlinks, guest appearances, co-commenting, cross-posting) construct transnational structures invisible to single-platform or single-country analysis. [[Heft2021-ky]] establishes the baseline pattern via hyperlink networks among right-wing news sites in six Western democracies, finding that U.S. sites function as an integrating hub and that—counterintuitively—legacy media, not fellow alternative outlets, are the dominant shared transnational reference points. [[Askanius2026-de]] zooms into the Swedish case on YouTube, showing how guest appearances, hyperlinks, and in-video mentions weave a cohesive but ideologically diverse "alternative influence network" that hybridizes activism, microcelebrity branding, and monetization. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] generalizes the comparative lens across five platforms simultaneously, demonstrating that the same actor set produces structurally distinct networks (broadcast-like on Twitter, fragmented echo chambers on Instagram, hierarchical on Facebook) depending on platform affordances for connectivity, replicability, and scalability. [[Groebner2026-pc]] closes this arc at even larger scale, using commenter co-occurrence networks across four English-language far-right YouTube channels to document a genuinely transnational, US-centered audience structure that forms within months of a channel's founding. Across this quartet, the throughline is that transitive ties (shared commenters, shared links, shared guests) are the operational signature of an otherwise dispersed ideological movement, and that affordance-sensitive, multi-platform network analysis is necessary to see it.

## Temporal Process and the Limits of Static Graphs

[[Fan2026-af]] complements this arc methodologically by arguing that cross-sectional, aggregated network measures miss the hyper-longitudinal structure of user behavior; its comparative review of sequence analysis, event history models, HMMs, process mining, and network analysis on multi-platform trace data shows that network-based community detection (e.g., modularity splits of platform-switching behavior) is only one of several complementary lenses on temporal structure, each surfacing different facets of practice clustering that a purely static graph would flatten.

## Beyond Human Networks: Structural Priors for Agentic Systems

Finally, [[Ng2026-og]] extrapolates the topic's structural logic to a new substrate entirely, arguing that agentic AI systems operating in social environments require social theory itself as a structural prior rather than being modeled as isolated agents. Its formalization of interaction networks, network-constrained dependence, and co-evolving influence dynamics—empirically grounded in the MoltBook AI social network—suggests that the same graph-theoretic and causal-network apparatus developed across this topic for human communities (hubs, periphery, distributional drift) is becoming directly applicable to the emerging domain of multi-agent AI ecosystems, closing the arc from descriptive network mapping to a generative theory of structure itself.
