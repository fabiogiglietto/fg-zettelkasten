# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## From Networked Ties to Algorithmic Clusters

The foundational tension running through this collection is captured most explicitly by [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]], which argues that platforms like TikTok have replaced "networked publics" — organized around visible, explicit interpersonal ties — with "clustered publics," statistically inferred neighborhoods of shared interest. This reframing sets the conceptual stakes for much of what follows: if community formation online is no longer simply a matter of who-follows-whom, then computational methods for detecting structure must grapple with latent, algorithmically-constructed affiliation rather than declared connection. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Brown2026-br]] empirically interrogate this claim from the supply-side: by comparing algorithmic and chronological feeds, Efstratiou et al. show that Twitter's recommender inflated the centrality of provocateurs and of Elon Musk himself, while Brown et al.'s audit of YouTube finds the algorithm produces strong "rabbit holes" (sequential, Markov-like content dependence) but only weak ideological echo-chamber effects, which are mostly attributable to user choice rather than the ranking system. Together these papers suggest that "clustering" is neither a pure artifact of algorithms nor pure user agency, but an entangled property that must be causally decomposed — a methodological lesson that recurs across the corpus.

## Scaling and Learning Community Structure

A second cluster addresses the technical problem of detecting community and practice structure at scale. [[Ferrara2026-io]] (ECHO) directly engages the architectural bottlenecks of self-supervised graph embeddings — over-smoothing in heterophilic graphs and the O(N²) memory wall of pairwise similarity clustering — proposing a topology-aware router and chunked contrastive objective that pushes attributed community detection to million-node graphs such as Pokec. This systems-level concern with scalability is echoed in [[Minici2024-tf]]'s IOHunter, which fuses language models with graph neural networks into a "graph foundation model" capable of generalizing information-operation detection across disparate campaigns, and in [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE/t-CANE framework, which sidesteps platform-specific interaction signals entirely by embedding users via shared participation in latent narrative clusters — a move that lets cross-platform "bridge users" become visible as a structural (rather than behavioral) phenomenon. [[Bruns2025-fz]] offers a complementary, more interpretive proposal — "practice mapping" via vector embeddings of multimodal actions — aimed at the same representational problem that motivates ECHO and CANE: how to fuse heterogeneous interaction signals into a single coherent map without collapsing into an uninterpretable "hairball." [[Fan2026-af]] extends this representational ambition into the temporal dimension, arguing that communication research has under-used the fine-grained timestamps in digital trace data, and demonstrating via network analysis, process mining, and embedding models that cross-platform behavioral communities (e.g., TikTok-centric vs. Facebook/Instagram-centric routines) are visible once sequences rather than aggregates become the unit of analysis.

## Coordination as a Multiplex, Causal Network Problem

A closely related methodological thread concerns the detection and causal evaluation of *coordinated* ties, treating coordination itself as an emergent network structure rather than an individual trait. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] both argue that single-modality or flattened multi-layer representations systematically under- or over-detect coordination, proposing instead multiplex, time-aware architectures (temporal-kernel collaboration models; Generalized Louvain over multiplex layers) that preserve the complementary and redundant signals carried by retweets, mentions, hashtags, and URLs. These detection-oriented papers are explicitly put in dialogue with a parallel pair — [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and [[Di_Marco2026-xu]] — which push past detection to ask a causal question: once coordinated accounts are identified, how much influence do they actually exert on cascade outcomes? Using optimal-placement and greedy benchmarks on retweet trees, Di Marco et al. find that real coordinated accounts in the 2019 UK election dataset behave statistically close to random placement and far below their theoretical potential influence, a skeptical result that complements Mannocci and Iannucci's structural insight that coordination signatures are real and detectable, but also cautions against assuming structural coordination automatically equals outcome-level power.

## Latent Ideology and Curated Structure

A third strand uses network position itself as a measurement instrument for ideology and audience structure. [[Barbera2015-je]] is the methodological ancestor here, showing that Twitter follow-networks encode recoverable ideal points for elites and ordinary users alike, and documenting retweet-level echo chambers in the 2012 U.S. campaign. [[Lai2024-to]] extends this ideal-point logic from actors to content, inferring the latent ideology of YouTube videos from patterns of cross-subreddit sharing — a move that nicely bridges the ideology-estimation tradition with the platform-ecology concerns found later in the corpus. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates both lines by showing that "curation bubbles" operate at the story level, not the source level: domain-level partisan scores mask substantial heterogeneity in which specific stories circulate to which audiences, implying that structural/network measures of political alignment must be pitched at finer granularity than outlet identity to be valid.

## Mapping Political and Extremist Network Ecologies

The largest thematic cluster applies these computational and causal tools to map real-world community and practice formation among political actors, particularly on the right. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] shows, across 11 political subreddits, that cross-ideological ties (shared sources, cross-posting, cross-commenting) are minimal even between discursively similar communities, arguing that Reddit's "polarization" is a sedimented, multi-causal entanglement of design, moderation, and discourse rather than a simple network-structural outcome. This emphasis on the co-construction of structure and practice recurs in the far-right media-ecology papers: [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a cohesive Swedish "alternative influence network" on YouTube built from guest appearances, hyperlinks, and in-video mentions, emphasizing the hybridization of activist and influencer practices; [[Kakavand2026-kt]] compares the same German far-right actors across five platforms, showing that technology affordances (connectivity, replicability, scalability) produce markedly different network topologies for the same actor set, from Twitter's loose broadcast arena to Facebook's hierarchical AfD-dominated structure; and [[Groebner2026-pc]] traces a transnational far-right audience across four English-language YouTube channels via commenter-overlap networks, finding a U.S.-centered hub structure that mirrors the national-hub findings of [[Heft2021-ky]]'s earlier hyperlink study of right-wing news sites, where U.S. sites anchor a loosely transnational ecology while legacy media remain, counterintuitively, the most-cited shared reference points. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] push this ecological mapping toward the alternative/mainstream boundary itself: Zhu et al.'s cross-country audience-overlap networks show that alternative media's structural marginality is contingent on media-system type and populist parties' institutional access, while Balluff et al.'s named-entity co-occurrence networks around Nord Stream 2 coverage find substantial entity overlap between alternative and legacy German media, with divergence emerging in relational structure (transitivity, modularity) rather than in actor selection. Finally, [[Tuters2026-vo]] offers a more semiotic extension of the same network-analytic toolkit — co-hashtag analysis of the "Great Reset" conspiracy — arguing that conspiratorial communities cohere through patterned circulation and symbolic adjacency across platforms before any coherent shared narrative stabilizes, a finding that resonates with Gerbaudo's opening claim that platform-era community formation increasingly precedes, rather than follows from, articulated social or ideological affiliation.

Read together, these papers trace an arc from identifying the problem (networked publics giving way to algorithmically clustered ones) through building the computational infrastructure to detect such clusters at scale and across modalities, to using those tools — and interrogating their causal limits — in order to map the concrete practice-communities, coordination networks, and ideological ecologies that now constitute online political and social life.
