# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## Scaling and diversifying the representation of network structure

The methodological backbone of this collection is the move from classical, purely topological community detection toward learned, self-supervised representations of network and practice structure. [[Ferrara2026-io]] exemplifies this shift most directly: ECHO diagnoses two concrete bottlenecks in graph neural approaches to attributed community detection—an over-smoothing "Semantic Wall" in dense or heterophilic graphs and an O(N²) "Systems Wall" in pairwise similarity clustering—and resolves them with a topology-aware router, attention-guided diffusion, and memory-sharded contrastive learning that scales to million-node networks like Pokec. This same impulse toward representation learning as a substitute for brittle heuristics recurs, in different modalities, across the topic. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes "practice mapping," using vector embeddings of multimodal user actions to escape the "hairball" problem of conventional network visualization, effectively asking ECHO's question—how do we represent complex relational structure tractably?—at the level of user practice rather than node embedding. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the representation-learning logic to visual content, showing that routing images through vision-language models into text embeddings captures *connotative* meaning that CNN-based clustering misses, again trading a rigid single-encoder pipeline for a more semantically adaptive one. [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE/t-CANE framework generalizes the same idea to cross-platform discourse: rather than embedding nodes from interaction graphs, it embeds users as distributions over latent narrative clusters, sidestepping the platform-specific signals (reposts, follows) that are increasingly unavailable post-API-lockdown. Together these papers trace an arc from graph-native embeddings (ECHO) to embeddings of practices, images, and narratives—all converging on the claim that emergent structure is best recovered not from raw ties but from learned semantic proxies for them.

## Detecting coordination in multiplex, temporal networks

A second cluster operationalizes "community" not as an organic clustering outcome but as a target of adversarial detection: coordinated inauthentic behavior. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] both argue that single-modality, time-agnostic collaboration networks are inadequate for distinguishing deliberate coordination from coincidental synchronicity, and both converge on multiplex representations analyzed via Generalized-Louvain-style community detection. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] adds a temporal-decay kernel to Newman's collaboration model, showing empirically that combining time-awareness with multiplex layering yields state-of-the-art weighted precision across 26 benchmark datasets and, crucially, robustness against adversarial "dilution" tactics. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] complements this with a systematic comparison of five ways to operationalize multimodality (monomodal, independent layers, union flattening, multiplex, intersection flattening), demonstrating a fundamental trade-off between integration strength and detection inclusiveness, and showing that naive flattening—the dominant practice in the coordination-detection literature—discards structurally central nodes that multiplex methods preserve. Read together, these two papers effectively settle a methodological debate within the coordination-detection subfield in favor of multiplex, temporally-aware graph construction over monomodal or flattened alternatives.

## Causal mechanisms behind emergent ties

Where the previous cluster asks how to *detect* community structure, a pair of papers asks how such structure is *causally produced*. [[Smith2025-kc]] introduces the "attention broker" (tertius amplificans) mechanism, using a difference-in-differences design on Twitter/X follower-event data to show that algorithmic amplification by high-degree accounts causally accelerates triad closure among their followers—offering a rare causal microfoundation for the long-observed macro-pattern of transitivity in directed social networks. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] operates in the same empirical terrain (Twitter/X's recommendation system) but inverts the lens, using observational network-centrality comparisons between algorithmic and chronological feeds to show that apparent political bias in visibility is substantially explained by behavioral covariates (agitating content, proximity to the platform owner) rather than ideology per se, and that centralization itself is disproportionately driven by one actor (Musk) becoming structurally dominant. Both papers thus treat network structure not as a static object to be clustered but as the causal residue of platform-mediated amplification dynamics, extending the topic beyond description into mechanism.

## Ideology, polarization, and the limits of overlap

A third thread examines what community detection and relational analysis reveal about ideological fragmentation. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] uses a battery of overlap measures (shared users, sources, cross-posting, cross-commenting, linking) across eleven political subreddits to show that cross-ideological interaction is minimal even between discursively similar communities, challenging "town square" framings and proposing a "sedimentation of polarisation" arising from the entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse. [[Lai2024-to]] operationalizes a related but distinct question—not whether communities overlap, but where content falls on a latent ideological axis—by using correspondence analysis on cross-subreddit sharing of YouTube videos, extending ideal-point-style ideology estimation from actors to content items. [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates the polarization narrative further by showing, via named-entity co-occurrence networks in German alternative versus legacy media, that the *same* entities appear across media types; divergence lies not in *which* actors are referenced but in *how* they are relationally structured (transitivity, modularity) and how heavily alternative outlets reference other media. Collectively these three papers push back against simple bifurcation stories, showing that polarization and alternative-media divergence are structural and relational phenomena that require network-level rather than purely content-level measures to detect.

## Practice clusters and multi-platform ecosystems: the far right as testbed

The far right recurs as an empirical proving ground for multi-platform structural analysis. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] provides the conceptual anchor, showing via affordance theory and comparative network analysis across five platforms that the same actors produce starkly different network topologies (Twitter's scalable, low-reciprocity broadcast arena; Telegram's clustered fringe channels; Instagram's fragmented echo chambers; Facebook's hierarchical party structure), demonstrating that "community formation" is inseparable from platform-specific affordances. [[Askanius2026-de]] zooms into a single platform (YouTube) and a single national case (Sweden), using guest-appearance, hyperlink, and mention networks to show how far-right actors hybridize activist and influencer practices into a cohesive alternative influence network. [[Groebner2026-pc]] scales this logic transnationally, using co-commenting networks across four English-language far-right YouTube channels to reveal a genuinely transnational audience structure centered on US content. Read as a sequence, Kakavand–Askanius–Groebner move from a comparative affordance framework, to a national single-platform deep dive, to a transnational audience-network analysis, jointly illustrating how the topic's graph-based methods (co-occurrence networks, community detection, centrality) can be layered to trace practice clusters at increasing geographic and platform scale.

## Toward temporal and dynamic extensions

Finally, [[Fan2026-af]] gestures at where this body of work might go next: it argues that the field's community- and practice-detection methods still largely rely on time-aggregated, cross-sectional representations, and proposes treating digital trace data as chronologically ordered "user-sequences" analyzable via sequence analysis, event history models, HMMs, network analysis, process mining, and neural embeddings. This resonates directly with [[Iannucci2025-eg]]'s temporal-decay kernel and [[Smith2025-kc]]'s causal, event-time framing, suggesting a convergence point for the topic: the next generation of structural methods will need to fold the temporal dimension—already central to coordination detection and causal brokerage analysis—more systematically into embedding-based community detection itself.
