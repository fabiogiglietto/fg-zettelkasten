# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## From Detection to Explanation: The Arc of This Literature

The papers gathered here trace an arc from *how do we computationally find structure in networks* to *what does that structure mean, and can we trust the tools that reveal it*. At one end sit papers building or benchmarking the machinery of community and coordination detection; at the other end sit applied studies that use (or presuppose) such machinery to map ideological ecosystems, platform migrations, and algorithmic effects. A recurring worry threads through both halves: structural patterns that look like "communities" or "coordination" or "influence" can be artifacts of embedding choices, aggregation decisions, or platform affordances rather than genuine emergent order — a tension this note tries to surface rather than paper over.

## Scaling and Repairing Community Detection

Ferrara2026-ub sits at the technical core of the topic, diagnosing two concrete bottlenecks in self-supervised graph learning for community detection — over-smoothing in dense/heterophilic graphs (the "Semantic Wall") and O(N²) memory costs (the "Systems Wall") — and proposing a topology-aware router plus chunked similarity extraction to reach million-node attributed graphs like Pokec. This is explicitly a synthesis of classical modularity-based network science (Louvain, Infomap, OSLOM) with modern GNN representation learning, and it exemplifies a broader move in the corpus: rather than treating "community" as a single well-defined graph-theoretic object, recent work increasingly asks which encoder, which similarity metric, or which layer combination is *appropriate* to a given morphology of social data.

That pluralism is echoed by [[Mannocci2025-ig]] and [[Iannucci2025-eg]], both of which argue that single-modality or time-agnostic representations of online coordination systematically distort what counts as a "community." Mannocci et al. show a structural trade-off between the strength of multimodal integration and the inclusiveness of detected coordination — union-flattening discards central nodes, intersection-flattening is too austere, and multiplex Generalized-Louvain detection best preserves monomodal findings while adding new structure. Iannucci et al. extend this logic temporally, showing that a time-decayed, multiplex collaboration model outperforms both single-modality and time-agnostic baselines on 26 labeled coordination datasets, and that per-layer modularity-optimized decay parameters let the method adapt without prior knowledge of a campaign's tempo. [[Mannocci2026-kc]]'s survey consolidates this entire subfield, proposing a general definition of coordinated online behavior along four orthogonal dimensions (authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, time-variance) and showing that platform and academic definitions have historically been contingency-driven rather than theoretically grounded — a critique that retroactively frames why papers like Mannocci2025-ig and Iannucci2025-eg needed to build more principled multiplex/temporal representations in the first place.

[[Bruns2025-fz]] and [[Gerard2025-br]] push the representational question further, arguing that the conventional network graph itself — the "hairball" or "furball" of edges — may be the wrong unit of analysis. Bruns proposes practice mapping via vector embeddings of user actions as a way to see commonalities in *practice* rather than direct ties. Gerard's CANE/t-CANE operationalizes a similar intuition at scale: users are represented as distributions over latent narrative clusters rather than through interaction edges, which lets the method reconstruct cross-platform discourse networks even where direct interaction data (reposts, follows) is unavailable — increasingly the norm in the post-API era. Applied to Truth Social and X, this reveals a tiny population of high-entropy "bridge users" who account for the large majority of narrative migration, reframing cross-platform influence as a structural, discourse-alignment phenomenon rather than a direct-tie phenomenon — a genuinely new kind of "community" made visible only because the representation moved beyond interaction graphs.

## Does Structure Imply Influence? A Causal Corrective

Two papers push back against the assumption that detected structure automatically confers causal importance. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formalizes influence in information cascades via optimal and greedy node-placement benchmarks and finds that real coordinated accounts in UK election retweet cascades achieve influence far below either benchmark, with placement patterns statistically resembling *random* assignment — a sobering counterpoint to the assumption, common in the coordination-detection literature above, that detecting a coordinated cluster implies that cluster is pivotal to diffusion. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] performs an analogous move at the level of platform-level recommendation: apparent right-leaning algorithmic amplification on pre-X Twitter mostly dissolves once agitating content, verification status, and Musk-proximity are controlled for, with the algorithmic feed's dominant structural feature being extreme centralization around a single account (Musk) rather than partisan bias per se. Together these two papers model a methodological discipline this topic increasingly demands: detected structural correlations (coordination clusters, amplified accounts) require causal or counterfactual benchmarking before being read as evidence of strategic or biased design.

## Measurement Hygiene: What Embeddings Encode

[[Fan2025-ut]] supplies a foundational caution for every embedding-based method above: pretrained text embeddings encode confounders like source or language that masquerade as semantic similarity, and linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove this contamination cheaply and without degrading out-of-domain performance. Framed as a structural-decomposition problem — removing the loading on observed confounders from dot-product similarity — this paper is a methodological companion to ECHO, CANE, and practice mapping alike: any claim that embedding-derived clusters reflect genuine community structure (rather than platform, language, or source artifacts) implicitly depends on the kind of deconfounding Fan et al. formalize.

## Applied Mapping: Ideological and Platform Ecosystems

A cluster of empirical papers applies network/audience-overlap methods to map how communities of practice and belief cohere across platforms and borders, largely in far-right and alternative-media contexts. [[Heft2021-ky]] and [[Groebner2026-pc]] both find transnational integration where insulation might be expected: right-wing news sites hyperlink across borders with the U.S. as central hub, and far-right YouTube commenters overlap substantially across four English-speaking countries, with the US occupying a structurally central position. [[Askanius2026-de]] and [[Kakavand2026-kt]] complicate the picture by showing that *within* a national far-right ecosystem, cohesion and role differentiation vary sharply by platform and connective practice — guest appearances and hyperlinks weave a cohesive Swedish alternative-influence network, while the same set of German far-right seed accounts produces starkly different network topologies (broadcast-like on Twitter, echo-chamber-like on Instagram, hierarchical on Facebook) purely as a function of platform affordances. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] extend this affordance-and-context sensitivity to audience- and content-level structure: alternative-mainstream audience overlap is generally low but strongly shaped by whether populist parties hold institutionalized political access, and named-entity co-occurrence networks show alternative media referencing largely the same actors as legacy media while organizing them into more cohesive, less modular constellations. [[Lai2024-to]] contributes a complementary measurement technique — recovering the latent ideology of individual YouTube videos from cross-subreddit sharing patterns via correspondence analysis — extending ideal-point-style latent scaling from actors to content itself, a move that could in principle feed directly into the audience-overlap and entity-network analyses above.

[[Wang2026-ub]] and [[Zheng2026-bi]] address the practical and infrastructural conditions under which such network mapping is even possible. Wang's study of academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration shows that dense internal network connectivity among motivated migrants was insufficient to sustain a community absent field-specific server identity and cross-server engagement diversity — a finding about the fragility of migrated community structure in decentralized ecosystems. Zheng and colleagues address a prior, infrastructural problem: without genuine random sampling tools (TubeStats, TokStats), any claim about "typical" community structure on YouTube or TikTok risks being an artifact of opportunistic, popularity-biased sampling, a caution that applies retroactively to several of the platform-mapping papers above.

## Theoretical Framing and Peripheral Extensions

Two papers step back to argue for social-theoretic priors in the design of computational systems. [[Ng2026-og]] argues that agentic AI systems should be modeled with social theory (heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) as structural priors rather than as isolated agents, empirically grounding these claims in a real AI social network (MoltBook) whose karma dynamics show network-topology effects strikingly similar to those documented in the human platforms above. [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]], considerably earlier and more speculative, anticipates this move by arguing Internet architecture itself should encode social-capital metrics (trust, reach, bridging) rather than purely technical centrality measures — a reminder that the tension between network-topological and socially meaningful notions of centrality predates the graph-learning methods surveyed above.

Finally, [[Fan2026-af]] and [[Meher2025-qb]] sit at the edges of this topic's core concerns. Fan et al.'s survey of temporal user-sequence methods (including a network-analysis and modularity-based component showing platform-communities of activity) offers a complementary temporal lens to the largely cross-sectional community-detection methods discussed above. Meher et al.'s ConflLlama is more tangential still — a fine-tuned LLM for conflict-event classification — but it is filed here as an illustration of the adjacent methodological toolkit (efficient fine-tuning, domain adaptation) that increasingly accompanies graph-based methods in large-scale computational social science pipelines.
