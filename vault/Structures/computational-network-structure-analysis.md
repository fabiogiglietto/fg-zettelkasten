# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## Scaling embeddings to detect emergent structure

The technical core of this topic is the move from raw topology toward learned representations of network position. [[Ferrara2026-io]] is the clearest statement of this shift: ECHO tackles the twin bottlenecks that have kept self-supervised GNNs from scaling to million-node attributed graphs—semantic over-smoothing in dense/heterophilic graphs and the O(N²) cost of pairwise similarity clustering—by routing graphs to different encoders and chunking similarity extraction, explicitly positioning itself as an heir to the modularity-maximization tradition (Louvain, Leiden, Infomap) rather than its replacement. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes a kindred but more programmatic move at the qualitative end: "practice mapping" uses vector embeddings of interaction actions to escape the interpretive limits of hairball visualizations, converging on the same intuition as ECHO—that learned similarity spaces reveal structure that raw adjacency matrices obscure—even though it stops short of ECHO's engineering apparatus. [[Gerard2025-br]] pushes the embedding logic further still: CANE/t-CANE models users not by their observed ties but by their distribution over latent narrative clusters, allowing "community" to be reconstructed from shared discursive alignment even when direct interaction data across platforms is unavailable. This is a direct methodological response to the post-API environment that [[Zheng2026-bi]] describes, in which platform-sanctioned interaction data can no longer be assumed. Because all three approaches ultimately rest on vector similarity, they inherit a shared vulnerability that [[Fan2025-ut]] names explicitly: embeddings routinely encode confounders like source or language that inflate spurious similarity, and its LEACE-based erasure procedure offers a low-cost preprocessing fix relevant to any pipeline—ECHO's, CANE's, or practice mapping's—that clusters or retrieves on embedding similarity.

## Coordination as latent community structure

A parallel strand treats "coordination" as a community-detection problem in its own right. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] both argue that collapsing heterogeneous co-action signals (temporal, modal) into a single layer destroys the very structure one is trying to detect—echoing ECHO's diagnosis that a single inductive bias is insufficient across graph morphologies. Iannucci extends Newman's collaboration model with a temporal decay kernel and multiplex layers to separate hashtag, retweet, mention, and URL coordination; Mannocci systematically compares five ways of operationalizing multimodality and finds multiplex community detection the best-balanced strategy between integration and inclusiveness. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] synthesizes this whole subfield into a formal survey, proposing actors–actions–intent as the atomic components of coordination and four orthogonal dimensions (authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, time-variance), while noting that community discovery in this space still leans on the same Louvain/Leiden machinery that [[Ferrara2026-io]] explicitly reengineers for scale and that [[Gerard2025-br]] sidesteps altogether via affiliation modeling. But detecting coordinated structure is not the same as showing it matters: [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formalizes influence within cascade trees and finds that real coordinated accounts achieve far less influence than optimal or even resource-matched greedy placements, resembling random assignment—an important corrective that structural presence of a "coordinated community" need not translate into outsized causal effect on diffusion.

## Mapping ideological and practice clusters in the wild

A third cluster of papers applies classical SNA metrics—density, modularity, transitivity, centrality—to map empirically how communities and practice clusters form in politically charged ecosystems, testing the abstractions above against real cases. [[Askanius2026-de]] traces the Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" through guest-appearance, hyperlink, and mention ties, finding star-like clusters anchored by legitimizing media hubs and a densely coordinated subcluster. [[Groebner2026-pc]] extends the same logic transnationally, using co-commenting networks across four English-language far-right YouTube channels to show audience-level community formation that transcends national boundaries, with US content occupying a structurally central position. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] holds the actor set constant and varies the platform, showing that affordances for connectivity, replicability, and scalability—not the underlying actors—determine whether the same far-right network looks like a broadcast arena, an echo-chamber cluster, or a hierarchical party structure; this is essentially an empirical demonstration of the graph-morphology heterogeneity that motivates ECHO's adaptive routing. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] push into audience- and entity-level community boundaries: Zhu's chance-corrected overlap networks show that alternative/mainstream distinctions are structurally fluid, contingent on media system type and populist parties' institutional access, while Balluff's named-entity co-occurrence networks find that alternative outlets share nearly the same actors as legacy media but organize them into more cohesive, less modular constellations. Together these four papers reinforce Bruns's and Kakavand's shared claim that community boundaries revealed by network structure are relational and context-dependent rather than fixed properties of platforms or actors.

## Algorithmic curation, causal shocks, and network fragility

Several papers ask what happens to network structure under exogenous shocks—algorithmic or infrastructural. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] provides a causal-network-analysis complement to the mapping papers above, showing that Twitter's post-acquisition recommendation algorithm dramatically concentrated centrality around the platform owner and rewarded agitating content independent of political leaning. [[Wang2026-ub]] examines a community under platform collapse rather than curation: despite forming a dense, well-connected network of academic early adopters on Mastodon, most migrants failed to sustain engagement, with survival modeling showing that cross-server engagement diversity—not internal density—predicted retention. Both findings echo [[Di-Marco2025-aa]]'s broader lesson that structural cohesion (density, connectivity, centrality) does not guarantee functional persistence or influence. [[Lai2024-to]] sits methodologically adjacent: using correspondence analysis on Reddit cross-subreddit sharing of YouTube videos, it recovers latent ideological position from a bipartite affiliation network without any direct interaction data—the same affiliation logic that underlies [[Gerard2025-br]]'s cluster-affiliation embeddings, applied to a simpler scaling task.

## Temporal, agentic, and infrastructural extensions

A final set of papers stretches the topic along the time axis and toward general theory. [[Fan2026-af]] argues for representing digital trace data as time-evolving "user-sequences" rather than cross-sectional snapshots, surveying six approaches—including network analysis and neural embedding models—that together bridge this topic's structural/embedding tradition with finer-grained behavioral dynamics, directly relevant to the temporal-decay logic of [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and the timing-sensitive influence formalism of [[Di-Marco2025-aa]]. [[Meher2025-qb]] contributes at the level of the annotation pipeline rather than the network itself: efficient LLM fine-tuning for event classification exemplifies the kind of scalable labeling technique that could feed categorical attributes into the attributed graphs ECHO analyzes or the intent judgments that coordination detection ultimately requires. [[Ng2026-og]] generalizes the empirical regularities documented throughout this topic—hub/periphery heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolving influence, distributional drift—into formal structural priors for any multi-agent social system, validating them on a large AI-agent network and arguing that agentic AI design must inherit social theory rather than model agents as isolated task-completers. Two papers bookend the topic historically and infrastructurally. [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]], writing over a decade earlier, already flagged the mismatch between networking notions of centrality (degree, betweenness, closeness) and their social-capital analogues (bridging, trust, transitivity)—a tension that resurfaces whenever structural centrality stands in for social influence, as in [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] or the optimal-influence formalism of [[Di-Marco2025-aa]]. [[Zheng2026-bi]] addresses the precondition all of this depends on: without defensible random samples of platform content, the denominators behind any community or practice map are shaped by the same engagement biases that algorithmic curation produces, a reminder that the embedding- and coordination-detection tools surveyed here are only as trustworthy as the sampling frames that feed them.
