# Computational Approaches to Social Network Structure and Community Formation

## Scaling and Learning Community Structure

At the methodological core of this collection sits the problem of how to detect emergent community structure in networks that are too large, too dense, or too heterophilic for classical algorithms. [[Ferrara2026-io]] tackles this head-on with ECHO, a self-supervised graph embedding architecture that routes graphs to different encoders based on structural heuristics and replaces O(N²) similarity clustering with a chunked, degree-adaptive extraction step, pushing attributed community detection to million-node graphs like Pokec. This is squarely a "detect and map" contribution: it inherits the multi-scale philosophy of modularity-based community detection (Louvain/Infomap) but reengineers it for neural, feature-rich settings. [[Bruns2025-fz]] approaches the same underlying problem—how to make sense of dense, multimodal interaction structures—from a more qualitative angle, proposing "practice mapping" via vector embeddings of user actions as an alternative to hairball-prone network visualizations. Read together, these two papers frame a shared diagnosis (existing network-analytic tools break down under scale and multimodality) with complementary responses: one an engineering solution for scalable embedding-based clustering, the other a conceptual reframing of what a "map" of practice should represent.

## Detecting Coordination: From Single Signals to Multiplex, Temporal, Foundation Models

A cluster of papers extends this methodological program specifically to the problem of coordinated inauthentic behavior. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] provides the survey backbone here, synthesizing 122 papers into a general framework (actors, synergic actions, intent) and cataloguing the field's reliance on co-action networks, similarity functions, and Louvain/Leiden-style community discovery—while flagging the fragmentation of ad hoc definitions across industry and academia. The other three papers in this cluster can be read as concrete instantiations and refinements of exactly the pipeline this survey describes. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] systematically compares five ways of operationalizing multimodality (monomodal, flattening, multiplex), showing a fundamental trade-off between integration strength and inclusiveness, and finding multiplex community detection the most balanced choice—a finding that anticipates [[Iannucci2025-eg]]'s proposal to combine multiplex layering with a temporal decay kernel, which empirically outperforms both time-agnostic and single-modality baselines across 26 benchmark datasets. Where these two papers refine the *representation* of coordination networks, [[Minici2024-tf]] pushes on the *learning* side, proposing IOHunter, a graph foundation model fusing language and structural signals to generalize IO detection across campaigns and platforms, including out-of-distribution and few-shot regimes. Together, this quartet traces an arc from taxonomy (Mannocci2026-kc) to representational refinement (Mannocci2025-ig, Iannucci2025-eg) to model generalization (Minici2024-tf), all converging on the same network-science toolkit of co-action graphs, multiplex layers, and modularity-based clustering flagged in the survey.

## Causal Mechanisms Behind Emergent Ties

A second thread turns from detecting existing structure to explaining *why* it forms. [[Smith2025-kc]] is the most explicitly causal paper in the set: using a difference-in-differences design on Twitter/X follower events, it identifies "attention brokerage" (tertius amplificans) as a local mechanism generating the well-documented tendency toward triad transitivity—amplification by high-degree nodes causally accelerates followers' tie formation to amplified accounts. This paper stands somewhat apart methodologically (causal inference rather than embedding or clustering) but shares the collection's core concern with explaining emergent structural regularities in directed social networks, and it offers a mechanism-level complement to the more descriptive community-detection work above: transitivity is not just an aggregate pattern to be measured but a locally producible causal outcome.

## Reconstructing Networks Across Fragmented Platforms

Several papers grapple with the fact that user ties are often invisible or platform-specific, requiring networks to be reconstructed from indirect signals. [[Gerard2025-br]] proposes CANE/t-CANE, embedding users as distributions over shared latent narrative clusters rather than direct interactions, enabling cross-platform discourse network reconstruction between Truth Social and X and identifying a small set of "bridge users" who disproportionately seed migrating narratives—an empirical demonstration of brokerage dynamics that resonates with Smith2025-kc's attention-broker mechanism, though here the bridging is discursive rather than follow-driven. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] takes a more traditional tie-based approach but applies it comparatively across five platforms, showing that the same actor set (German far-right accounts) produces starkly different network topologies depending on platform affordances (connectivity, replicability, scalability)—a caution against assuming that "the network" is a platform-independent object. [[Wang2026-ub]] extends this cross-platform lens to a migration scenario, using longitudinal network analysis and survival modeling to show that even a well-connected community of academic early adopters largely failed to sustain a move from Twitter to Mastodon, with retention driven by field-specific server communities and cross-server engagement diversity rather than raw network cohesion—demonstrating that structural connectivity alone is a poor predictor of community persistence.

## Community Structure as a Lens on Alternative Media and Extremism

A final set of papers applies these graph-based approaches to substantive questions about far-right mobilization and alternative media ecosystems, using network position and clustering as the *object* of interpretation rather than the target of methodological innovation. [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube through guest-appearance, hyperlink, and mention networks, showing a cohesive but hybridized ecosystem knit together by professionalized influencer practices. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] both examine alternative media's structural position relative to the mainstream—the former through cross-country audience overlap networks (finding low but context-dependent overlap, with a distinctive isolated Swedish enclave), the latter through named-entity co-occurrence networks around Nord Stream 2 coverage (finding overlapping content but more cohesive, less modular relational structures in alternative outlets). Both complicate simple "echo chamber" narratives by showing that alternative media's marginality is a relational, context-dependent network property rather than an inherent trait. Rounding out this group, [[Fan2026-af]] steps back methodologically to advocate for a temporal, sequence-based reorientation of digital trace data analysis, reviewing network analysis alongside sequence and process-mining approaches; its cross-platform user-sequence networks (e.g., modularity splitting TikTok/YouTube from Facebook/Instagram) echo the platform-specific clustering patterns found by Kakavand2026-kt and Wang2026-ub, reinforcing this collection's recurring finding that community structure is not a fixed property of users or content, but emerges from the interaction of practice, platform affordance, and time.
