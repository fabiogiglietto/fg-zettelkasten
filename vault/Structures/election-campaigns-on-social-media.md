# Election Campaigns on Social Media

## Election Campaigns on Social Media

This body of work forms a longitudinal, comparative laboratory in which the researcher's coordination-detection toolkit has been repeatedly stress-tested against evolving political and platform conditions: Italy's 2018, 2019, and 2022 elections, the German 2021 and 2025 federal campaigns, and Brazil's post-Bolsonaro turmoil. Read together, the papers trace an arc from foundational method-building, through case-by-case empirical application, to a more recent reckoning with data access, generative AI, and platform governance as the conditions of possibility for this research program itself.

### Founding the coordination paradigm in Italy

The methodological backbone of the topic is laid down in the Italian trilogy. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] establishes the empirical terrain of the 2018 campaign, showing how populist-aligned news ecosystems (M5S, Lega) exhibit distinctive insularity and attention dynamics compared to mainstream parties—effectively motivating why actor- and behavior-level analysis, rather than simple partisan-exposure mapping, is needed. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] then formalizes "coordinated link sharing behavior" as a construct, arguing for an ecological, action-based reframing of disinformation research away from content-veracity or bad-actor detection; it demonstrates on 2018–2019 Facebook data that coordinated networks disproportionately carry problematic domains and cluster into centralized or clustered topologies independent of political orientation. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends this into a dynamic workflow—monitoring known coordinated accounts to surface new ones in near-real time—applied to the 2022 snap election, uncovering an M5S hyperpartisan echo-chamber, a religious-page clickbait economy, and a proselytizing Messenger-bot operation, illustrating that coordination detection generalizes across ideological, commercial, and religious motives alike. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] steps back to theorize why these methods are necessary at all, identifying blurred organizational boundaries, microtargeted advertising, and strategic amplification as three big-data-driven transformations that undermine traditional exposure measurement.

Complementary methodological refinements sharpen the toolkit for Italian data specifically: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks embedding models for clustering Italian political news, finding OpenAI's embeddings outperform UmBERTo, while [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] documents the validation challenges of building LLM-in-the-loop pipelines (classification, clustering, labeling) for the same 2018/2022 Facebook corpora—both papers quietly documenting the infrastructure underlying the substantive Italian findings. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] provides an earlier, pre-coordination-era baseline, showing that even in 2013 Twitter's "second screen" role during televised talk shows was narrow and TV-driven rather than agenda-shaping—an important reminder that hybrid-media dynamics long precede the coordinated-inauthentic-behavior framework.

### From detection to governance: what happens once coordination is found

A second cluster asks what coordinated or algorithmically shaped visibility actually does to democratic communication, increasingly turning attention toward the platforms' own governance choices. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] tracks Meta's political-content-reduction policy in Italy, finding it suppressed MPs' reach by 72% well before Meta's announced global rollout, with extremist accounts compensating through volume rather than losing total reach—revealing a policy applied asymmetrically and disclosed opaquely. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] broadens this to the US 2017–2022 context, showing Facebook actively curates visibility (dampening partisan content, rewarding journalistic quality) in ways that fluctuate with governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures, directly challenging the "neutral conduit" framing used in Meta-partnered election studies. This governance thread connects to broader work on deplatforming's ripple effects: [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows Twitter's 2021 suspension of election-audit accounts collapsed the authority structure of the Arizona Election Review network well beyond the mechanical loss of banned nodes, arguing targeted network interventions may outperform today's visibility-reduction approaches.

Adjacent papers extend coordination detection to new platforms and geographies. [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapts "Rapid Spread Network" methods to Brazilian WhatsApp groups, tracing coordinated misinformation from the 2022 election through the January 8 coup attempt and documenting how encrypted-platform coordination bypasses forwarding countermeasures. [[Oprea2025-lf]] applies an "Authenticity Matrix" to hyperactive Facebook sharers in Romania's 2024 EU campaign, while [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] argues Serbia's ruling party's CIB is qualitatively more mimetic and harder to detect than centralized bot farms—both suggesting that as detection methods mature, coordination strategies evolve to evade them, echoing concerns raised in [[Appel2026-qr]]'s account of financially- and politically-motivated deceptive networks in the US 2020 election, where indirect resharing rather than direct network posting drove most reach.

### Brazil: misinformation, trust, and democratic erosion

The Brazilian case studies form a tight, mutually reinforcing cluster examining how coordinated and organic misinformation translated into eroded institutional trust and intolerance. [[Rossini2026-mj]] and [[Rossini2026-jn]], drawing on the same three-wave panel, show that belief in electoral fraud misinformation both lowers trust in election results and increases political intolerance over time, with messaging-app use working indirectly through misinformation belief rather than directly. [[Ventura2025-sw]] complements this with a WhatsApp-deactivation field experiment, underscoring that private messaging—not just public feeds—is a crucial locus of misinformation exposure in Brazil. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] examines a different register of elite behavior: how parliamentarians across the political spectrum contested epistemic authority itself during the Manaus healthcare collapse, with pro-government actors attacking journalistic gatekeeping while opposition figures mimicked its conventions. [[Marino2026-he]] closes the loop longitudinally, tracking three years of a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network and finding that hyperpartisan communities are far less stable than echo-chamber theory predicts, with affective engagement collapsing around Lula's inauguration and the January coup attempt—suggesting coordination and affect are event-contingent rather than fixed features of partisan infrastructure. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] bookend this cluster with earlier ad-transparency concerns (Brazil 2018 crowdsourced ad auditing; US 2016 geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising), establishing that platform advertising itself—not only organic coordination—has long been a suppression and manipulation vector.

### Germany and cross-national comparison

The German case anchors the comparative axis. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision models for analyzing visual political communication on Instagram during the 2021 campaign, finding LLMs substantially outperform bespoke pipelines and revealing distinct "concentrated visibility" strategies between candidate and party accounts. [[Schulte2026-df]] examines the 2025 German election as an infrastructure problem rather than a findings-driven study, documenting how X and TikTok scraping limitations and algorithmic filtering constrain what can even be observed, while still surfacing substantive party-platform targeting differences and a TikTok "politainment" style with weakly correlated textual, vocal, and facial signals. These sit alongside broader European comparative work: [[Iris2026-pg]] finds disproportionate media visibility for the Radical Right across five countries' 2024 EP election coverage, [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric differently across Facebook, X, and TikTok, [[Tornberg2025-ir]] links misinformation sharing specifically to radical-right populism (not populism or right-ideology alone) across 26 countries' parliamentary Twitter data, and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] connects far-right electoral performance to voters' disinformation concerns. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] extends the comparative lens to Scandinavia, showing populist parties' Facebook posts reliably attract "Angry" reactions that in turn predict sharing—an affective-affordance mechanism paralleling the anger-driven dynamics documented in Brazil and the US.

### Adjacent mechanisms: bots, media capture, and narrative framing

Several papers probe mechanisms that interact with, but are distinct from, classic coordination: [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] shows social bots function as agenda-builders against 2022 Ohio campaign messaging, especially shaping negative sentiment; [[Knupfer2025-vt]] traces how a "logic of connective faction" links digitally networked Republican elites to hyper-partisan media around the Critical Race Theory issue, radicalizing intra-party politics through engagement-driven feedback loops; [[Balluff2026-bv]] documents covert media capture in Austria's advertising-for-coverage scandal; and [[Mosca2026-yh]] proposes a "Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification" model for how "fake news" accusations themselves become rhetorical weapons in Italian negative campaigning. On the sensemaking side, [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] analyze US election-integrity rumors as products of evidence-frame interactions and collaboratively performed "deep stories" rather than simple falsehoods, complicating any purely fact-checking approach to the coordination problem, while [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] calls for multi-level ("story, outlet, content") cartographies of news sharing to avoid oversimplifying partisan measurement, and [[Eady2025-vm]] offers a URL-sharing-based measurement model placing media, politicians, and citizens on a common ideological scale. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations themselves as a form of political brokerage in the Philippine case, a useful conceptual counterpoint to the more structural, network-based coordination measures used elsewhere in this topic.

### Method frontiers: LLMs, ads, and the data-access crisis

A final cluster addresses the tools and infrastructural conditions shaping future coordination research. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[Paci2025-ag]] probe LLMs' capacities and limits for political text analysis—positioning texts on ideological scales versus struggling with implicit, manipulative discourse in Italian political speech—while [[Lin2025-xp]] demonstrates that brief human-AI dialogues can produce persuasion effects exceeding traditional political ads across four national elections, raising new concerns about AI-driven campaign influence that dovetail with the ad-transparency literature ([[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Votta2025-xz]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]) showing ad and content diffusion effects on Facebook/Instagram are often smaller, more concentrated, or more algorithmically mediated than assumed. Underpinning virtually all of this is the sharpening data-access crisis: [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Lukito2026-nb]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] chart how the EU Digital Services Act's Article 40 has reopened—unevenly, and amid platform resistance—the data pipelines that this entire topic's coordination-detection tradition depends on, framing continued access not as a solved problem but as an ongoing governance struggle that will determine whether the comparative, cross-national research agenda traced above can continue.
