# Comparative Digital Media and Elections

## Extending Coordination Detection Beyond Italy

The methodological core of this cluster is the migration of coordination-detection techniques—originally built for Twitter/X in the Italian context—into new platforms and political systems. [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapts the "Rapid Spread Network" approach to 13 million WhatsApp messages in Brazil, showing that coordinated accounts drove misinformation and even helped mobilize the January 8, 2023 attacks, while demonstrating that WhatsApp's forwarding limits are largely irrelevant since coordinated actors bypass them entirely. [[Luceri2025-tr]] pushes the same family of methods into a genuinely different modality, arguing that TikTok's video-first architecture demands new similarity signals rather than a straight port of text-centric detection. [[Pante2025-pq]] turns the toolkit reflexively on the field itself, re-examining claims of inter-state collaboration between state-backed information operations and finding that many reported coordination patterns dissolve once modern indicators and control datasets are applied—a methodological corrective as important as any substantive finding. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] complicates detection further by describing Serbian SNS-linked networks that mimic organic grassroots support so convincingly that they evade standard bot-farm heuristics altogether, suggesting that "coordination" in electoral-authoritarian contexts may look structurally different from the Italian or US template. Together these papers mark a shift from applying one detection paradigm everywhere to asking what coordination even looks like on encrypted messaging apps, video platforms, and in hybrid regimes.

## Brazil as a Recurring Laboratory

Brazil emerges as the most densely studied non-Italian case, offering a layered picture of platform-mediated political conflict. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during the Manaus COVID crisis, with pro-Bolsonaro elites bypassing journalism entirely while opposition figures borrowed its visual conventions. [[Rossini2026-jn]] links belief in electoral-fraud misinformation to rising political intolerance across the 2022 election, identifying messaging-app news use as an indirect radicalizing pathway. [[Ventura2025-sw]] complements this with a WhatsApp-deactivation field experiment isolating the causal role of encrypted messaging in shaping political knowledge and misinformation exposure. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] documents an early (2018) volunteer-driven audit of Facebook political ads, prefiguring later transparency battles. Most dramatically, [[Ventura2026-yc]] traces the 2024 Brazilian ban on X, showing that conservative users disproportionately evaded the ban while liberal users exited, producing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" in the platform's political ecosystem—evidence that state intervention in platforms can itself become a partisan variable, not just a corrective to one.

## Beyond Brazil: Germany, Serbia, the Philippines, and the Global South

Other national contexts extend the comparative reach further. In Germany, [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs for analyzing candidate visibility on Instagram during the 2021 federal campaign, while [[Schulte2026-df]] uses the 2025 German election to argue that the central obstacle to multi-platform electoral research is not empirical ignorance but a lack of reusable research infrastructure—a claim that reframes much of this literature's methodological struggles as an infrastructural rather than a substantive problem. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceptualizes influence operations in the 2022 Philippine elections as a form of political brokerage, foregrounding the political-economic scaffolding (client relationships, intermediaries) that sustains manipulation rather than treating it as free-floating disinformation content. [[Beacken2026-zb]] broadens the aperture furthest, comparing generative-AI propaganda across six democratically weakened states including Nigeria, Bolivia, Hungary, India, Tunisia, and Turkey, arguing against technologically deterministic narratives in favor of structurally grounded, context-sensitive comparison—directly modeling the kind of cross-national analytic stance this whole topic cluster aspires to.

## Cross-National Media Visibility and Platform Comparison

A second strand compares media and platform dynamics across many countries simultaneously rather than through single case studies. [[Iris2026-pg]] finds systematic Radical Right overrepresentation in news coverage across Austria, Germany, Ireland, Poland, and Portugal ahead of the 2024 European Parliament elections, intensifying in the campaign's final weeks. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] examines the Sweden Democrats' platform-tailored visual securitization strategy, contrasting argumentative Facebook/X content with meme-driven TikTok content aimed at younger voters. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] compares Norway, Sweden, and Denmark, showing that populist parties elicit more "Angry" reactions and that anger—not love—drives sharing. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s US-based news-quality methodology to Germany, France, and Italy, finding untrustworthy-URL shares rising in electoral years in Germany and Italy but not France. [[Nenno2025-xa]] scales this comparative logic to 24 countries, finding that news-value patterns in flagged misinformation differ meaningfully between WEIRD and non-WEIRD contexts, with the US and Brazil forming a distinct polarization-driven cluster. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames this entire drive explicitly, introducing a special issue devoted to correcting misinformation research's WEIRD bias through comparative, cross-platform inquiry.

## Platform Governance, Data Access, and Research Infrastructure

A parallel concern across the cluster is the practical and political question of how researchers can even study these dynamics. [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's content moderation during the 2024 EU elections, finding inconsistent application of authority-signaling labels across languages and opaque removal communications. [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes this problem, cataloguing 72 data-access tools and identifying "price, proficiency, or permission" as the three barriers structuring who can do election research at all, with consumption-side data particularly scarce and access regimes skewed toward WEIRD researchers. [[Oprea2025-lf]] shows the human cost of these access constraints concretely, manually auditing hyperactive Facebook accounts amplifying Romanian party pages in the 2024 EU election precisely because API restrictions blocked automated detection. These infrastructural papers collectively argue that the comparative ambitions of this whole research program are bottlenecked less by theory than by platform-controlled observability.

## Advertising, Exposure, and Behavioral Effects

A final set of papers moves from detection and infrastructure to causal consequences of exposure. [[Kim2026-wg]] documents geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising in the 2016 US election and links exposure to measurable turnout declines concentrated among targeted subgroups. [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[Allcott2025-jb]], both part of the Meta-collaborative FIES program, offer more cautionary findings: deceptive networks reached tens of millions but attitudinal correlations largely vanish after controlling for pre-exposure characteristics, and randomized ad-removal produced no detectable effects on knowledge or polarization. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] characterizes the diffusion structure of misinformation itself, finding it disproportionately represented among rare, highly viral posts. [[Votta2025-xz]] shifts attention to the supply side, showing that ad-delivery algorithms—not just advertiser targeting—shape reach and cost in ways opaque to campaigners and regulators alike. Looking toward the frontier, [[Lin2025-xp]] tests a genuinely new intervention, showing that brief human–AI dialogues produce persuasion effects across four national electoral contexts that exceed traditional advertising, while revealing a partisan asymmetry in AI-generated factual accuracy. Read together with [[Beacken2026-zb]], this signals the cluster's next frontier: coordination and influence-operations research must now contend not only with cross-national platform and regulatory variation but with generative AI as a new persuasive and propagandistic actor in its own right.
