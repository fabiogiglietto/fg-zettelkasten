# Sociocybernetics & Social Systems Theory

## Cybernetic Foundations: Observation, Second-Order Reflexivity, and the Social

The intellectual core of this topic is the move from classical, first-order descriptions of social phenomena toward a second-order cybernetic stance in which observers, media, and systems co-constitute what counts as "information," "truth," or "the political." [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] is the clearest articulation of this foundation: drawing on von Foerster, Bateson, and Luhmann, it argues that "fake news" cannot be defined by creator intent alone because truthfulness is observer-dependent — a difference that makes a difference only in the eye of a propagator who judges, decides, and re-circulates. Its micro–meso–macro model (individual judgment, injector/propagator matrices, emergent cascades) is itself a sociocybernetic architecture, treating misinformation circulation as an autopoietic, multi-level process rather than a linear transmission chain. This observer-dependent, multi-level logic recurs as a structuring device across the corpus: [[Frischlich2025-vn]] explicitly borrows a four-level (micro/meso-I/meso-II/macro) complex-adaptive-systems frame to show how dominant analogies for misinformation — virus, warfare, pollution — each capture only one layer of a recursively coupled system, and that no single level's logic can be extrapolated to govern the whole. Both papers converge on the same meta-claim: single-level, linear-causal accounts of information and truth are category errors when applied to a system whose parts (individuals, groups, platforms, institutions) observe and reshape one another recursively.

[[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] supplies an earlier, infrastructural variant of this systems orientation, arguing that Internet architecture itself must be redesigned as a socially-driven system rather than a purely technical stack. Its account of the "prosumer" and "network prosumer," and its critique of how networking notions of centrality (degree, betweenness, closeness) diverge from social-capital notions of trust, reach, and bridging, anticipates the topic's broader concern: that social systems theory must be read into the technical substrate of the internet itself, not applied only after the fact to its content. Read together, these three papers establish the volume's foundational claim — that internet-era social systems must be *observed* cybernetically, with attention to recursive, multi-level, observer-relative dynamics — before any empirical or normative claims about platforms and democracy can be made.

## From Hybrid Media to Platform Infrastructure: Observing Democracy Under Strain

A second cluster extends this systems-theoretic sensibility to the empirical terrain of contemporary political communication, tracing an arc from media hybridity to full-blown democratic crisis. [[Chadwick2013-ns]] is the pivotal historical anchor: its concept of the "hybrid media system" — old and new media bound by adaptation and interdependence rather than displacement — and its "political information cycle" model are, in effect, an early systems-theoretic account of political communication as an assemblage of loosely coupled, co-evolving actors (exemplified by WikiLeaks' dependence on both encryption infrastructure and elite newspapers). This hybridity logic feeds directly into [[Bennett2025-xs]], which argues that democratic backsliding cannot be explained by "technocentric" (individual cognitive effects of disinformation) or "institutionalist" (parties, elites) paradigms alone, but requires a "communication-as-organization" account in which digitally networked publics become new "digital surrogate organizations" pulling parties toward illiberalism — a reframing that is essentially systems-theoretic in treating platforms, memes, and institutions as co-evolving components of a single political system rather than independent causal variables.

[[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes this logic to its regulatory conclusion, arguing — in the context of the EU's Digital Services Act — that platform scholarship must move "beyond causal effects" toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure, a direct descendant of the systems/infrastructure framing shared with [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] and [[Chadwick2013-ns]]. [[Esau2025-tf]] complements this by working at the normative-conceptual level, theorizing "destructive polarization" as a pathological state of connective democracy and proposing deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening as systemic correctives — in effect asking what healthy feedback loops within a democratic social system would look like. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] broadens the empirical and geographic scope of this inquiry, criticizing polarization research for its disciplinary fragmentation and Western (especially U.S.) skew, and calling for frameworks capable of capturing polarization dynamics as they play out differently across diverse political and media systems worldwide — a call that is consonant with the sociocybernetic insistence that social systems be observed in their own structural and historical specificity rather than through a single universalized model.

## Boundary Work: What Counts as Political, What Counts as Information

[[Guo2026-fi]] sits adjacent to this cluster but performs a distinct, meta-theoretical function: it interrogates how scholars draw the boundary between "political" and "non-political," arguing for a relational rather than substantialist ontology of politics in which the political is constituted through its relations to its ostensible outside. This boundary-work sensibility parallels Giglietto's argument that "fake news" is itself a divisive, observer-positioned label, and Frischlich's warning that analogy choice smuggles in implicit assumptions about where the "misinformation system" begins and ends. Together these papers suggest that a sociocybernetic research program must remain reflexively attentive to its own acts of demarcation — of the political, of truth, of the system's boundary — as constitutive moves rather than neutral descriptions.

## Extending the Frame: Machine Agency and Multi-Agent Social Systems

The final arc extrapolates social systems theory beyond human social systems to artificial agents, testing whether the topic's foundational concepts survive the transition to agentic AI. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes machine agency as a "demarcation problem" in social theory, arguing that agency is not an inherent technical property but a socially granted and monitored license to act — directly continuous with the observer-dependent, status-conferring logic of second-order cybernetics found in [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] and the boundary-work concerns of [[Guo2026-fi]]. [[Ng2026-og]] operationalizes this theoretical move into a formal research program: a Multi-Agent Social Systems (MASS) framework treating agentic AI populations as dynamical social systems (information exchange, influence dynamics, interaction network) governed by structural priors — strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability — empirically validated on a large AI social network. This paper functions as the volume's forward horizon: it explicitly argues that decades of social-theoretic insight (the very corpus assembled here) must become a *structural prior* for how we design, evaluate, and govern populations of interacting human and machine agents, closing the loop between sociocybernetics' classical concerns with observation and recursivity and the newest empirical frontier of big-data-scale, machine-populated social systems.
