# Sociocybernetics & Social Systems Theory

## Line of Argument

These five papers, spanning more than a decade, trace a single sociocybernetic conviction across quite different objects of study: that social phenomena — networks, misinformation, and now artificial agents — cannot be understood by reducing them to properties of individual units, but only by attending to the emergent, self-referential dynamics of the systems in which those units are embedded. Read together, they show sociocybernetics migrating from infrastructure theory, through media studies, into the newest terrain of agentic AI.

### Social Capital as an Architectural Prior

[[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] is the earliest and most infrastructural entry, arguing that Internet architecture itself must be redesigned around social-theoretic categories — reach, engagement, influence, trust — rather than purely technical metrics of throughput and centrality. Its core move is already sociocybernetic in spirit: centrality, a formally neutral graph-theoretic property, means something different once it is read through Bourdieu, Coleman, and Putnam, where a "bridger" node performs a structurally different function than a merely high-betweenness relay. This early insistence that technical and social descriptions of the same structure can diverge, and that design must reconcile them, anticipates the later papers' shared suspicion of single-level, single-discipline accounts of complex socio-technical systems.

### Second-Order Observation and the Propagation of Falsehood

[[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] pushes this systems-theoretic sensibility into the domain of misinformation, explicitly invoking second-order cybernetics (von Foerster, Bateson) and Luhmann to reframe "fake news" away from creator intent and toward the recursive judgments of propagators. Its micro–meso–macro model — individual truth-judgments, a four-cell matrix of injector/propagator combinations, and emergent global cascades — is a direct precursor to the multilevel architecture later formalized by [[Frischlich2025-vn]]. Both papers share the conviction that false information is not a fixed property of content but an observer-dependent, recursively reconstituted phenomenon: what counts as "true" is decided anew at every point of propagation, and no single vantage (creator, platform, or state) can arrest this circulation. Giglietto's rejection of the very label "fake news" as a "difference that makes a difference" claim by a truth-claiming observer is itself a piece of applied second-order cybernetics: the theory turns back on the discourse that names its own object.

### Complexity Against Single-Level Analogies

[[Frischlich2025-vn]] generalizes this multilevel logic into an explicit complex-adaptive-systems framework, arguing that the dominant metaphors for misinformation — virus, warfare, pollution — each illuminate one level (micro, meso, macro) while obscuring the others, and that none captures the recursive coupling between psychological, social, technical, and political subsystems. This is functionally a restatement, in the vocabulary of complexity science rather than Luhmannian systems theory, of Giglietto's insistence that misinformation is irreducible to any single mechanism or intent. Where Giglietto diagnoses the analytic error as creator-centrism, Frischlich et al. diagnose it as analogy-driven reductionism; both converge on the prescription that interventions must be portfolio-like and iterative rather than linear-causal, treating the phenomenon as an ongoing autopoietic process rather than a problem to be solved once.

### The Agency Question: From Human Systems to Machine Systems

The two most recent papers extend this sociocybernetic apparatus to a genuinely new referent: artificial agents. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes the question of AI agency as a "demarcation problem" for social theory, arguing — in a move deeply continuous with Luhmann's anti-substantialist stance — that agency is not an inherent property of a system but a socially granted and monitored *status*, a license to act conferred and revoked through social practice. This dissolves the question "can AI really act?" into the sociocybernetic question of how social systems attribute and police the boundary of agency, echoing the observer-dependence at the heart of Giglietto's treatment of truth-judgments.

[[Ng2026-og]] operationalizes a cognate insight at the level of formal modeling: agentic AI populations, it argues, cannot be understood through single-task, individualistic paradigms but require treatment as genuine social systems, formalized as an information-exchange/influence-dynamics/network triple exhibiting strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, and distributional instability. Empirically demonstrating these dynamics in a real AI social network, the paper effectively re-derives, from first principles of multi-agent systems research, structural priors that Luhmannian and cybernetic theory have long supplied for human social systems — that structure and behavior co-evolve, that macro-level regularities are emergent rather than aggregative, and that populations resist reduction to their constituent agents' capabilities. Read alongside Anicker's status-based account of agency, Ng et al. supply the formal, quantitative complement: if agency is socially attributed, MASS shows what the resulting systems look like once that attribution is in play at scale.

### Synthesis

Across infrastructure design, misinformation studies, and agentic AI, this body of work enacts a consistent sociocybernetic method: refuse single-level, intent to any single actor, or metaphor-driven explanation; instead track how observation, propagation, and structural coupling generate system-level phenomena irreducible to their parts. The arc from [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] through [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] and [[Frischlich2025-vn]] to [[Anicker2024-vp]] and [[Ng2026-og]] traces sociocybernetics' own recursive extension — applied first to the Internet's wiring, then to the circulation of falsehood, and now to the very question of who, or what, counts as a social agent at all.
