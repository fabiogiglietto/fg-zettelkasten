# Platform Governance & Regulation

## The data-access turn: from APIcalypse to regulated rights

A large cluster of papers in this collection narrates a single institutional arc: the collapse of the open API era, the subsequent dependence of researchers on platform goodwill, and the uncertain emergence of a regulatory alternative in the EU's Digital Services Act. [[Gillespie2010-as]] supplies the conceptual prehistory, showing how "platform" itself was coined as a discursive strategy of neutrality that let intermediaries dodge responsibility for curation — a framing [[van-Dijck2018-up]] extends into the "platform society" thesis that private infrastructures have displaced public institutions across whole sectors. [[Bruns2019-nr]] names the rupture directly: the post-Cambridge-Analytica "APIcalypse," in which platforms used privacy scandal as cover to frustrate critical scholarship, with Social Science One exemplifying an "independence by permission" model. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes this history systematically (prehistory → laissez-faire → authentication → limited options → academic cooperation) and classifies today's regimes into laissez-faire, academic, walled-garden, and pay-to-play APIs, while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the cost empirically: a 13% decline in Twitter-based scholarship by 2024 after the $42,000/month Enterprise API pricing shock. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] synthesizes this into a three-phase "Wild West → Golden Age → Walled Garden" trajectory and shows the DSA's Article 40 reframing access as regulatory right rather than philanthropy — though fragile and unevenly enforced, with the Commission's 2025 findings that TikTok and Meta breached Article 40.12.

Several papers dig into what this "regulatory right" actually delivers in practice. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] assess Article 40's practical reshaping of research conditions and election-research feasibility respectively, while [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] offers the collection's most explicit normative stance: current platform resistance to Article 40 is not evidence of regulatory failure but confirmation that binding rules are finally constraining entrenched power, and researchers should "stand firm" rather than retreat. [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] catalogue the concrete opportunities and bottlenecks — vetting delays, circular resource problems, platform legal obstruction — while [[Annabell2026-ev]] shows how even the core concept of "data" in Article 40 is a contested "floating signifier" that platforms narrow toward quantified, pre-aggregated assets, sidelining qualitative traditions. [[Peters2026-mo]] extends this contestation to "data quality," finding it nearly absent from official DSA texts yet successfully inserted into recitals through academic/NGO advocacy against platform resistance (Meta notably inverting the argument to claim low quality makes access futile). Infrastructural critiques of the resulting access regimes abound: [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] both interrogate the Meta Content Library/CrowdTangle lineage, showing how "clean room" environments and API design don't just gate data but actively shape what counts as legible research, privileging quantitative, well-resourced, Global-North scholarship. Platform-specific audits reinforce the point: [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API still inadequate for systemic-risk research, and [[Rieder2025-ju]] demonstrates YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," with findable videos decaying within 20–60 days — a methodological vulnerability with direct DSA compliance implications. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency mandates themselves as a data source, revealing stark disparities in moderator allocation to non-English, Global-South languages. Zooming out, [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues the field should move beyond causal-effects paradigms entirely toward treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure, a framing [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] operationalizes empirically by showing Facebook actively, variably curates visibility rather than passively transmitting shares.

## Industry entanglement and the credibility of platform-adjacent research

A second throughline interrogates the research ecosystem's dependence on platform cooperation itself. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] form a tight sequence arguing — with escalating empirical specificity — that social media research is structurally vulnerable to industry capture analogous to tobacco and pharma science: roughly half of high-profile papers have undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small repeatedly-engaged cohort who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, yielding ~80% "industrial saturation" once reviewers and editors are counted. [[Munger2025-cz]] turns this critique onto the canonical Meta2020 partnership itself, arguing that even its most rigorous experiments lack "temporal" and "poetic" validity — platforms change too fast for one-off studies to generalize, and null findings conveniently align with Meta's interests — concluding that proactive regulatory disclosure mandates, not reactive social science, are the only tenable path. This skepticism toward platform-mediated "independence by permission" research recurs across [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]], and is complicated by [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], whose working paper on Meta's political-content-reduction policy in Italy exemplifies both the promise and the limits of DSA-enabled infrastructure: the Meta Content Library allowed detection of a policy rollout concealed from DSA transparency reports (effects appearing ten months before Meta's announced timeline), yet the very existence of such a transparency deficit underscores why independent scrutiny remains necessary. [[Rogers2026-zz]] extends this genealogy of definitional narrowing — "fake news" to "false news" to "misinformation" to "borderline" — arguing Meta's shift from engagement to reach metrics (and its progressive API closures) conveniently obscures that top-performing borderline content remains disproportionately right-wing.

## Platform self-narration and the politics of discursive framing

Adjacent to data-access politics is a strand examining how platforms rhetorically manage their own policy changes and problem-framing. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects Meta's Newsroom discourse, showing how "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" and similar floating signifiers externalize blame for problematic content while Meta casts itself as capable solver — language that appears to have migrated into EU and Australian regulatory texts. [[De2026-ld]] generalizes this into "changecraft," a cross-platform discursive repertoire (visible infrastructure, ideological continuity-framing, incremental patchworking) that normalizes consolidation of platform power, while [[Petre2019-zh]] shows the inverse rhetorical move — "platform paternalism" — in which platforms moralize creators' visibility-seeking as illegitimate "gaming" even as platforms themselves automate and scale without equivalent scrutiny. [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] finds a parallel pattern specific to climate misinformation: soft interventions (labeling, demotion) dominate over removal, accompanied by "governance-washing" discourse that redistributes responsibility to creators and external experts. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] provides the conceptual anchor for this whole pattern, arguing that "reduction" (demotion without removal) has been strategically kept out of public and regulatory debate even as it has become platforms' default moderation tool — a blind spot [[McNally2025-dn]] partially closes by showing Facebook's News Feed algorithm changes leave detectable, laggedtraces in news engagement data, challenging the "black box" framing and bolstering the case for DSA-mandated algorithmic audits.

## Empirical effects of platform policy and algorithmic design

A substantial empirical cluster directly tests platform-policy and algorithm effects on political outcomes, often explicitly engaging the Meta2020 legacy. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s X field experiment finds algorithmic feeds cause asymmetric, persistent conservative attitude shifts via a following-behavior mechanism that helps reconcile the earlier Meta null results, while [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment during the 2024 election shows engagement-based feeds amplify moralized/toxic content and distort social-norm perceptions — but that a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment, offering a rare prosocial-design intervention. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] audits the Musk-era Twitter/X transition itself, finding right-leaning visibility gains are better explained by agitating content and proximity to Musk than by ideology per se. [[Votta2025-xz]] examines ad-delivery algorithms' role in political campaign reach and cost, and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] uncovers X's recommender system inadvertently encoding users' ideological position as a near-linear embedding direction, with direct implications for profiling law. Crowdsourced moderation alternatives receive critical scrutiny too: [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows Community Notes structurally undermoderates polarizing content by design, a concern [[Miller2026-cp]]'s own account of the AI Note Writer API partially answers from the platform side, while [[Goldberg2026-eb]] situates bridging-based systems within a broader agenda for AI-augmented digital public squares.

## Fact-checking, moderation retreat, and the post-2025 rupture

A final, more recent cluster captures the sharp 2025 reversal in platform governance norms following Meta's termination of its Third-Party Fact-Checking Program. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically dismantles Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing, finding content removal occurs in only ~30% of fact-checked cases and that fact-checkers overwhelmingly favor transparency and counter-speech over deletion, while expressing skepticism toward Community Notes. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines how fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform-funding dependencies via apologia strategies, and [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the broader destabilization of the professional "trust and safety" field amid layoffs and political pressure. [[Donovan2025-ws]], [[Bruns2026-yv]], and [[Boyd2026-op]] offer longer-durée diagnoses of this retreat: misinformation-at-scale as a structural feature of engagement-driven business models, Twitter's "death" as a space for prosocial public debate under Musk, and a broader claim that "social media" has become "parasocial media" no longer capable of generating genuine social fabric — a thesis echoed by [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]'s account of TikTok's shift from networked to algorithmically-clustered publics. [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Karo2026-dn]] extend moderation's limits into extremism research, showing how deplatformed figures and jihadist content persist through "ambient" recirculation and vernacular camouflage that structurally outpaces removal. [[Jurg2025-ur]] closes the loop back to regulation, auditing YouTube's 2024 EU election moderation and arguing that transparency itself must be reconceived as "observability" requiring expanded researcher data access — a fitting bookend to the DSA-era data-access debates with which this literature began. Cross-cutting legal and infrastructural questions are addressed by [[Ahuja2025-ku]] (mapping dark patterns onto DSA Article 25), [[Crosset2026-mq]] (comparing EU/US content-moderation regimes as security governance), [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] (platform accountability and democratic backsliding), [[Park2026-tr]] (legal chilling effects on platform researchers), and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]], [[Swartz2026-zb]] (platforms as scam infrastructure demanding gatekeeper-focused liability regimes) — together indicating that platform governance research is expanding well beyond misinformation into fraud, dark patterns, security law, and AI-mediated persuasion ([[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]], [[Kasianenko2026-tn]], [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]]), even as foundational comparative-media-systems scholarship like [[Hallin2004-kg]] continues to anchor how these platform dynamics are read against national regulatory and journalistic traditions. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] round out the empirical base with studies of alternative-platform polarization and independent ad-auditing in Brazil, both gesturing toward the same unresolved question that structures this entire topic: who gets to see what platforms do, and on what terms.
