# Health Misinformation & Infodemic Research

## Mapping the Infodemic: Exposure, Coordination, and Correction

The papers filed here trace an arc from *how much* misinformation people actually encounter, through *how* it circulates as an organized phenomenon, to *what can be done* about it — methodologically and institutionally. Read together, they push back against a simple contagion story in which health misinformation is ubiquitous and indiscriminately consumed, replacing it with a more structured picture: exposure is concentrated, circulation is often coordinated and strategically laundered through legitimate gatekeepers, and correction is itself a contested communicative act.

### How much exposure, and to whom?

Two papers use behavioral tracking rather than self-report to ask a deceptively basic question — who actually sees low-quality health content? [[Lyons2026-ca]] finds that in the US, exposure to low-credibility health websites and YouTube videos is rare in aggregate but starkly concentrated: the top 10% of users account for over three-quarters of all exposure, with older adults, poor health-discernment individuals, and consumers of dubious political news disproportionately represented. Crucially, this exposure is not driven by search engines or social platforms but by referrals from other low-credibility sites, suggesting a habitual, cross-domain consumption ecology rather than incidental encounters. [[Adam2026-tz]] complements this with panel-plus-web-tracking data from the German-speaking pandemic outbreak, showing that conspiracy belief formation is likewise not a matter of blanket exposure but of a "marriage" between media content and preexisting predispositions: populist and mistrustful individuals selectively engage with conspiracy-supporting alternative media and avoid — or counterargue against — debunking content in mainstream outlets. Both studies converge on a key methodological point: real behavioral exposure data reveals selection and concentration effects invisible to survey-based prevalence estimates, and both complicate the assumption that mainstream media exposure is uniformly protective (Adam2026-tz notes that even quality outlets carried substantial conspiracy-supporting content).

### From correlation to causal and mechanistic accounts

Where these exposure studies establish who is exposed, [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes toward causal inference at the population level, building a novel SIRVA compartmental epidemic model that links geolocated antivaccine tweets to county-level vaccination, case, and death data via Bayesian estimation. Its lower-bound estimate — roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and several hundred additional cases attributable to Twitter antivaccine content — offers a rare bridge between platform-level speech and offline epidemiological outcomes, echoing Adam2026-tz's "contagion effect" but at a coarser, population-scale resolution. Meanwhile, [[Scalco2026-bd]] approaches the ecosystem from the supply side, proposing a quantitative operationalization of "information voids" — anomalous imbalances between information demand (Wikipedia, Google Trends) and supply (Facebook, Twitter, GDELT) — and showing across six European countries that these voids are empirically associated with reduced credible content and elevated misinformation prevalence. Together with Bollenbacher2026-vz, this establishes a mechanistic throughline: the *structure* of the information environment (voids, hesitancy dynamics, coordinated amplification) is not incidental background but a causal contributor to health outcomes.

### Coordination as infrastructure: mapping nefarious networks

A cluster of Italy-based papers, evidently part of a sustained MINE-FACTS-style research program (echoing the topic's Gates Foundation/MUR funding lineage), treats coordinated link-sharing behavior (CLSB) as the operational signature of problematic health information. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] is the anchor: it documents the rise of an Italian covid-skeptic coordinated network via the CooRnet package, details evasion tactics (link laundering, first-comment placement, religious-group infiltration), and reports on a working prototype fact-checking tool tested with Facta.news that surfaced problematic content at nearly double the hit-rate of routine fact-checking. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this network mapping into content analysis, showing that an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of alternative influencers generates disproportionate share-driven engagement, and — crucially — that legacy journalists and right-leaning newspapers remain central nodes, with AIN narratives frequently built by *remediating* televised or printed expert statements rather than fabricating claims outright. This finding about hybridity and remediation dovetails with [[Song2025-yh]], which applies the same CLSB/CooRnet methodology comparatively to the UK and US, finding that coordination itself is ideologically agnostic — dense anti-vaccine networks dominate in the UK (framed around safety concerns) while larger pro-vaccine networks dominate in the US (institutionally grounded, framed around individual freedom debates on the opposing side). Read as a set, Giglietto2022, Marino2023, and Song2025 argue that coordination detection is a transferable, content-agnostic diagnostic — but that what gets coordinated, and to what political effect, is deeply shaped by national media and political culture.

### Gatekeepers and the politics of visibility

Two papers examine how *legitimate* institutional gatekeepers, not just fringe coordinated actors, shape which health and science voices circulate. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] shows that Google News Italia's aggregation choices favor medical-scientific and healthcare-system stories, but that Facebook engagement rewards emotionally charged categories like celebrity health news and healthcare malfunction — a dual-edged dynamic that can either scaffold health literacy or amplify sensationalized debate. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] extends the gatekeeping lens to scientific expertise itself, showing that UK newspapers of different political orientations systematically differ in which COVID-19 scientists they cite (Great Barrington Declaration versus John Snow Memorandum signatories), with polarization visible even in academic co-authorship and Twitter-sharing networks — notably among mainstream, non-fringe scientists. Together with Marino2023's remediation finding, this suggests that infodemic dynamics cannot be reduced to malicious coordination alone: ordinary editorial selection and political alignment of legitimate media outlets are themselves vectors that structure the visibility of competing scientific claims.

### Correcting the record: the pragmatics of fact-checking

Finally, [[Xue2025-bp]] turns the analytic lens onto the correctives themselves, finding that fact-checking posts on US social media are frequently emotionally charged despite the practice's normative claim to dispassionate objectivity — and that this emotionality shapes both engagement and audience sentiment toward fact-checked targets. This sits in productive tension with the tool-building work of Giglietto2022: while the latter treats fact-checking capacity as a scarce resource to be scaled via automated, content-agnostic detection, Xue2025-bp reminds us that even when fact-checks reach audiences, their rhetorical form — not just their veracity content — determines uptake, closing the loop between detection infrastructure and the ultimately human, affectively mediated task of persuasion.
