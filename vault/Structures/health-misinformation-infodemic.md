# Health Misinformation & Infodemic Research

## The shape of the infodemic: coordination, voids, and consequences

The papers filed here trace a shared analytic problem — how problematic health information circulates online, who coordinates its spread, and whether that spread actually matters for offline health behavior — across three funding-linked research communities (Gates Foundation-supported cross-national infodemic mapping, and Italian MUR-funded platform studies). Rather than treating "misinformation" as a fixed category of false content, the collection increasingly reframes the infodemic as an *ecosystem* problem: one of supply, demand, coordination, and asymmetric attention, with truth-value often a poor predictor of harm.

### Mapping coordinated actors: the Italian CLSB program

A cluster of Italian studies builds a methodological throughline around Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) and the CooRnet toolkit. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] is the anchor: it documents the MINE-FACTS project, showing how a content-agnostic, behavior-first detection approach can surface problematic COVID-19 content on Facebook faster than manual fact-checking, and traces the genealogy of an Italian covid-skeptic network emerging from pre-existing political coordination (League, Five Star). [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this dataset to ask *who* gets amplified within these networks, identifying an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of influencers whose content — often recycled from legacy TV and print via remediation — achieves disproportionate share-driven engagement despite low posting volume. Together these two papers argue that coordination is not a marginal phenomenon at the fringe but is deeply entangled with mainstream media and politics: link laundering, image-macro cross-posting, and religious-group infiltration are tactics of evasion, while legacy journalists and right-leaning outlets remain central nodes even for ostensibly "alternative" narratives.

[[Song2025-yh]] widens this coordination lens cross-nationally (UK/US) and complicates the moralized framing of CLSB itself: coordination is "ideologically agnostic," deployed as heavily by pro-vaccine institutional actors (NHS, CDC) as by anti-vaccine communities, with national political culture shaping content (UK anti-vaccine discourse emphasizes safety; US emphasizes individual freedom). This paper functions as a bridge between the Italian CLSB tradition and the broader comparative, cross-national ambition of the topic — showing that the same behavioral signature can serve public health communication or its opposite, depending on who wields it and where.

[[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] sits adjacent to this cluster, shifting from coordinated networks to algorithmic gatekeeping: Google News Italia's aggregation choices structure which health topics reach Facebook audiences at all, with emotionally charged categories (celebrity health, healthcare malfunction) generating outsized engagement even though scientific/medical-system topics dominate raw supply. This paper anticipates the "supply vs. engagement" mismatch that becomes central to later demand-side work in the topic.

### From detection to demand: information voids and exposure concentration

Where the CLSB papers ask who coordinates supply, [[Scalco2026-bd]] asks a complementary question: what happens when supply *fails* to meet demand? Its "information void" framework — operationalizing gaps between search/Wikipedia demand and Facebook/Twitter/news supply — gives the topic a quantitative vocabulary for a phenomenon the CLSB papers describe qualitatively (e.g., the opportunistic exploitation of uncertainty around AstraZeneca or Sputnik V). Empirically, voids are shown to correlate with drops in credible content and rises in misinformation share, directly linking a structural/ecosystem property to content quality — a mechanistic complement to Giglietto and Marino's actor-centred accounts.

[[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Adam2026-tz]] push the demand-side question toward individual exposure patterns using web-tracking data, outside the Italian CLSB tradition but squarely within the same infodemic paradigm. Lyons shows that low-credibility health exposure is rare in aggregate but extremely concentrated (older adults, poor discernment, cross-domain "dubious" content consumers) and — crucially — driven by referrals from other low-credibility sites rather than search or social media, undercutting platform-centric intervention models. Adam's German/Swiss panel-plus-tracking study complicates the alternative-vs-mainstream-media dichotomy that underlies much of the coordination literature: mainstream media itself circulated substantial conspiracy-supporting content, and predispositions (populism, mistrust) determine whether exposure reinforces or triggers backfire. Both papers converge on a claim increasingly central to the topic: exposure is not randomly distributed, and *who* is exposed (and with what prior beliefs) matters more than raw volume of "bad" content in the ecosystem.

### Does any of this matter? Causal impact and its limits

The most direct interrogation of consequence appears in the pairing of [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] and [[Allen2024-av]], which both attempt to move from correlational infodemic mapping to causal estimates of behavioral harm, but reach strikingly different emphases. Bollenbacher's SIRVA compartmental model estimates that antivaccine Twitter content causally suppressed vaccination rates enough to produce measurable (if modest) excess cases and deaths, validating the core assumption motivating tools like MINE-FACTS: that platform coordination has downstream epidemiological cost. Allen, by contrast, delivers a more unsettling result for the field: fact-check-flagged misinformation, despite being more persuasive per view, had negligible aggregate impact because of its tiny reach, while unflagged, factually-accurate-but-"harmful-to-health"-framed content from mainstream outlets drove the overwhelming majority of vaccine hesitancy. This directly challenges the CLSB/coordination paradigm's implicit target (falsity, fringe coordination) and instead relocates the policy problem toward mainstream media framing — echoing Adam's finding that quality media itself circulates conspiracy-adjacent content, and Ghezzi's finding (below) that polarization operates through legitimate scientific voices, not just fringe actors.

### Polarization without falsehood, and the rhetoric of correction

[[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] extends this destabilization of the misinformation/legitimate-disagreement boundary furthest: UK newspapers' selective citation of GBD versus JSM signatories shows political polarization operating entirely among mainstream, non-fringe scientists, with no misinformation in the classical sense — only selective amplification. This sits provocatively next to the coordination papers, suggesting that "problematic" circulation dynamics (selective amplification, bubble formation) generalize beyond conspiracy content to elite scientific disagreement itself.

Finally, [[Xue2025-bp]] turns the lens onto the correctors rather than the corrected: fact-checking posts themselves are shown to rely on emotional framing to drive engagement, in tension with the genre's claim to dispassionate objectivity. Read alongside Giglietto's and Marino's accounts of how covid-skeptic networks evade and outpace fact-checkers, and Allen's finding that flagging strategies address only a sliver of actual persuasive harm, this paper closes the topic's arc on a reflexive note: the infodemic response apparatus — fact-checking, labeling, void-detection tools — is itself an actor in the attention economy it seeks to correct, subject to the same engagement pressures documented throughout the corpus.
