# Computational Text Analysis & LLMs

## The arc of the topic

This collection traces a field in rapid, somewhat anxious consolidation: LLMs have moved from a curiosity to a default instrument of text analysis in political and social-media research, and the papers filed here document both the expansion of what the instrument can do and the mounting evidence that its outputs cannot be trusted without bespoke validation. Read together, they sketch a methodological frontier moving from "can LLMs do X" demonstrations toward second-order questions about what validating an LLM pipeline even means — directly relevant to how this should be taught at the PhD level.

## From classification to measurement: what LLMs are being asked to do

A first cluster simply extends the classification/annotation toolkit established by supervised ML into LLM territory. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to classify collective-action frames in extremist Telegram discourse, grounding a causal argument about online–offline mobilization cycles in classical content-analysis categories — a reminder that not all computational text work here is LLM-based; it is the baseline against which LLM-era claims should be judged. [[Meher2025-qb]] (ConflLlama) and [[Bailard2024-pj]] share a lineage with older event-coding traditions (GTD, Schrodt, ConfliBERT), but ConflLlama shows how QLoRA fine-tuning of open-weight LLMs can leapfrog BERT-era approaches on rare-category detection while staying within consumer hardware budgets — a democratization argument echoed methodologically by [[Balluff2026-if]]'s insistence that smaller, open models often suffice.

Beyond classification, several papers push LLMs toward genuinely new *measurement* tasks that older NLP could not easily perform: positioning political actors on ideological scales by "asking and averaging" ([[Le-Mens2025-qz]]), inferring latent partisan identity from ostensibly nonpolitical text ([[Lee2026-je]]), quantifying cross-lingual "narrative similarity" between documents ([[Waight2025-al]]), and operationalizing Greimas' actantial model to measure "narrative polarisation" in YouTube/comment ecosystems ([[Elfes2026-jb]]). These papers share a move from classification (is X present?) to relational and comparative measurement (how does X's narrative position relate to Y's?), and collectively argue that LLMs make previously intractable estimands — narrative structure, implicit ideology, rhetorical role-assignment — newly measurable. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] extend this to framing analysis freed from fixed, predefined frame categories, while [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] inverts the logic: rather than treating the LLM as a measurement instrument to be validated against a fixed codebook, it treats divergence between LLM and human codes as a *diagnostic* for refining the codebook itself, positioning the model as an "analytical collaborator" rather than a classifier.

## Embeddings and clustering: finding structure without supervision

A second strand concerns unsupervised and semi-supervised structure-finding via embeddings. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and its companion piece [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] form a tight pair: the former benchmarks OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against the Italian-specific UmBERTo for clustering political news, while the latter documents the full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline (classification → embedding → cluster labeling) built on those results and confronts, head-on, the validation problem that gives this topic its title — the difficulty of finding competent human evaluators, the variable granularity of LLM-generated cluster narratives, and the need for task-specific rather than monolithic validation. [[Fan2025-ut]] addresses a more technical but closely related problem: embeddings routinely encode confounds (source, language) that corrupt similarity-based clustering, and linear concept erasure (LEACE) offers a cheap, principled fix — a methodological complement to the embedding-choice question raised by Giglietto. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the embedding-clustering paradigm into the visual domain, arguing that vision-LLMs can capture *connotative* (socially meaningful) rather than merely denotative image content, directly paralleling Achmann-Denkler's and Arora's multimodal turn in political communication. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] itself shows GPT-4o outperforming specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person-counting in campaign imagery, reinforcing a recurring finding across this topic: general-purpose multimodal LLMs increasingly beat bespoke pipelines on narrowly specified tasks, at the cost of interpretability and reproducibility that other papers worry about.

## The validation problem, generalized

If there is a thesis uniting this topic, it is that "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipelines generate a validation crisis distinct from that of earlier supervised-ML text analysis. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] names this problem explicitly for narrative/cluster labeling; [[Ober2026-vd]] encounters the same problem in qualitative coding, proposing topic modeling as a transparent scaffold onto which LLM labeling can be layered without sacrificing human interpretive authority; [[Brown2025-jk]] interrogates a different validity threat — demographic bias in LLM annotation of subjective labels — and finds that item difficulty (annotator disagreement) swamps demographic effects, a reassuring but methodologically important null result that complicates simpler bias narratives. [[Paci2025-ag]] stress-tests LLMs on pragmatic inference (implicature, presupposition) in real political speech and finds a persistent gap between model and expert interpretation, suggesting limits on how far "LLM as annotator" can be pushed for genuinely difficult, context-laden political meaning. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys this entire sub-literature on LLM-based annotation and synthesis, situating the topic's many bespoke validation protocols within a broader emerging methodological subfield. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] raises the stakes further by testing whether AI coding *agents* (not just annotators) can reproduce published social-science findings outright — an important bridge from validating single annotation tasks to validating end-to-end LLM-mediated replication, while also documenting new failure modes (sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting) that are directly relevant to how LLM-in-the-loop workflows might be gamed or self-deceived.

[[Balluff2026-if]] functions as something of a critical conscience for the whole topic: it collects the trade-offs — reproducibility (closed, shifting commercial models), environmental cost, demographic/linguistic bias, corporate dependency — that the more applied papers tend to discover piecemeal, and argues for a default preference toward smaller, open, task-appropriate models. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] offers a concrete illustration of exactly this dynamic in the fact-checking domain: general model capability (reasoning, web search) does far less for accuracy than curated retrieval context, and even search-augmented models show a left-leaning citation skew — a finding that should give pause to anyone treating "bigger/newer model" as a proxy for "better measurement."

## Downstream of measurement: persuasion, bias, and institutional influence

A further set of papers moves past text-analysis methodology per se into using LLMs as social actors — chatbots that persuade, debunk, or converse — but they remain tightly linked to this topic's validation concerns because their findings depend on the same measurement infrastructure. [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] show LLM dialogue durably shifting conspiracy beliefs and policy attitudes; [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically decomposes *why* conversational AI persuades (information density, post-training, prompting) and documents a persuasion–accuracy trade-off; [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] both show that perceived source characteristics (partisan bias, disclosed persuasive intent) materially attenuate these effects, adding "perceived neutrality/transparency" as a lever alongside Hackenburg's. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the intuitive expectation that conspiracy-prone users would distrust balanced AI chatbots. These papers collectively suggest that the same models being validated as annotation tools are simultaneously being deployed as communicative agents, and that measurement questions (does the model classify accurately) and persuasion questions (does the model change minds, and should it) are increasingly entangled — as [[Waight2026-ts]] shows starkly, when state media control literally shapes what "neutral" LLM output means in the first place.

## Infrastructure, scale, and the limits of the paradigm

Finally, several papers push the topic toward its outer edges: [[Fan2026-af]] argues that digital-trace analysis needs a temporal/sequential reorientation for which language-based (embedding) models are promising but data-starved; [[Kim2026-br]] and [[Nenno2025-xa]] apply explainable classifiers and news-values coding at a cross-national, decades-long scale that only computational pipelines make feasible, implicitly testing the generalizability concerns raised by [[Balluff2026-if]] and [[Lai2024-to]]'s ideology-scaling work. [[Matias2025-px]] and [[Goldberg2026-eb]] zoom out to governance and participatory-science perspectives, asking not just whether LLM pipelines are internally valid but whether the people affected by them should shape evaluation criteria and public-square design — a normative frame that complements the technical validation debates elsewhere in the topic. [[Ramos2026-qo]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]], though focused respectively on disinformation narrative-tracking and automated scientific review, round out the topic by showing the same "LLM-in-the-loop with human oversight" logic propagating into adjacent professional practices — fact-checking and peer review — suggesting that the validation challenges catalogued here for political-text analysis are a special case of a much broader epistemic problem posed by LLM-mediated knowledge work.
