# Electoral Campaigns on Social Media

## The MINE programme and its comparative arc

The topic is anchored by the empirical programme initiated in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] and [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], which established coordinated link sharing behaviour (CLSB) as an ecological, action-based alternative to content- or actor-centred disinformation detection during the 2018 Italian election and 2019 European election. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] situates this early work theoretically, naming the blurring of party boundaries, micro-targeting, and strategic amplification as the three structural shifts that make old media-effects methods inadequate. The programme's longest-running thread then bifurcates methodologically and geographically. Methodologically, it moves from manual/statistical detection toward LLM-assisted pipelines: [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] and [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] validate embedding- and classifier-based tools for clustering Italian political news across the 2018 and 2022 elections, while [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends CLSB into a continuously updating workflow applied to the 2022 Italian snap election, uncovering not only partisan hyperpartisan networks but click-economy and religious-proselytism operations that content-based detection would miss. Geographically, the programme scales outward into cross-national comparison: [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s US newsfeed-quality framework to Germany, France and Italy, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares concentrated in election years and among older users, while [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] turn to the Meta Content Library — the DSA's flagship transparency infrastructure — to show that Facebook actively and unevenly curates visibility (partisan penalties, journalistic-quality rewards, and a covert Italian political-content-reduction rollout that suppressed mainstream MPs' reach while extremist accounts compensated by posting more). Together these papers trace a single argument: platforms are not neutral pipes but actively governed systems whose governance is itself an object of empirical, often DSA-enabled, study.

## National campaigns as case studies: Italy, Germany, Brazil, Europe

A second cluster uses single-election fieldwork to test how platform-specific affordances shape campaign style. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] documents the earliest Italian hybrid-media moment — Twitter as second screen to televised talk shows in 2013 — finding narrow, TV-dominated participation, a baseline against which later Italian coordination studies can be read. The German 2021 and 2025 elections generate two complementary infrastructural papers: [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o) for visual political communication at scale, revealing distinct front-runner visibility strategies across Instagram stories/posts and party/candidate accounts, while [[Schulte2026-df]] documents the practical bottlenecks of multi-platform (X/TikTok) collection for the 2025 election, arguing that platform observability itself must become reusable infrastructure. Brazil furnishes the most extensive single-country arc: from ad-transparency auditing in 2018 ([[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]]) through crisis-communication and narrative-authority struggles during COVID ([[Rodarte2026-dk]]), to WhatsApp's role in 2022 coordination and post-election mobilization ([[Kansaon2025-id]], [[Ventura2025-sw]]), and finally to longitudinal affective-engagement dynamics within pro-Bolsonaro Facebook networks that destabilize the echo-chamber assumption ([[Marino2026-he]]). European-level elections (2024) receive parallel treatment in media-visibility studies of the Radical Right ([[Iris2026-pg]]), platform-specific audits of inauthentic Facebook sharing in Romania ([[Oprea2025-lf]]) and YouTube content moderation ([[Jurg2025-ur]]), and party-level visual strategy on TikTok ([[Bouchafra2026-ts]]), collectively showing that cross-national comparison is now a settled methodological norm rather than an afterthought.

## Coordinated and inauthentic networks beyond Italy

The CIB paradigm generalizes well beyond its founding Italian case. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] applies it to Serbia's SNS, arguing that mimetic, decentralized manipulation evades detection tools built for centralized bot farms; [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes Philippine influence operations as political brokerage embedded in political-economic infrastructure; and [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] revisit the 2020 and 2016 US elections respectively, the former mapping the concentrated, largely reshare-driven reach of 49 Meta-identified deceptive networks, the latter documenting geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising with measurable turnout effects. Read together with the Italian and Brazilian coordination work, this cluster suggests CIB is a genuinely cross-regime phenomenon whose detection methods must adapt to local media ecologies — a point [[Rodarte2026-dk]] makes explicitly for how politicians themselves become narrative producers competing with journalism.

## Effects on attitudes, trust, and polarization

A third strand asks what all this exposure actually does to citizens. The Brazilian panel-survey papers by Rossini and colleagues ([[Rossini2026-jn]], [[Rossini2026-mj]]) show that belief in electoral misinformation — rather than platform use per se — predicts both intolerance and declining trust in election results, with messaging apps operating indirectly through misinformation belief and mainstream news use functioning as a resilience factor even among Bolsonaro voters. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] extends this logic experimentally, showing that media narratives *about* interference (not interference itself) can produce asymmetric partisan polarization in the UK. The large platform-collaboration studies — [[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], and the meta-commentary [[Munger2025-cz]] — largely report null or fragile effects of ad exposure and misinformation diffusion on attitudes, prompting Munger's methodological verdict that one-off platform experiments lack the "temporal validity" to keep pace with evolving systems. Against this backdrop, [[Gauthier2026-iq]] and [[Brady2026-ln]] offer sharper causal purchase: X's algorithmic feed shifts attitudes rightward via persistent following-network changes, and a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm on Bluesky can reduce norm misperception and perceived animosity, together suggesting that algorithmic *architecture*, not mere exposure, is the operative causal variable — a claim reinforced structurally by [[McNally2025-dn]], which detects Facebook's News Feed interventions as lagged, non-black-box effects on hard-news engagement, and by [[Smith2025-kc]], which identifies "attention brokerage" as a causal micro-mechanism of network structure formation.

## Elites, ideology, and the supply side of misinformation

A parallel line interrogates who produces polarizing content rather than who consumes it. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation-sharing, but their conjunction — radical-right populism — does; [[Knupfer2025-vt]] traces how the CRT issue let ideologically extreme Republican factions build symbiotic ties to hyper-partisan media, rewarded by engagement; [[Tai2026-qk]] asks whether institutional and ideological position checks elite misinformation-sharing; and [[Eady2025-vm]] provides the underlying measurement infrastructure, showing URL-sharing behaviour cleanly separates politicians by party and correlates with electoral competition. [[Balluff2026-bv]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] extend the elite-capture logic to media systems themselves — Austrian government-advertising bribery and the relationship between far-right electoral performance and voter disinformation concern, respectively — while [[Lai2024-to]] and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] supply complementary tools for scaling ideology-estimation to video content and multi-level news circulation.

## Infrastructure, access, and the future of the field

A final, increasingly self-reflexive cluster addresses the conditions of possibility for all this research. [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Lukito2026-nb]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] chart the shift from ad hoc scraping toward DSA-mandated researcher access, cataloguing persistent inequities (the "price, proficiency, permission" problem) and arguing that current turbulence reflects regulation finally biting rather than failing. [[Votta2025-xz]] shows that even with data access, ad-delivery algorithms themselves opaquely shape political-ad reach and cost, reinforcing the paper's core argument — echoed from [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] through [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] — that transparency infrastructure is not a precondition external to the phenomenon but an integral, contested part of what electoral campaigns on social media now are. Finally, [[Lin2025-xp]] gestures toward the field's next frontier, showing that conversational AI dialogue can out-persuade traditional ads across four national elections, a finding that will likely reorganize this entire research programme's next decade around generative, rather than merely algorithmic or coordinated, manipulation of the electorate.
