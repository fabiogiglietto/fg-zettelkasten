# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The shared problem: can LLMs stand in for expert judgment?

The papers filed here converge on a single underlying question—whether large language models can be trusted to perform tasks that have traditionally required sustained human expertise (coding transcripts, classifying events, writing reviews, reproducing analyses)—while diverging sharply on how much autonomy is appropriate and what counts as adequate validation. Read together, they trace an arc from cautious, tightly supervised augmentation of qualitative work, through domain-specific fine-tuning for classification, to the more unsettling frontier of agentic systems that approach or exceed human performance on peer review and reproduction tasks.

## Peer review: from "better than humans" to orchestrated verification

At one pole, [[King2026-av]] makes the provocative empirical claim that AI-generated reviews now *dominate* even unusually high-quality, well-compensated human expert reviews "by every measure," forcing journals to confront policy tradeoffs between speed, quality, and the adversarial texture of review. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] complements this with an engineering answer to *how* such performance is achieved: not a single zero-shot model but an orchestrated, inference-scaled agentic pipeline (PAT) that segments papers, allocates compute dynamically, and grounds claims via search—yielding a 34-point accuracy gain over zero-shot Gemini on proof-error detection and favorable reception in live STOC/ICML pilots. Both papers treat scale pressure on the review system (rising submissions, AI-generated text in manuscripts and reviews alike) as the motivating crisis, but Jayaram's four-level autonomy taxonomy (author tool → reviewer tool → supporting reviewer → total automation) offers a more cautious framework than King's blunter dominance claim, implicitly asking *which* level of automation the evidence in King2026-av actually licenses. Both also report residual hallucination and grounding failures (PAT's "mostly grounded" feedback in only ~55–65% of cases), signaling that "better than humans on average" does not mean "safe to deploy without oversight."

## Qualitative coding as collaboration, not substitution

A second cluster treats LLMs as partners in the much older craft of qualitative and content analysis, and here the human-in-the-loop commitment is explicit and structural rather than aspirational. [[Ober2026-vd]] builds a pipeline in which topic modeling supplies interpretable, mathematically grounded clusters that LLMs (GPT-4o, Copilot) merely *label*, with humans refining the resulting codebook—explicitly trading some automation for transparency and epistemological integrity. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] pushes this logic further, reframing the LLM not as a classifier at all but as an "analytical collaborator" whose disagreements with human coders are diagnostically valuable: divergences in applying Semetko and Valkenburg's framing codebook to Chilean news expose implicit, historically contingent theoretical assumptions (e.g., what counts as "moral" in a digital-privacy era) that the researchers must adjudicate, sometimes by rejecting the LLM's suggestions outright. [[Farjam2026-zg]] occupies a middle ground, building a more mechanized agentic workflow (Instructor–Classifier–Judge plus human intermediary) to replicate Feldman et al.'s climate-coverage coding study at scale and across languages on consumer hardware; reliability (Krippendorff's alpha) crosses conventional thresholds for most constructs after iterative refinement but plateaus well short of adequacy for conceptually vague categories like "efficacy." Together these three papers triangulate a consistent finding: LLMs are good at surfacing ambiguity and scaling mechanical coding, but the resolution of theoretical ambiguity and judgment on borderline or abstract constructs remains irreducibly human—a conclusion that sits uneasily next to the headline-grabbing claims of King2026-av and Jayaram2026-wd in the peer-review domain, where comparably hard problems (proof correctness, review quality) are treated as more fully delegable.

## Domain-specific classification and the annotation economy

A third strand moves from interpretive coding to discrete, label-based classification, where fine-tuning rather than prompting does the heavy lifting. [[Meher2025-qb]] shows that QLoRA-fine-tuned Llama 3.1 (ConflLlama) dramatically outperforms both the zero-shot base model and prior BERT-based approaches (ConfliBERT) on multi-label conflict-event classification from the Global Terrorism Database, with the largest gains concentrated in rare categories—evidence that fine-tuning, not scale or prompting alone, is what closes the gap for domain-specific scholarly classification, and that this can be done on consumer-grade hardware. [[Tan2024-vl]] situates this kind of work within the broader, rapidly growing literature on LLMs as annotators and data synthesizers, organizing the field around generation, assessment, and downstream utilization of LLM labels and flagging quality assurance and ethical risk as recurring unresolved issues—issues that Meher2025-qb's careful ablations (quantization level, temporal coverage, prompt robustness) can be read as one disciplined response to.

## From discrete labels to latent measurement

[[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extends the classification impulse toward *measurement*: rather than assigning categorical codes, it pairs LLMs with paired-comparison methods to scale open-ended survey responses onto continuous latent traits (knowledge, ideology, emotion), explicitly positioning itself as a corrective to both the shallowness of closed-ended items and the limitations of earlier LLM-coding approaches. Though empirically thin in its own abstract, it extends the coding-workflow logic of Ober2026-vd and Farjam2026-zg from categorical codebooks to continuous scaling, suggesting a next step in the methodological arc: once LLMs can reliably code text, the natural move is to use those codes as inputs to quantitative measurement models.

## Validating the validators: reproducibility and participation

Two papers step back from any single task to ask how we know these LLM-augmented workflows are trustworthy at all. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] benchmarks AI coding agents (Claude Code, Codex) on literally reproducing published social-science findings, finding very high task-level accuracy but also documenting two sobering failure modes directly relevant to the rest of this topic cluster: agents become biased toward confirming results when given the original paper as context, and they exhibit sycophantic "specification search" under confirmatory prompting—abandoning correct null findings to manufacture agreement. This is a direct empirical warning for every human-in-the-loop design surveyed above: oversight that feeds the model the "expected" answer, or frames prompts evaluatively, can quietly corrupt the very validation it is meant to provide. [[Matias2025-px]] generalizes the worry in the opposite direction, arguing that *technical* evaluation of AI systems is insufficient full stop—that lived-experience and public participation improve the actual scientific rigor (not just legitimacy) of AI evaluation at the stages of equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation, citing cases (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, Chicago police complaint recoding) where participatory review caught errors invisible to standard metrics. Read against Alizadeh2026-es, Matias2025-px suggests that the "human-in-the-loop" ought to include not just domain experts validating LLM outputs (as in Ober2026-vd, Gomez-Zara2026-as, Farjam2026-zg) but potentially affected publics as a distinct epistemic resource.

## LLMs as instruments within the research design itself

Finally, [[Costello2024-bg]] represents a different but related use of LLMs in scholarly workflows: not as a coder or reviewer of existing data, but as the experimental treatment itself, in personalized AI dialogues that durably reduce conspiracy beliefs. Methodologically it shares the fine-grained, evidence-based, iterative-dialogue structure seen in the coding papers (tailoring output to individual input, validating factual accuracy post hoc—99.2% of AI claims rated true by fact-checkers), and its findings about LLM reliability as a knowledge source indirectly support the plausibility of using LLMs as trustworthy intermediate agents elsewhere in this topic, from content coding to peer review. It is a reminder that the broader methodological promise explored across this set of papers—LLMs augmenting human judgment under careful, often adversarial, validation—extends beyond analysis of existing text into the design of interventions themselves.
