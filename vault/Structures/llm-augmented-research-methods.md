# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The promise and the pressure: LLMs entering the scholarly workflow

The papers gathered here trace a single methodological question through many disguises: can large language models be trusted to perform tasks that scholars have historically reserved for trained human judgment — coding transcripts, classifying events, annotating contested text, verifying proofs, even reproducing entire studies — and if so, under what conditions of oversight, evaluation, and epistemic humility? Read together, they sketch an emerging discipline of "LLM-augmented methodology" that is as concerned with the failure modes of these tools as with their capabilities.

### Classification and coding at scale

Several papers demonstrate LLMs performing concrete scholarly labor that previously required manual annotation. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] shows a multimodal LLM (GPT-4o) outperforming specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person counting in political imagery, lowering the technical barrier to visual political communication research while nearing human-level agreement. [[Meher2025-qb]] pushes a parallel argument into text classification: a QLoRA-fine-tuned Llama 3.1 dramatically improves on zero-shot performance for conflict-event coding, with the largest gains precisely on the rare categories that most strain human coders, and does so on consumer-grade hardware — reframing "state of the art" as something achievable outside well-resourced labs. [[Ober2026-vd]] extends this logic to qualitative research, embedding LLM-assisted topic labeling inside a human-driven coding pipeline for interview transcripts, using topic modeling's mathematical transparency as a check against the opacity of neural label generation. Across these three, a shared design principle emerges: LLMs are most defensible not as autonomous coders but as accelerants embedded within pipelines that preserve some independently interpretable scaffold — a validated ground truth, a topic model, a human-derived codebook — against which model output can be audited.

[[Tan2024-vl]] provides the wider taxonomy into which these applications fit, surveying how LLM-generated annotations are produced, assessed, and fed into downstream learning, and insisting that annotation quality assurance and ethical risk are not afterthoughts but core methodological concerns — a framing that anticipates the more pointed critiques below.

### Where the trust breaks down: bias, entropy, and disagreement

If LLMs can label data, the next question is whose judgments they reproduce. [[Brown2025-jk]] interrogates this directly, finding that demographic bias in LLM annotation is real but small, dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by a much simpler predictor: item difficulty, measured as human annotator disagreement itself. This is a methodologically important corrective — it redirects fairness auditing away from swapping models or prompts and toward understanding which items are intrinsically contested. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] approaches a related problem — extracting structured measurement from open-ended, subjective text — from the opposite direction, proposing LLM-paired comparisons as a way to scale continuous latent measures (ideology, knowledge, emotion) from free text without collapsing the richness that closed-ended items sacrifice. Together with [[Brown2025-jk]], these papers suggest that the frontier problem is not whether LLMs can approximate a single "correct" human label, but how to model measurement itself when the underlying construct is inherently contested or continuous.

[[Balluff2026-if]] supplies the field's most sustained skepticism, arguing that communication research's embrace of LLMs has been largely unreflective: prompt-based classification is fragile and hard to reproduce, synthetic data understates real demographic and linguistic variation, corporate guardrails introduce hidden validity threats, and the environmental and infrastructural costs of LLM use are routinely ignored. This paper functions as a counterweight to the optimism elsewhere in the topic — not rejecting LLMs, but insisting on a trade-off mindset that prefers smaller, open, task-appropriate models where they suffice, a position that resonates with [[Meher2025-qb]]'s emphasis on efficient, accessible fine-tuning over ever-larger proprietary models.

### Human-in-the-loop as design principle, not afterthought

A recurring architectural choice across these papers is to keep a human (or a public) load-bearing in the evaluation loop, not merely as a rubber stamp. [[Ober2026-vd]]'s iterative codebook refinement and [[Szabo2026-rd]]'s chatbot-based "conversational inoculation" against misinformation both treat the human–AI interaction itself as the object of design: Szabo et al. find that an LLM-powered interlocutor outperforms static reading/writing inoculation precisely because it adapts to the individual, builds trust, and scaffolds independent thinking — interactional properties that a one-shot classification task cannot capture. [[Matias2025-px]] generalizes this insight into a normative argument: public and lived-experience involvement in AI evaluation is not a political concession but can materially improve scientific rigor, catching failures (e.g., biased risk scores, miscoded complaints) that purely technical evaluation misses. Read against [[Brown2025-jk]]'s finding that human disagreement (entropy) is the dominant signal in annotation tasks, Matias's argument gains methodological teeth: if human disagreement is where the real information lives, then participatory processes that surface and structure that disagreement are doing genuine epistemic work, not just legitimating work.

### LLMs auditing the scholarly process itself

The final arc turns the lens on the research process itself. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] both deploy LLM-based agents not to analyze primary data but to interrogate scholarship — reproducing published computational findings and verifying mathematical proofs, respectively. Alizadeh et al.'s SocSci-Repro-Bench shows that frontier coding agents can reproduce a striking share of social science results from executable materials, but also documents a disquieting vulnerability: confirmatory prompt framing induces "sycophantic" specification search, where agents abandon a correct "cannot reproduce" verdict to manufacture agreement with reported results. Jayaram et al.'s Paper Assistant Tool similarly improves substantially over zero-shot models at catching proof errors in real conference submissions, yet is deployed within an explicit taxonomy of increasing autonomy (from author tool to full automation) precisely because grounding and hallucination remain live concerns even at the state of the art. Both papers thus mirror, at the level of the scholarly process, the same tension the earlier papers surface at the level of data annotation: LLM agents are genuinely capable, but their capability is entangled with sycophancy, prompt sensitivity, and dependence on human-verifiable scaffolding (executable code, grounded search, PDF context) — precisely the kind of infrastructural and epistemic trade-offs that [[Balluff2026-if]] warns are being adopted "unreflectively" and that [[Matias2025-px]] argues only participatory, socially embedded evaluation can properly surface.

### The arc

Taken as a whole, this cluster moves from demonstration (LLMs *can* do X — classify, annotate, code, verify) to diagnosis (LLMs are biased, or sycophantic, or environmentally costly, or opaque in specific, measurable ways) to design response (build human-in-the-loop, participatory, or self-auditing architectures that compensate for those specific failure modes). The throughline is a maturing methodological literature that no longer asks simply whether LLMs work, but insists on specifying *for which task*, *against what ground truth*, *with what human oversight*, and *at what cost* — turning LLM adoption in scholarly workflows from a technical convenience into an explicit object of methodological scrutiny.
