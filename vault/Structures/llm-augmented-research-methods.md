# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The shift from automation to collaboration

The papers filed here collectively trace a methodological arc: LLMs enter scholarly workflows not as replacements for human judgment but as instruments whose value depends on how carefully they are integrated into it. [[Tan2024-vl]] frames the terrain broadly, surveying how LLMs can automate data annotation and synthesis and cataloguing the methodological choices—generation, assessment, utilization—that determine whether such automation is trustworthy. The rest of the collection can be read as empirical elaborations and complications of exactly those choices, applied to peer review, qualitative coding, survey analysis, and domain-specific classification.

## Qualitative coding as human-AI co-interpretation

Two papers tackle deductive and inductive qualitative analysis directly, and both converge on the same structural solution: LLMs should surface ambiguity and generate candidate structure, while humans retain final interpretive authority. [[Ober2026-vd]] integrates topic modeling with LLM-assisted labeling to refine a human-derived codebook on teacher conceptions of communication and digital literacy, arguing that topic modeling's mathematical transparency counterbalances the opacity of generative labeling, and that no hallucinations survived human review—only minor phrasing divergences. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] pushes this logic further by explicitly rejecting the "LLM as classifier" framing in favor of "LLM as analytical collaborator." Applying Semetko and Valkenburg's framing codebook to Chilean news, the authors find that LLM/human divergences are not noise but diagnostic: they expose implicit theoretical assumptions (e.g., what counts as "moral" framing) and reveal historically contingent category boundaries. Notably, the authors describe actively *rejecting* an LLM-suggested distinction as theoretically unsound—a concrete enactment of the human-in-the-loop principle that recurs across this whole topic. Together these two papers suggest that the most productive use of LLMs in qualitative research is not labeling accuracy per se but friction: the moments of disagreement that force researchers to articulate tacit theoretical commitments.

## Annotation, bias, and the limits of substitution

A second cluster interrogates whether LLMs can stand in for human annotators on contested, subjective tasks. [[Brown2025-jk]] offers a sobering corrective to fears of systematic demographic bias in LLM annotation: across four contentious datasets and three models, demographic effects are small and dataset-specific rather than model-specific, while item difficulty (human label entropy) dwarfs demographic identity as a predictor of LLM-human agreement. This reframes the fairness question away from "which model is biased" toward "which items are inherently contested," a methodological pivot with implications for how researchers should evaluate LLM annotation pipelines generally. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] approaches a related problem—scaling open-ended survey text into usable measures—from the measurement side, proposing LLM-paired comparisons as a way to extract continuous latent traits from free text without the labor costs of manual coding or the crudeness of closed-ended items. Both papers treat the LLM as a measurement instrument whose properties (bias, precision, calibration) must be characterized empirically rather than assumed.

## Domain-specific fine-tuning as a parallel strategy

[[Meher2025-qb]] represents a different but complementary strategy: rather than prompting a general-purpose model, fine-tune an open-source LLM (Llama 3.1 via QLoRA) directly on domain classification data—conflict event coding from the Global Terrorism Database. The payoff is large accuracy gains over zero-shot baselines, especially for rare categories, achieved on consumer-grade hardware, positioning efficient fine-tuning as a democratizing alternative to both manual coding and older BERT-based pipelines like ConfliBERT. This paper's emphasis on computational accessibility and prompt robustness complements the annotation-bias literature by showing that when tasks are well-specified and labeled data exists, targeted adaptation can outperform general-purpose prompting—raising the question of when researchers should fine-tune versus when they should design better prompts and human oversight loops, as in [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]].

## Agentic systems and the frontier of automation

The final cluster pushes beyond annotation into higher-stakes, more autonomous territory: reproducing findings and reviewing papers. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] benchmarks AI coding agents (Claude Code, Codex) on reproducing published social science results, finding surprisingly high accuracy but also documenting critical failure modes—agents become biased validators when given original PDFs, and they exhibit "sycophantic" specification search under confirmatory prompting. This paper is important for this topic because it empirically demonstrates *why* human-in-the-loop design matters: performance metrics alone mask systematic vulnerabilities that only show up under adversarial or naturalistic evaluation conditions. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] extends the automation question to peer review itself, describing Google's Paper Assistant Tool and its deployment across thousands of real conference submissions. Its proposed four-level taxonomy (author tool → reviewer tool → supporting reviewer → total automation) offers a useful organizing scheme for the whole topic: most papers here (Ober, Gomez-Zara, Brown, DiGiuseppe) operate at the "tool" end of this spectrum, while Alizadeh and Jayaram probe what happens as systems move toward higher autonomy, including new risks (hallucinated grounding, confirmatory bias) that only emerge at scale.

## A tangential but resonant case

[[Szabo2026-rd]] sits at the edge of this topic—its application is misinformation resistance rather than scholarly workflow—but it is methodologically relevant: it demonstrates that conversational, adaptive LLM interaction can outperform static content delivery (reading/writing) by engaging users dynamically, a finding that echoes the broader lesson of this topic that the *interactive design* of human-LLM exchange, not just model capability, drives outcomes. The interactional-friction and trust-building themes it identifies parallel concerns about hallucination, grounding, and researcher trust that recur in [[Jayaram2026-wd]] and [[Alizadeh2026-es]].

## Synthesis

Read together, these nine papers trace a consistent methodological commitment: LLMs are most defensible as research instruments when their outputs are treated as provisional, inspectable, and contestable rather than authoritative. Whether the task is coding interview transcripts, classifying terrorism events, annotating offensive text, or reviewing a mathematics proof, the strongest papers here build in mechanisms—topic-model scaffolding, entropy-aware evaluation, fine-tuning transparency, agent self-correction, taxonomies of autonomy—that preserve a channel for human correction and theoretical judgment. The frontier problem, visible most sharply in [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]], is that as these systems become more agentic and are deployed at greater scale, the human-in-the-loop safeguard becomes harder to maintain even as it becomes more necessary.
