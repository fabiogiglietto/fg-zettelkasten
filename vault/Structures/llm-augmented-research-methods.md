# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## Augmenting qualitative analysis without losing interpretive depth

A recurring concern across this literature is how to keep a human analyst meaningfully "in the loop" while letting LLMs do the heavy lifting of pattern discovery at scale. [[Ober2026-vd]] is the clearest articulation of this stance: topic modeling supplies a mathematically transparent first pass over interview transcripts, LLM prompting (GPT-4o and Copilot compared against each other) generates candidate topic labels, and a human analyst iteratively folds these into a refined codebook. The paper's emphasis on cross-model label agreement and the absence of hallucination after human review frames LLM assistance as a check-and-balance system rather than a replacement for grounded-theory-style coding. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the same logic to visual data, showing that vision-LLMs can convert images into connotative textual descriptions that are then clustered and audited via TF-IDF summaries—explicitly designed so that a human researcher can inspect *why* images were grouped together, addressing the opacity that has long dogged CNN-based image clustering in computational social science. Both papers converge on a shared methodological move: use an LLM to produce an intermediate, human-legible representation (a topic label, a cluster description) rather than a black-box classification, so that interpretability and scalability are pursued jointly rather than traded off.

[[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] pushes this agenda into survey research, proposing LLM-paired comparisons as a way to convert open-ended survey text into continuous latent scales—addressing the classic tension between the depth of open-ended responses and the tractability of closed-ended coding, and explicitly positioning itself as an improvement over "existing LLM-based approaches" to open-text scaling. Read alongside [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Arminio2025-tw]], it suggests a broader pattern: across text, image, and survey modalities, researchers are converging on hybrid pipelines where LLMs handle semantic heavy lifting but structured, auditable intermediate outputs preserve room for human correction.

## Domain-specific fine-tuning and classification at scale

Where the previous cluster asks how LLMs can *augment* human interpretive judgment, [[Meher2025-qb]] asks how far a fine-tuned open-source model can go *replacing* manual coding for a well-defined classification task. ConflLlama fine-tunes Llama 3.1 with QLoRA to classify terrorism event types from the Global Terrorism Database, and its central methodological claim is one of accessibility: strong classification performance (macro AUC 0.791) is achievable on consumer-grade hardware, democratizing domain adaptation for political scientists who lack industrial compute. This is a useful counterpoint to the hybrid, deliberately-slow pipelines above—here the argument is that with enough labeled data and efficient fine-tuning, an LLM can be made to internalize domain categories robustly (as shown by its prompt-invariance), reducing the need for continuous human oversight once the model is validated. The paper's framing explicitly positions LLM fine-tuning as the successor to rule-based and BERT-based event coding (e.g., ConfliBERT), raising a question the rest of the topic returns to: when is heavier human-in-the-loop scaffolding necessary, and when can a well-validated fine-tuned classifier stand largely on its own?

[[Tan2024-vl]] provides the meta-level scaffolding for this question, surveying the broader landscape of LLM-based data annotation and synthesis. Its organizing scheme—generation, assessment, and utilization of LLM-produced labels, plus attendant quality-assurance and ethical challenges—offers a vocabulary for classifying where each paper in this topic sits: [[Meher2025-qb]] is squarely a generation-and-utilization exercise on a single domain task, while [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Arminio2025-tw]] emphasize the assessment side, building interpretability checks directly into the pipeline.

## LLMs as inferential instruments over unstructured behavioral data

A related but distinct use of LLMs is not to code or classify a fixed scheme but to *infer* latent respondent or user attributes from free text at massive scale. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] uses Claude-powered classifiers to infer occupation, career stage, and productivity experience from ~81,000 open-ended survey responses, then links these inferred attributes to independently observed AI usage patterns. This is methodologically adjacent to [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]]'s paired-comparison scaling of open-ended text, but oriented toward large-N attribute inference rather than latent trait measurement, and it foregrounds a validity concern the other papers only gesture at: occupation could be inferred for just 39% of respondents, underscoring the coverage limits of LLM-based extraction even at industrial scale.

[[Fan2026-af]] broadens the computational toolkit further, surveying six methods—including "language-based models," i.e., neural embeddings—for analyzing digital trace data as temporally ordered user-sequences rather than aggregated cross-sectional measures. Its case study treats an embedding model as just one tool among sequence analysis, hidden Markov models, and process mining, explicitly noting that language-based approaches are promising but data-hungry. This positions LLM/embedding methods not as a universal solution but as one instrument in a larger computational-methods toolbox, echoing [[Tan2024-vl]]'s point that methodological choices about how models are used matter as much as model choice itself.

## Automating evaluation: peer review and reproducibility

The final arc of this topic turns LLMs onto the scholarly process itself—reviewing and reproducing research rather than analyzing primary data. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] describes Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an agentic system for deep review of math/CS manuscripts that substantially outperforms zero-shot Gemini on error detection and has been piloted at STOC and ICML with generally positive author reception. Its four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review (author tool → reviewer tool → supporting reviewer → full automation) offers a useful framework for locating every other paper in this topic along a human-oversight spectrum, from the heavily human-mediated coding of [[Ober2026-vd]] to the more autonomous classification of [[Meher2025-qb]]. Notably, PAT's own evaluation surfaces the same tension found elsewhere: grounding and hallucination remain live concerns even in a system explicitly built to reduce them, with roughly a third to nearly half of respondents at some venues reporting less-than-fully-grounded feedback.

[[Alizadeh2026-es]] pursues a structurally similar but distinct goal—not reviewing novel claims but reproducing published computational findings—via SocSci-Repro-Bench, evaluating Claude Code and Codex on 221 tasks across social-science disciplines. Its findings sharpen the human-in-the-loop debate raised throughout this topic: agents can reproduce results with high fidelity and even infer research questions abstractly, but the paper also documents concrete failure modes directly relevant to any deployment of LLMs as scholarly infrastructure—paper context biases agents toward false-positive reproduction, and confirmatory prompting induces sycophantic specification search. Together, [[Jayaram2026-wd]] and [[Alizadeh2026-es]] extend the topic's central methodological question—how to combine LLM scale with human (or human-designed) safeguards—from the analysis of empirical data to the evaluation of scholarship itself, closing the loop opened by [[Ober2026-vd]]'s insistence that human oversight remains essential even as LLMs take on ever more of the analytic burden.
