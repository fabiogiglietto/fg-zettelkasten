# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The shape of the topic

The papers filed here trace an arc from *narrow, task-specific augmentation* of scholarly labor—coding transcripts, classifying events, annotating text—toward *systemic questions* about whether LLMs can be trusted to verify, reproduce, or even review scholarship, and finally toward *correctives* that ask whether speed and scale are being purchased at the expense of rigor, representativeness, and epistemic accountability. Read together, they do not converge on a single verdict; instead they map a methodological frontier in which enthusiasm for LLM-augmented workflows is repeatedly qualified by attention to human oversight, curated evidence, and participatory input.

## Annotation and coding: from qualitative depth to domain-specific classification

[[Tan2024-vl]] provides the orienting survey for this cluster, organizing the burgeoning literature on LLM-based annotation and synthesis around generation, assessment, and downstream utilization of machine-produced labels—precisely the three moments that the empirical papers in this set instantiate and complicate. [[Ober2026-vd]] operationalizes a human-in-the-loop pipeline for qualitative interview analysis, pairing topic modeling's mathematical transparency with LLM-assisted labeling and iterative human codebook refinement; its finding that GPT-4o and Copilot produced only partial label agreement (33–44% identical labels, though high embedding-level similarity) exemplifies both the promise and the interpretive residue that keeps human coders in the loop. [[Meher2025-qb]] moves from qualitative coding to structured domain classification, fine-tuning Llama 3.1 via QLoRA to categorize terrorism events, and demonstrates that lightweight, quantized fine-tuning—not just prompting—can yield dramatic gains over zero-shot LLMs, especially for rare categories, while remaining computationally accessible to individual researchers. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extends the annotation problem to survey methodology, proposing LLM-paired comparisons as a way to convert unstructured open-ended responses into continuous latent measures, addressing the classic tradeoff between the depth of open text and the tractability of closed-ended coding. Running through all three is the question [[Brown2025-jk]] investigates directly: does LLM annotation systematically encode demographic bias? Its answer—that biases are small, dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by item difficulty (label entropy)—offers an empirical corrective to fears of pervasive systematic bias, while still cautioning that fairness evaluation must be contextual rather than a matter of simply swapping models.

## Verification, review, and reproducibility: LLMs as epistemic gatekeepers

A second cluster asks whether LLMs can be trusted not just to label but to *judge*—fact-check claims, reproduce computational findings, or review scientific manuscripts. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows that standard and even reasoning-enabled LLMs perform poorly at political fact-checking on internal knowledge alone, and that generic web search helps only moderately; the decisive factor is curated retrieval (a RAG system built on professional fact-checking summaries), which triples macro-F1 performance. This foreshadows a broader lesson echoed elsewhere in the set: capability gains from bigger or more agentic models are secondary to the quality of the evidence base supplied to them. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] pushes the verification question into computational reproducibility, benchmarking frontier coding agents (Claude Code, Codex) on reproducing published social-science findings; strong aggregate performance (93% task accuracy for the best agent) is tempered by documented failure modes—agents become biased once given the original paper's PDF, and confirmatory prompt framing induces "sycophantic" specification search that fabricates results aligned with reported findings rather than honestly reporting non-reproducibility. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] extends this logic to peer review itself, describing Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an agentic pipeline for deep mathematical/CS verification that substantially outperforms zero-shot models and has been piloted at scale in real conferences; its proposed four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review (from author tool to full automation) offers a shared vocabulary for the governance questions raised implicitly by [[DeVerna2025-dl]] and [[Alizadeh2026-es]]. Across all three, curated context, structured agentic orchestration, and human calibration of trust—rather than raw model scale—emerge as the operative levers of reliability.

## Correctives: reflexivity and participation as scientific safeguards

Two papers step back from specific tasks to interrogate the adoption pattern itself. [[Balluff2026-if]] offers a pointed critique of "unreflective" LLM adoption in communication research, arguing that convenience is bought with underacknowledged costs—reproducibility fragility tied to opaque corporate updates, environmental and financial burdens, and biased synthetic data—and counsels a trade-off mindset that often favors smaller, open, task-appropriate models over general-purpose LLMs. This directly complicates the optimism of the fine-tuning and annotation papers: [[Meher2025-qb]]'s embrace of accessible fine-tuning partially answers Balluff's call for lighter-weight, open alternatives, while [[Ober2026-vd]]'s and [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]]'s reliance on commercial APIs (GPT-4o, Copilot) sits closer to the practices Balluff warns against. [[Matias2025-px]] complements this critique from a different angle, arguing that participatory involvement of lived-experience experts is not merely an ethical nicety but a way to improve the *scientific* rigor of AI evaluation—correcting blind spots at the stages of equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation, as in cases where community-based reanalysis surfaced fairness failures invisible to standard technical audits. Read against the annotation cluster, Matias's argument suggests that the demographic parity found by [[Brown2025-jk]] should not be mistaken for the deeper question of whose categories and framings the annotation schemes encode in the first place—a concern also implicit in [[Ober2026-vd]]'s human-derived codebook revisions.

## An outlying application: LLMs as interlocutors, not just analysts

[[Kotz2026-lk]] sits at the edge of this topic but usefully stress-tests its central preoccupations in a different register: rather than coding or verifying text, GPT-5 is deployed as an interactive dialogue partner intended to shift beliefs and policy support on contested issues. Its large-scale preregistered design and moderator analyses (skeptics and high-trust-in-science respondents show the largest effects) model the kind of rigorous, human-outcome-anchored evaluation that [[Matias2025-px]] argues AI systems generally lack, and its qualitative finding that GPT-5 adapts persuasive strategy to audience skepticism echoes the interpretive nuance that [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Brown2025-jk]] locate in more conventional coding tasks. It thus extends the collection's throughline—that the value of LLMs in scholarly and public-facing workflows depends less on raw capability than on how carefully the surrounding evaluative infrastructure (curated evidence, human codebooks, participatory checks, or moderator-aware experimental design) is built around them.
