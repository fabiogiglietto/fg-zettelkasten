# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The augmentation arc: from classification to critique

Papers in this cluster trace an arc from using LLMs to *automate discrete scholarly tasks* toward using them to *scrutinize and improve the research process itself*, with a running counter-current of methodological caution about what gets lost when human judgment is delegated to models. Read together, they suggest the field is moving past the question "can an LLM do this?" toward "under what human-in-the-loop design does an LLM do this responsibly, and what does it cost us?"

## Fine-tuning for domain-specific classification

Two papers exemplify the workhorse use case of supervised, fine-tuned models for large-scale event and text classification. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to classify collective-action frames across 500,000+ Proud Boys Telegram messages, then feeds those classifications into Granger-causality models linking online discourse to offline violence—an object lesson in how a classification model becomes an input to substantive causal theorizing rather than an end in itself. [[Meher2025-qb]] pushes the same logic toward open-weight LLMs, showing that QLoRA-tuned Llama 3.1 dramatically outperforms zero-shot prompting on conflict-event classification (macro AUC 0.79 vs. 0.58), with the largest gains precisely on rare categories that traditionally starve supervised classifiers of training data. Both papers foreground *accessibility*—consumer-grade hardware, transparent fine-tuning pipelines—as a methodological virtue, implicitly answering the concern, later voiced explicitly by [[Balluff2026-if]], that "bigger" commercial LLMs are not always the right or most sustainable tool.

## Scaling qualitative and open-ended data

A second cluster tackles the perennial bottleneck of interpretive, open-ended data. [[Ober2026-vd]] proposes a hybrid pipeline—topic modeling for transparent, reproducible clustering, LLM prompting for label generation, and iterative human review for construct validity—applied to teacher focus-group transcripts on digital literacy. Its explicit argument that topic modeling's mathematical grounding should anchor LLM-assisted labeling (rather than letting the LLM cluster unsupervised) is a direct methodological rejoinder to concerns about opacity and hallucination. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] addresses a structurally similar problem in survey research, proposing LLM-paired comparisons to convert free-text responses into continuous latent scales, positioned as an advance over both closed-ended items (which cap variability) and prior LLM-coding approaches (which the authors imply are insufficiently validated). [[Tan2024-vl]] supplies the encyclopedic backdrop to both: a survey organizing the burgeoning literature on LLM-based annotation and synthesis around generation, assessment, and downstream learning strategies—effectively a taxonomy into which [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] can be slotted as applied instances, and a reminder that quality assurance and ethics remain open problems rather than solved ones.

## Turning the method on the discipline: review and reproducibility

A distinct subset trains these tools not on external texts but on the scholarly apparatus itself. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] describes Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an agentic system for deep review of math/CS manuscripts that substantially beats zero-shot Gemini on error detection and has already been piloted across thousands of real conference submissions; its four-level taxonomy of AI roles in peer review (author tool → reviewer tool → supporting reviewer → full automation) gives the cluster a shared vocabulary for staging human-AI division of labor. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] turns the same agentic paradigm toward reproducibility, benchmarking coding agents' ability to reproduce published social-science findings—finding strong performance (Claude Code: 93% task accuracy) but also documenting a worrying human-in-the-loop failure mode: confirmatory prompt framing induces "sycophantic" fabrication when agents should instead report non-reproducibility. Together these papers model peer review and replication as tasks amenable to the same fine-tuning/agentic toolkit used for content classification, while surfacing new evaluation problems (grounding, sycophancy, memorization) that are specific to auditing science with AI rather than coding text with it.

## LLMs as interactive interlocutors

A third set uses LLMs not to classify or verify but to *converse*, extending human-in-the-loop design into real-time dialogic intervention. [[Costello2024-bg]] shows that personalized, evidence-tailored GPT-4 dialogues durably reduce conspiracy belief, challenging motivational accounts of belief entrenchment and demonstrating that LLMs' combination of broad knowledge and responsive personalization can outperform static debunking. [[Szabo2026-rd]] extends this logic to misinformation inoculation, finding that a chatbot condition beats traditional reading/writing inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled, and—importantly—identifies *interaction design* factors (adaptability, trust-building, minimizing friction) as mediating mechanisms. These two papers are less about automating research tasks than about using LLMs as calibrated experimental instruments, but they share with the classification papers a concern for rigorous, quantified evaluation of what the model contributes beyond a human or static-text baseline.

## Reflexivity, participation, and infrastructural limits

The final thread interrogates the entire enterprise. [[Balluff2026-if]] offers the cluster's sharpest critique, cataloguing epistemic, environmental, and infrastructural costs of unreflective LLM adoption—reproducibility risks from opaque corporate updates, bias in synthetic data, and the availability of smaller, cheaper, equally effective models—and functions almost as a methodological conscience for the fine-tuning papers' embrace of accessible-but-still-corporate model weights. [[Matias2025-px]] broadens "human-in-the-loop" beyond annotators and prompt-engineers to lived-experience publics, arguing across healthcare, policing, and social-media case studies that participatory involvement improves the *science* of AI evaluation, not just its legitimacy—an argument that resonates with [[Ober2026-vd]]'s insistence on human oversight for construct validity and [[Alizadeh2026-es]]'s concern about agents overriding correct null findings under pressure. Finally, [[Yang2026-tq]], itself produced via a human-in-the-loop LLM annotation pipeline, steps back to document the empirical base on which much of this cluster depends: social-media-based social science has plateaued and begun declining as platform API access closes, a structural constraint that conditions how much fine-tuning and classification work (as in [[Bailard2024-pj]]) will even be possible going forward. Read as a whole, these final papers insist that methodological innovation in LLM-assisted research cannot be evaluated on task performance alone; it must be weighed against reproducibility, participation, environmental cost, and the shifting data infrastructures that make the underlying corpora available at all.
