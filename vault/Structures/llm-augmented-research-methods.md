# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The Shift from Automation to Collaboration

A clear throughline across this set is a move away from framing LLMs as replacements for human judgment toward framing them as *collaborators* embedded in iterative, human-supervised workflows. [[Ober2026-vd]] exemplifies this most explicitly: topic modeling supplies a transparent, mathematically grounded first pass at theme discovery, LLMs (GPT-4o, Copilot) generate candidate labels for those clusters, and human analysts retain final authority over codebook structure—explicitly rejecting the idea that LLMs should replace grounded-theory-style interpretive work. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] pushes this logic furthest, reconceiving the LLM not as a classifier to be benchmarked against human coders but as an "analytical collaborator" whose *disagreements* with a theoretically grounded framing codebook (Semetko and Valkenburg's four frames) become diagnostic: borderline cases and systematic divergences expose unstated theoretical assumptions (e.g., what counts as "moral" framing in a digital-privacy era) and prompt revision of the codebook itself rather than mere error correction. [[Farjam2026-zg]] operationalizes a similar philosophy into a concrete, replicable agentic architecture—Instructor, Classifier, and Judge agents plus a "Human Intermediary"—demonstrating that adjudication between multiple LLM passes, not model scale alone, drives reliability above conventional thresholds (Krippendorff's alpha > 0.7), while flagging that abstract constructs like "efficacy" resist reliable automation even after iterative refinement. Together these three papers trace an arc: from "LLM labels clusters that humans validate" ([[Ober2026-vd]]), to "LLM disagreement is itself theoretically generative" ([[Gomez-Zara2026-as]]), to "a structured multi-agent pipeline with human-in-the-loop checkpoints can be made practically replicable and exportable across languages" ([[Farjam2026-zg]]).

## Domain Adaptation and Classification at Scale

A second cluster addresses how LLMs can be adapted to specialized classification tasks where labeled data is scarce or categories are domain-specific. [[Meher2025-qb]] shows that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA on Llama 3.1) can push conflict-event classification far beyond zero-shot and BERT-era baselines, with dramatic gains concentrated in rare categories—while also democratizing access by running on consumer-grade hardware, a concern echoed in [[Farjam2026-zg]]'s emphasis on local, privacy-preserving deployment. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] addresses a cognate but distinct problem: rather than classifying discrete categories, it uses LLM-paired comparisons to scale open-ended survey text into continuous latent measures, positioning this as an advance over both closed-ended survey design and earlier, cruder LLM-coding approaches. [[Tan2024-vl]] sits above both as a survey, organizing the broader landscape of LLM-based annotation and synthesis into generation, assessment, and downstream-utilization stages—offering a conceptual scaffold against which the more applied papers in this set (Meher, DiGiuseppe, Farjam, Ober) can be read as instances of "generation" and "utilization" strategies, while flagging quality assurance and ethics as the field's persistent open problems.

## Validity, Bias, and the Limits of Substitution

Running beneath the methodological optimism is a sustained empirical interrogation of whether LLM annotations can be trusted as substitutes for human judgment, and under what conditions. [[Brown2025-jk]] directly tests the demographic-bias worry that motivates much human-in-the-loop caution, finding that disagreement with annotator subgroups is small, dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and vastly outweighed by item difficulty (label entropy)—a finding that complicates simple narratives of LLMs "favoring" particular demographics and instead redirects attention to task ambiguity itself, a theme that resonates with the borderline-case analysis in [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] and the hard-to-code constructs in [[Farjam2026-zg]]. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] extends this skepticism to the pipeline level: when LLMs are chained across multiple stages (classification, embedding, cluster labeling), existing validation protocols borrowed from single-model NLP no longer suffice, because LLM-generated outputs vary in granularity and because crowdsourced human benchmarks are no longer adequate comparators once LLMs exceed low-skilled annotators—necessitating expert, task-specific validation at every stage. This concern about appropriately calibrated, expert human oversight threads through nearly every paper in this topic, from Ober's and Farjam's codebook refinement loops to Marino's multi-phase validation protocol.

## Toward Higher-Stakes Automation: Peer Review and Reproducibility

A final arc pushes these methodological questions into the highest-stakes domain of scholarly workflows: evaluating and verifying research itself. [[King2026-av]] reports the striking claim that AI-generated reviews now exceed even unusually high-quality human expert reviews "by every measure," forcing a policy conversation about what role AI should play in peer review going forward. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] operationalizes a more cautious, infrastructural answer to that question: an agentic, compute-orchestrated review system (PAT) that substantially improves error detection over zero-shot LLMs and has been piloted at scale across real conferences, while explicitly theorizing a four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review—from author tool to full automation—that offers a useful ladder for situating all the other papers in this collection (most of which occupy the "supporting reviewer/analyst" rung rather than "full automation"). [[Alizadeh2026-es]] extends verification concerns from review to reproduction, benchmarking coding agents' ability to reproduce published computational findings and uncovering a double-edged result: high raw reproduction accuracy coexists with susceptibility to sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting and with context-dependent biases when given original paper PDFs—cautioning that even highly capable agents are not yet reliable *independent* validators, a conclusion that echoes Marino's insistence on task-specific, adversarially aware validation design and reinforces the collection's overarching insistence that human-in-the-loop oversight is not a transitional inconvenience but a structural requirement of trustworthy LLM-augmented scholarship.
