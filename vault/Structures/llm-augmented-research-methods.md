# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The shifting role of LLMs in scholarly workflows

The papers collected here trace an arc from enthusiastic methodological uptake to increasingly sober, structured accounts of what LLMs can and cannot responsibly do within the research process. At one pole sits [[Balluff2026-if]], a deliberately skeptical intervention arguing that communication scholars have adopted LLMs "unreflectively," ignoring reproducibility, environmental, and bias costs, and urging a trade-off mindset that favors smaller, open, task-appropriate models. This critique functions as a frame-setting counterweight to the rest of the set: nearly every other paper here can be read as an attempt to operationalize exactly the kind of "critical reflexivity" Balluff and colleagues call for, by building in evaluation, human oversight, or task-appropriate scoping rather than treating LLMs as generic black-box substitutes for human labor.

## Human-in-the-loop qualitative and content analysis

A cluster of papers treats LLMs as *collaborators* in interpretive tasks rather than replacements for human coders. [[Ober2026-vd]] integrates topic modeling with LLM-assisted labeling to analyze teacher interview transcripts, explicitly designing topic modeling as a transparent, mathematically grounded layer that keeps LLM contributions auditable, with humans iteratively refining the resulting codebook. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] pushes this logic further in deductive content analysis of news framing, reconceiving the LLM not as a classifier but as an "analytical collaborator" whose disagreements with human coders expose latent theoretical assumptions (e.g., what "morality" framing even means in a digital-privacy era) — a genuinely dialectical use of divergence as data rather than error. Together these two papers suggest a shared methodological commitment: friction between LLM output and human judgment is productive, revealing codebook ambiguity and theoretical underspecification that purely human coding might paper over. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extends the human-in-the-loop sensibility to survey methodology, proposing LLM-paired comparisons to scale open-ended response coding into continuous latent measures, addressing the classic tension between the richness of open text and the labor cost of manual coding.

## Bias, fairness, and the limits of demographic proxying

A second strand interrogates whether LLMs reproduce or distort the diversity of human judgment they are meant to approximate. [[Brown2025-jk]] directly tests this on four contentious annotation datasets, finding that demographic "bias" in LLM-human agreement is small, dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by item difficulty (label entropy) — a finding that complicates blanket claims (including those voiced in [[Balluff2026-if]]) about systematic demographic skew in LLM outputs. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the broader landscape of LLM-based annotation and synthesis, organizing the field around generation, assessment, and utilization of LLM labels, and flagging quality assurance and ethics as recurring open problems — a useful scaffolding against which the more empirical papers in this set can be situated. [[Matias2025-px]] reframes the fairness question at a higher level of abstraction, arguing that genuine scientific rigor in AI evaluation requires structural public involvement (not just demographic balancing of model outputs), since AI systems are sociotechnical artifacts whose failures (e.g., biased child-welfare risk scores, miscoded police complaints) are often invisible to technical metrics alone. Read together, these three papers push back against both uncritical trust in LLM objectivity and reflexive alarm about bias, insisting on empirical, task-specific, and participatory grounding.

## Domain-specific fine-tuning and classification

Where the previous cluster asks whether general-purpose LLMs are fair enough to use, [[Meher2025-qb]] and [[Arminio2025-tw]] ask whether they are powerful enough, and show how targeted adaptation can close the gap. ConflLlama ([[Meher2025-qb]]) fine-tunes an open-source Llama model via QLoRA for conflict-event classification, demonstrating dramatic gains over zero-shot baselines (especially for rare categories) while keeping compute accessible on consumer hardware — a direct answer to Balluff et al.'s concern about resource-intensive, dependency-creating LLM use, since it favors open weights and modest infrastructure. [[Arminio2025-tw]] takes a parallel approach in the visual domain, using vision-language models to generate connotative textual descriptions of images for clustering, improving semantic/interpretive quality over CNN pipelines while remaining interpretable via TF-IDF summaries, and showing the approach generalizes to open-source VLLMs (LLaVA) as well as proprietary ones. Both papers exemplify a "fit-for-purpose" philosophy: rather than treating LLMs as all-purpose oracles, they adapt specific architectures to specific classification or clustering problems and rigorously benchmark the gains.

## Scaling and reproducing the research pipeline itself

A further set of papers turns LLMs toward infrastructural tasks in the research pipeline rather than toward the texts under study. [[Fan2026-af]] is a partial outlier methodologically (it centers sequence-mining and language-embedding approaches to digital trace data rather than LLM-based coding per se), but it shares the collection's broader concern with matching computational method to the structure of the data and explicitly flags language-based models as a promising but data-hungry future direction for communication research. More central to the "research workflow" framing are [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]], which turn LLM-based agents on the processes of reproduction and review themselves. Alizadeh et al.'s SocSci-Repro-Bench shows that frontier coding agents can reproduce a striking share of published social-science findings, while also documenting failure modes—sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting, and bias introduced by giving agents the original paper—that directly echo the human-in-the-loop caution found in the qualitative-coding papers: more context or more "helpfulness" is not unambiguously better. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] extends this logic to peer review, presenting Google's Paper Assistant Tool as an agentic, inference-scaled verification system piloted at STOC and ICML, and proposing a taxonomy of AI roles in review (from author tool to full automation) that usefully generalizes the human-in-the-loop spectrum visible across the whole collection—from LLM-as-collaborator in coding to LLM-as-verifier in review.

## Human-facing deployments: chatbots as research subject and research tool

Finally, [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Dubey2026-bl]] shift the lens from LLMs-as-analysis-tools to LLM-powered chatbots as interventions whose effects on human cognition are themselves the object of study — misinformation inoculation and balanced-news exposure, respectively. Both report that conversational, adaptive interaction outperforms or interacts in counterintuitive ways with static content (e.g., conspiracy-believing users trusting the chatbot *more*, not less), reinforcing a theme that runs throughout the topic: the value of LLM-mediated tools lies less in their raw generative capacity than in the interactional and evaluative design choices—adaptivity, trust-building, interpretability, prompt framing—that determine whether human judgment is genuinely augmented or merely automated.
