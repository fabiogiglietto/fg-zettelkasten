# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The Shape of the Field

Across these eight papers a common arc emerges: LLMs are increasingly recruited not merely as writing tools but as research infrastructure — coding qualitative data, classifying domain-specific events, annotating contested constructs, scaling survey analysis, and even reviewing and reproducing scientific papers. The methodological question uniting them is not *whether* LLMs can do these things but *under what conditions of human oversight, evaluation, and epistemic transparency* their outputs can be trusted as scholarly evidence. Read together, they trace a progression from cautious, human-anchored augmentation of small-scale qualitative work toward increasingly autonomous agentic systems operating on the scientific process itself — with a persistent undertow of skepticism about what gets lost along the way.

## Human-in-the-Loop Coding and Measurement

At the more interpretive end of the spectrum, [[Ober2026-vd]] offers a concrete template for keeping humans central while letting LLMs do scalable labor: topic modeling supplies a transparent, mathematically grounded clustering of interview transcripts, and GPT-4-based models are then prompted to label and describe those clusters, with humans iteratively refining the resulting codebook. The paper's emphasis on stable cross-model label agreement and the absence of hallucination is less a triumphant claim about LLM capability than a validation exercise for a workflow explicitly designed so that AI augments rather than substitutes for interpretive judgment. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] pursues a structurally similar goal — unlocking the depth of open-ended text without the labor costs of manual coding — but through a different technical route, pairing LLM judgments with paired-comparison methods to construct continuous latent scales from open-ended survey responses. Both papers share the diagnosis that closed-ended or purely manual approaches to text-as-data are too limited, and both position LLMs as a way to recover richness at scale; the difference lies in whether the deliverable is a qualitative codebook or a quantitative latent measure.

## Domain-Specific Classification via Fine-Tuning

Where the previous cluster treats LLMs as generalist assistants, [[Meher2025-qb]] demonstrates the payoff of domain adaptation. ConflLlama fine-tunes Llama 3.1 with QLoRA to classify terrorism event types from the Global Terrorism Database, showing dramatic gains over zero-shot prompting — especially for rare categories — while running on consumer-grade hardware. This paper implicitly answers a question raised elsewhere in the topic: rather than relying on prompt engineering alone, targeted, resource-efficient fine-tuning can outperform both zero-shot LLMs and prior BERT-based domain classifiers (e.g., ConfliBERT), and it does so with documented prompt robustness that suggests genuine learned representations rather than surface pattern-matching. It is the topic's clearest empirical case for treating LLMs as *trainable instruments* tailored to a research domain rather than as fixed black boxes to be prompted.

## Annotation, Bias, and the Limits of Substitution

A second empirical strand interrogates whether LLMs can safely stand in for human annotators on subjective tasks. [[Brown2025-jk]] systematically tests demographic bias in LLM annotation across four contentious datasets and three models, concluding that biases are small, dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by item difficulty (annotator disagreement) as a predictor of LLM-human agreement. This is a notably reassuring, methodologically careful counterpoint to fears of systematic majority-group bias, though it also implies that swapping models or prompts is not a reliable bias-mitigation strategy. [[Tan2024-vl]] situates this kind of work within a broader emerging subfield, surveying methods for LLM-based data annotation and synthesis and the learning strategies built atop them, while flagging quality assurance and ethical risk as recurring, unresolved concerns — a framing that anticipates the more skeptical papers in this topic.

## A Critical Counter-Current

That skepticism is made explicit in [[Balluff2026-if]], which directly challenges the unreflective adoption implicit in much of the above. Surveying LLM use in political communication research across text analysis, synthetic data generation, and simulation, the authors argue that convenience obscures real costs: reproducibility failures from opaque corporate model updates, demographic and linguistic bias in synthetic data, environmental and infrastructural costs, and ethical uncertainty around LLM-driven field experiments. This paper functions as a methodological check on the optimism elsewhere in the topic — it does not reject papers like [[Meher2025-qb]] or [[Brown2025-jk]] so much as insist that their gains be weighed against a "least resource-intensive method sufficient to the task" principle, and that open-weight, domain-specific models be preferred over proprietary ones precisely because of the fine-tuning logic that [[Meher2025-qb]] demonstrates works well in practice.

## Scaling Up: Agentic Systems for Review and Reproduction

The final pair of papers extends the topic from *data annotation* to *the scientific process itself*, raising the stakes on human-in-the-loop design. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] describes Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an agentic system for peer review of math/CS manuscripts that substantially outperforms zero-shot prompting on error detection and has been piloted at scale across thousands of real conference submissions; its four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review (from author tool to full automation) offers a useful conceptual scaffold for locating every other paper in this topic along a spectrum of human oversight. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] pushes further still, benchmarking whether frontier coding agents can autonomously reproduce published social-science findings — finding high accuracy for Claude Code but also documenting concerning failure modes, including sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting and biased judgments when agents are shown original paper PDFs. Both papers show agentic systems achieving impressive quantitative benchmarks while surfacing exactly the risks [[Balluff2026-if]] warns about in the abstract: fragile grounding, susceptibility to framing effects, and the difficulty of auditing systems whose "reasoning" is not fully transparent even when their outputs look correct.

## Synthesis

Taken together, these papers sketch a maturing methodology rather than a settled one. The strongest results come where tasks are well-scoped and human oversight is structurally embedded — codebook refinement in [[Ober2026-vd]], fine-tuning validated against ground truth in [[Meher2025-qb]], bias auditing in [[Brown2025-jk]] — while the more ambitious agentic applications to peer review and reproduction ([[Jayaram2026-wd]], [[Alizadeh2026-es]]) show both the promise and the fragility of extending LLM augmentation from discrete annotation tasks to the open-ended judgment work of science itself. [[Tan2024-vl]] and [[Balluff2026-if]] bookend this arc as, respectively, an optimistic taxonomy of what is becoming possible and a sober reminder of what is being traded away — reproducibility, transparency, environmental cost, and epistemic autonomy — each time a research workflow outsources judgment to a model that is faster than a human but not yet as accountable.
