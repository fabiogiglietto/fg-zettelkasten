# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## Two paradigms for automating classification

A first fault line running through this literature separates domain-specific *fine-tuning* from general-purpose *prompting*. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes a DeBERTa model to classify collective-action frames in over 500,000 Proud Boys Telegram messages, treating the classifier as an instrument for a downstream causal question (does online framing Granger-cause offline violence?) rather than as the object of methodological interest itself. [[Meher2025-qb]] pursues the same fine-tuning logic more explicitly as method: ConflLlama shows that parameter-efficient QLoRA adaptation of an open Llama model can push terrorism-event classification far beyond zero-shot performance, especially on rare categories, while running on consumer-grade hardware—an argument for computational accessibility that implicitly answers the resource concerns raised by [[Balluff2026-if]]. Against this fine-tuning route, [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes a strikingly lightweight alternative for positioning political texts: simply ask an instruction-tuned LLM to place a text on a dimension and average many responses. The two approaches trade off differently—fine-tuning buys domain precision and reproducibility at the cost of training infrastructure, while ask-and-average trades some precision for near-zero setup cost and cross-lingual generalizability. [[Balluff2026-if]] frames this trade-off explicitly as a methodological choice problem, arguing that communication researchers have too often defaulted to large commercial LLMs when smaller, open, task-appropriate models (of exactly the kind [[Bailard2024-pj]] and [[Meher2025-qb]] deploy) would serve as well or better.

## From codebooks to clusters: LLMs in qualitative and interpretive analysis

A second cluster concerns interpretive rather than classificatory tasks. [[Ober2026-vd]] develops a human-in-the-loop pipeline that pairs topic modeling with LLM-assisted labeling to refine a human-derived codebook for interview transcripts, explicitly defending topic modeling's mathematical transparency as a check against LLM opacity—directly echoing [[Balluff2026-if]]'s worry about the "black box" character of prompt-based analysis. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends this logic to the visual domain: rather than clustering images by denotative content (as CNN pipelines do), a VLLM-to-text-to-embedding pipeline captures *connotative* meaning and, crucially, remains human-interpretable via TF-IDF summaries of the intermediate text. Both papers converge on the same design principle—LLMs should be inserted at a stage where their output remains legible and auditable to the human analyst, rather than substituting wholesale for coding or clustering judgment. [[Matias2025-px]] generalizes this human-in-the-loop commitment beyond expert analysts to lived-experience participants, arguing that participatory involvement is not merely an ethical add-on but improves the *scientific* rigor of AI evaluation itself—an argument that reframes the "human-in-the-loop" ideal shared by [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Arminio2025-tw]] as a matter of epistemic quality rather than convenience.

## Interrogating the annotator: bias, pragmatics, and the limits of judgment

A third strand asks how much we should trust LLMs as annotators or interpreters in the first place. [[Brown2025-jk]] finds that demographic bias in LLM annotation is real but small and dataset-specific, dwarfed by item difficulty (human label entropy)—a nuanced corrective to blanket claims of systematic LLM bias, and a useful counterweight to [[Balluff2026-if]]'s warnings about demographic skew in synthetic data. [[Paci2025-ag]] probes a different axis of competence: interpreting implicature and presupposition in Italian political speech, where even the best model falls well short of expert human interpretation and chain-of-thought prompting offers only modest gains. Read together, [[Brown2025-jk]] and [[Paci2025-ag]] suggest that LLMs are more reliable at surface-level subjective judgment tasks (toxicity, politeness) than at the kind of contextually loaded pragmatic inference political discourse analysis often requires. [[Tan2024-vl]] supplies the organizing survey for this whole strand, mapping the broader literature on LLM-based annotation and synthesis into generation, assessment, and downstream-utilization stages—a scaffold against which the empirical findings of [[Brown2025-jk]], [[Bailard2024-pj]], and [[Meher2025-qb]] can be located as instances of "assessment" and "utilization" respectively.

## Capability tensions: reasoning, persuasion, and their limits

[[Costello2024-bg]] complicates any simple narrative of LLM pragmatic weakness: durable, personalized AI dialogues meaningfully reduce conspiracy beliefs, suggesting LLMs can marshal detailed, evidence-responsive argumentation with real persuasive force. This sits in productive tension with [[Paci2025-ag]]'s finding that the same class of models struggles with implicit meaning—together the two papers suggest LLM reasoning is uneven: strong at explicit, evidence-based counterargument, weaker at inferring what political speakers *mean but do not say*. Both papers, despite their different tasks, imply that model capability should be assessed task-by-task rather than assumed from general benchmarks, a caution [[Balluff2026-if]] raises at the level of research design.

## Scaling verification: peer review and reproducibility as emergent workflows

A final pair extends LLM-augmented workflows to the review process itself. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] describes Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an agentic system for deep review of math/CS manuscripts that substantially outperforms zero-shot prompting on error detection and has been piloted at scale in real conferences, while explicitly theorizing a taxonomy of increasing AI autonomy in peer review. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] asks a complementary question—can AI coding agents *reproduce* published social-science findings?—and finds strong performance from frontier agents, but also documents failure modes structurally analogous to those [[Jayaram2026-wd]] flags for review (susceptibility to biased priors when given contextual metadata, and "sycophantic" specification search under confirmatory prompting, echoing [[Jayaram2026-wd]]'s grounding/hallucination concerns). Together they mark a shift in this topic's arc: where earlier papers ask whether LLMs can help *produce* scholarly analysis, these ask whether LLMs can *audit* it—raising the same human-in-the-loop and validity questions, now applied reflexively to the scholarly process itself, and closing the loop back to [[Balluff2026-if]] and [[Matias2025-px]]'s shared insistence that methodological convenience must be weighed against reproducibility, transparency, and epistemic accountability.
