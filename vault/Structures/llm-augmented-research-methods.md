# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The arc: from automation to augmentation

The papers filed here trace a shared methodological question—how should LLMs be inserted into the scholarly workflow, and what epistemic warrant does that insertion carry? Read together, they move from surveying what has already changed in empirical practice ([[Yang2026-tq]]), through a cluster of methods papers that operationalize "human-in-the-loop" LLM use for coding and annotation, to domain-specific fine-tuning and agentic systems that push toward higher degrees of automation in peer review and replication, and finally to critical and participatory counter-arguments that question whether the field's growing reliance on LLMs is being adopted too uncritically. The throughline is not whether LLMs work, but under what conditions of oversight, transparency, and task difficulty they can be trusted to stand in for human judgment.

[[Yang2026-tq]] is a useful entry point precisely because it is both an application and a diagnosis: its own systematic review of social-media-based social science relies on a human-in-the-loop LLM annotation pipeline to classify tens of thousands of papers, while its substantive finding—that platform API closures have begun to reshape what empirical social science can even study—frames the broader stakes of this topic cluster. If data infrastructures determine what can be known, then the tools used to process that data (LLMs, fine-tuned classifiers, agentic coders) are not neutral instruments but co-constitutive of the resulting knowledge.

## Human-in-the-loop coding and annotation

A substantial subset of the papers treats LLMs as collaborators in qualitative and content-analytic coding rather than autonomous labelers. [[Ober2026-vd]] combines topic modeling with LLM-assisted label generation to refine a human-derived codebook for interview data, explicitly arguing that topic modeling's mathematical transparency should anchor LLM outputs that are otherwise opaque; the authors find high semantic (though not identical) agreement between two LLMs and report no clear hallucinations under human review, concluding that oversight is what preserves interpretive depth. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] pushes this logic further by reframing the LLM's role entirely: rather than an automated classifier to be validated against ground truth, the LLM is treated as an "analytical collaborator" whose disagreements with a deductive framing codebook expose latent theoretical assumptions (e.g., what counts as "moral" framing in a digital context) that the codebook itself had left implicit. Divergence, in this framing, is data about the theory, not simply annotator noise.

[[Farjam2026-zg]] offers the most operational version of this paradigm: a replicable, locally-run agentic pipeline (Instructor/Classifier/Judge/Human Intermediary) for content analysis, validated against a published study and extended multilingually. Its findings—that a second "Judge" pass pushes most constructs above acceptable reliability thresholds, that abstract constructs like "efficacy" remain stubbornly hard to code even after iterative refinement, and that local deployment protects data privacy—speak directly to the practical design choices that [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] treat more conceptually. Together these three papers suggest a convergent best practice: structured, iterative, multi-pass workflows with explicit human checkpoints, rather than single-shot prompting.

[[Brown2025-jk]] complicates the fairness case for this kind of annotation work by showing that LLM-human disagreement is driven far more by item difficulty (inter-annotator entropy) than by annotator demographics, and that where demographic bias does appear it is dataset-specific rather than a stable property of any one model. This reframes the "bias audit" question that papers like [[Balluff2026-if]] raise more polemically: demographic bias is real but secondary to the harder problem of inherently ambiguous items, a point with direct implications for how codebooks like Gomez-Zara's should be revised. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extends the annotation agenda into survey methodology, proposing LLM-based paired comparisons to scale open-ended responses into continuous latent measures—addressing the long-standing tradeoff between the depth of open-ended data and the labor cost of coding it. [[Tan2024-vl]] situates all of this within the wider NLP literature, offering a survey-level taxonomy of LLM-based annotation and synthesis methods that functions as a reference map for the more applied papers in this cluster.

## Domain-specific classification and fine-tuning

Where the coding papers emphasize prompting and oversight of general-purpose LLMs, [[Meher2025-qb]] demonstrates an alternative path: parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) of an open-weight model for a narrowly defined classification task—terrorism event coding from the Global Terrorism Database. ConflLlama's gains over zero-shot baselines, especially for rare event categories, and its prompt robustness after fine-tuning, suggest that for well-defined, high-volume classification tasks, domain adaptation of smaller open models may be a more defensible and more reproducible strategy than reliance on proprietary general-purpose LLMs—an argument that resonates with [[Balluff2026-if]]'s broader critique of reflexive adoption of ever-larger commercial models.

## Automating evaluation: peer review and reproduction

A second cluster pushes the human-in-the-loop question toward the evaluation of scholarship itself. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] documents Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an agentic system for deep review and error-checking of math/CS manuscripts, piloted at scale (STOC, ICML) and evaluated both on a proof-error benchmark and via author surveys; its proposed four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review (from author tool to total automation) gives this topic cluster a shared vocabulary for locating how "deep" automation goes in any given workflow. [[King2026-av]] makes the strongest claim in the set—that AI-generated reviews now exceed even unusually high-quality human expert reviews on every constructed measure—and uses this finding to open a policy debate about what journals should do with that capability, rather than treating it as settled. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] extends the evaluative frontier from review to replication, benchmarking coding agents' ability to reproduce published computational findings; its most consequential result is not the headline accuracy figures but the discovery that confirmatory prompting induces sycophantic specification search and that providing original PDFs biases agents against correctly flagging non-reproducible analyses—a sharp empirical demonstration of exactly the kind of validity threat that motivates the critical papers in this set.

## Critical counterpoints and participatory alternatives

The remaining two papers function as a check on the optimism implicit in much of the above. [[Balluff2026-if]] systematically catalogs the epistemic, environmental, and ethical costs of reflexive LLM adoption in communication research—reproducibility risks from opaque corporate updates, demographic bias in synthetic data, and the environmental footprint of large models—and argues for a trade-off mindset favoring smaller, open, task-appropriate tools, a position that directly validates [[Meher2025-qb]]'s fine-tuning strategy over black-box prompting. [[Matias2025-px]] approaches the problem from a different angle, arguing that public and lived-experience participation is not a political add-on but a scientific necessity for evaluating sociotechnical AI systems, with case studies showing that community involvement has surfaced evaluation flaws (biased risk scores, hidden complaint categories) invisible to purely technical audits. Read against the rest of the collection, this paper suggests that the "human-in-the-loop" ideal running through [[Ober2026-vd]], [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]], and [[Farjam2026-zg]] should not stop at the level of the individual researcher-analyst, but could be extended to include the broader publics whose data and lives these methods ultimately describe.
