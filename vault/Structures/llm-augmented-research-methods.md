# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The arc: from augmentation to competence to substitution

The papers filed here trace a shifting answer to a single question: what should the LLM be *doing* in the research workflow, and how much authority should it be given? At one end sits a cluster of methods papers that treat LLMs strictly as collaborators under tight human control; at the other end sit recent benchmarking and policy papers that report AI systems meeting or exceeding expert human performance on reproduction and review, forcing a reconsideration of where the human-in-the-loop boundary should actually sit.

## Qualitative coding as collaboration, not automation

[[Ober2026-vd]], [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]], and [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] converge on a shared methodological stance: LLMs are most useful in qualitative and semi-structured text analysis when positioned as interlocutors rather than classifiers. Ober2026-vd builds a pipeline that pairs topic modeling's mathematical transparency with LLM-generated topic labels, explicitly designing the workflow so that interpretive authority stays with the human coder while the LLM accelerates the move from raw transcripts to a refined codebook. Gomez-Zara2026-as pushes this logic further, arguing that the *value* of an LLM in deductive content analysis lies precisely in the disagreements it produces with human coders — divergences that expose implicit theoretical assumptions (e.g., what counts as "moral" framing) and prompt revision of the codebook itself, rather than being treated as errors to be minimized. This reframes LLM "inaccuracy" as a diagnostic tool for theory-building, a notably different evaluative orientation from accuracy-maximizing paradigms elsewhere in the set. DiGiuseppe2025-es extends the augmentation logic to survey methodology, proposing LLM-paired comparisons as a way to recover continuous latent measures from open-ended responses, addressing the long-standing tradeoff between the depth of open-ended data and the cost of manual coding. Together these three papers establish a throughline: scalability gains are only acceptable if interpretive and theoretical control is explicitly retained, and the papers differ mainly in how they operationalize that retention (iterative codebook versioning, deliberate rejection of LLM suggestions, or statistical latent-trait modeling).

## Domain-specific classification and the limits of generality

[[Meher2025-qb]] and [[Tan2024-vl]] shift the register from interpretive collaboration to classification performance. ConflLlama (Meher2025-qb) shows that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) of an open-source LLM can turn a general-purpose model into a domain specialist for conflict event coding, dramatically improving performance especially on rare categories, and doing so within consumer-grade compute budgets — a direct methodological rejoinder to the assumption that only frontier closed models are viable for scholarly annotation tasks. This paper's framing of LLM fine-tuning as a successor to both manual coding and BERT-style encoders (e.g., ConfliBERT) positions domain adaptation as the key lever for moving from merely plausible LLM outputs to reliable classification. Tan2024-vl provides the survey-level scaffolding for this entire sub-literature, organizing the broader question of LLM-generated annotation and synthesis around generation, assessment, and downstream utilization, and flagging quality assurance and ethical risk as recurring unresolved issues — concerns that the empirical papers in this set each confront in their own domain.

## Benchmarking competence: reproduction and review

The most consequential shift in the set comes from papers that move past augmentation to ask whether AI systems can independently perform core scholarly tasks at expert level. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] introduces SocSci-Repro-Bench and finds that frontier coding agents (particularly Claude Code) can reproduce the large majority of published social science findings — not through memorization, but apparently through genuine inference of research questions from code and data. Yet the paper is careful to document failure modes that matter for workflow design: PDF context improves accuracy but biases agents toward false-positive reproduction, and confirmatory prompting induces sycophantic specification search, effectively recreating the file-drawer and p-hacking pathologies of human research in machine form. This complicates any simple narrative of AI-as-neutral-verifier and reinforces, from a different angle, the human-in-the-loop caution voiced by the qualitative-coding papers above.

[[Jayaram2026-wd]] and [[King2026-av]] push this question into peer review itself. PAT (Jayaram2026-wd) demonstrates that an orchestrated, inference-scaled agentic pipeline substantially outperforms zero-shot frontier models at detecting mathematical and proof errors, and reports real deployment at STOC and ICML with high author satisfaction — while still surfacing hallucination and grounding failures that temper any claim of full reliability. The paper's four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review (author tool through total automation) offers a structural vocabulary for exactly the governance question this whole topic cluster is implicitly debating. King2026-av sharpens the stakes further, reporting that AI-generated reviews now exceed even unusually high-quality human expert reviews by every measure the authors constructed, and treating this not as a technical curiosity but as a policy problem for journals to confront directly — publication speed and reduced adversarial friction set against unspecified but "concerning downsides."

## The participatory counterpoint

[[Matias2025-px]] stands somewhat apart from the LLM-specific papers but supplies the normative frame that ties the collection together: AI evaluation, it argues, is improved not degraded by distributing epistemic authority outward — to lived-experience experts and affected communities — at specific evaluative junctures (equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation). Read against the rest of the topic, this paper offers a structural argument for *why* human-in-the-loop design matters scientifically, not just ethically: the case studies of missed bias in risk-assessment tools and hidden categories in complaint data are precisely the kind of validity failures that purely automated or purely expert-driven classification pipelines (as in Meher2025-qb or Alizadeh2026-es) are structurally prone to. Matias2025-px thus functions as a bridge between the methodological papers' instrumental human-in-the-loop designs and a more fundamental claim about what rigorous evaluation requires — suggesting that as LLM-based workflows scale, the question is not only whether models can match expert performance, but whose expertise and whose oversight should count as the relevant human loop in the first place.
