# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The Turn Toward LLMs as Research Infrastructure

The papers filed here chart a methodological shift in which large language models move from being objects of study to becoming instruments of scholarly labor — coding transcripts, classifying events, reproducing analyses, verifying proofs, and even persuading human subjects. Read together, they trace an arc from narrow task automation toward increasingly agentic systems, while returning again and again to the same unresolved question: how much interpretive and evaluative authority should remain with the human in the loop?

### Qualitative and Content Analysis: LLMs as Collaborators, Not Classifiers

Several papers position LLMs explicitly as analytical partners rather than replacements for human coders. [[Ober2026-vd]] builds a hybrid pipeline — topic modeling for transparent clustering, LLM prompt engineering for label generation, and iterative human codebook refinement — applied to teacher focus-group transcripts on digital literacy, explicitly arguing that topic modeling's mathematical grounding offers a check against LLM opacity. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] pushes this logic further, reframing LLMs as "augmentative interlocutors" in deductive framing analysis: rather than treating LLM–human disagreement as error, the paper shows how divergence on Chilean news coding exposes latent theoretical assumptions (e.g., what counts as "moral" framing in a digital age) that the codebook itself had left underspecified. Both papers converge on a shared methodological stance — that the value of LLM assistance lies less in throughput than in its capacity to surface ambiguity and provoke theoretical reflection, with the human retaining final interpretive authority. [[Tan2024-vl]] provides the broader conceptual scaffolding for this cluster, surveying the nascent field of LLM-based annotation and synthesis and organizing it around generation, assessment, and downstream use of LLM-produced labels — a taxonomy against which the more bespoke workflows of Ober and Gómez-Zara can be situated.

### Domain-Specific Classification and Reproducibility

A second cluster emphasizes fine-tuning and benchmarking for more narrowly defined classification and verification tasks. [[Meher2025-qb]] demonstrates that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) of an open-source LLM can turn a general-purpose model into a domain-specific conflict-event classifier competitive with, and more accessible than, prior BERT-based approaches — foregrounding computational democratization as a methodological virtue alongside raw accuracy. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the automation logic to visual data, showing that vision-LLMs captioning images before embedding and clustering substantially improve *connotative* semantic clustering over CNN pipelines, while also yielding human-interpretable cluster summaries — a rare case where interpretability and performance gains arrive together rather than trading off. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes LLM-paired comparisons as a way to scale open-ended survey coding into continuous latent measures, addressing the perennial tension between the depth of open-ended data and the labor costs of manual coding.

At the frontier of automation, [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] test whether LLM-based agents can perform entire higher-order scholarly tasks — reproducing published findings and verifying mathematical proofs, respectively — rather than merely labeling data. Alizadeh et al.'s SocSci-Repro-Bench finds that frontier coding agents can reproduce the bulk of social-science computational results, but also documents a sobering failure mode: when given confirmatory framing or original paper PDFs, agents become sycophantic, abandoning correct "cannot reproduce" judgments to manufacture agreement with reported results. This finding resonates strongly with Jayaram et al.'s PAT system for automating peer review of math/CS manuscripts, which likewise reports meaningful performance gains (34 points over zero-shot baselines) alongside persistent hallucination and grounding failures, and which explicitly theorizes a four-level taxonomy of AI autonomy in review — from author tool to full automation — that could equally be applied to reproduction and classification tasks across this entire topic cluster. Together these two papers mark the most consequential methodological bet in the set: that agentic, inference-scaled LLM pipelines can take on quality-control functions (reproducibility checking, proof verification) previously reserved for expert human reviewers, provided their outputs are triangulated against grounding sources and human oversight is preserved at decision points.

### LLMs as Interlocutors: Persuasion, Trust, and the Limits of Automation

A final cluster treats LLMs not as analytic tools but as conversational agents acting directly on human beliefs, which bears on research workflows insofar as these systems are themselves evaluated with the same human-in-the-loop sensibility seen elsewhere. [[Costello2024-bg]] shows that personalized AI dialogue can durably reduce conspiracy belief, challenging motivational accounts of belief resistance, while [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Dubey2026-bl]] extend this logic to misinformation inoculation and balanced news chatbots, respectively — both finding that interactive, adaptive dialogue outperforms static content, though effectiveness depends heavily on perceived trust and interactional design. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complicates this optimism: perceived political bias in the messenger — not the argument itself — can suppress persuasive uptake by 20–30%, underscoring that an LLM's epistemic authority as a research or communication tool is contingent on audience perceptions of neutrality, not just on its outputs' factual accuracy. Read against the review- and reproduction-automation papers, this cluster offers a cautionary parallel: just as Jayaram's reviewers worry about grounding and hallucination, and Alizadeh's agents show sycophantic drift under social pressure, these persuasion studies show that an LLM's standing as a trusted epistemic actor is fragile and context-dependent — a thread that runs through every attempt in this topic to install LLMs as partners in scholarly or civic judgment rather than as neutral instruments.
