# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## Automated Annotation and Classification: Scaling Human Judgment

A first cluster of papers treats LLMs as replacements or extensions for the labor-intensive coding tasks that have long bottlenecked communication and political science research. [[Tan2024-vl]] provides the broadest frame, surveying how LLMs like GPT-4 can generate and assess annotations, and arguing that the field needs a dedicated methodology for LLM-based labeling distinct from prior work on LLM architecture. [[Meher2025-qb]] operationalizes this promise for a hard, domain-specific problem: fine-tuning Llama 3.1 via QLoRA to classify terrorism event types in the Global Terrorism Database. Its contribution is as much infrastructural as empirical—showing that consumer-grade hardware can produce dramatic gains over zero-shot baselines, especially for rare categories, thereby democratizing access to domain adaptation for political scientists who lack large compute budgets. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] extends the classification question into the visual domain, finding that a general-purpose multimodal model (GPT-4o) outperforms specialized computer-vision pipelines (RetinaFace, FaceNet512) for face recognition and person counting in political campaign imagery—an inversion of the usual expectation that task-specific models beat generalist ones. [[Arminio2025-tw]] pushes visual analysis further by reframing the goal from denotative to *connotative* clustering: using vision-language models to generate textual descriptions of images before embedding and clustering them, the paper shows this captures culturally embedded meaning (and remains interpretable via keyword summaries) that CNN pipelines miss. Together these four papers trace an arc from general survey to increasingly specialized (text, then multimodal, then explicitly semiotic) applications, with a shared insight that LLMs' strength lies less in beating narrow benchmarks than in flexibly handling ambiguity that rigid pipelines cannot.

## Human-in-the-Loop Qualitative Analysis

A second strand asks how LLMs should be integrated into interpretive, codebook-driven analysis without eroding its epistemological integrity. [[Ober2026-vd]] proposes a hybrid workflow combining topic modeling, LLM prompt engineering, and human-derived codes for analyzing interview transcripts, arguing that topic modeling's mathematical transparency provides a check against the opacity of neural methods, while LLMs assist with labeling and codebook refinement under human oversight. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] makes a closely related but conceptually sharper move: rather than treating LLMs as automated classifiers to be benchmarked against human coders, it positions them as "analytical collaborators" whose *divergences* from human classification are diagnostically valuable, surfacing latent theoretical assumptions (e.g., what counts as a "moral" frame) that the codebook itself had left underspecified. Both papers converge on a claim that also runs through [[Ober2026-vd]]'s emphasis on hallucination-checking: the value of LLMs in qualitative work lies in provoking reflection and revision, not in replacing interpretive authority. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] approaches a parallel problem—scaling open-ended survey responses into continuous latent measures—by pairing LLMs with paired-comparison methods, addressing the classic tradeoff between the depth of open-ended data and the labor cost of coding it. Read together, these three papers suggest a maturing methodological consensus: LLMs are most defensible in scholarly workflows when embedded in iterative, auditable loops with humans, rather than deployed as one-shot oracles.

## Reviewing the Reviewers: LLMs in Peer Review and Reproducibility

Two papers turn the lens on the scientific process itself. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] describes Google's Paper Assistant Tool (PAT), an agentic system for deep review of mathematics and computer science manuscripts that segments papers, allocates inference compute, and grounds claims via search—reporting substantial gains over zero-shot models on proof-error detection and positive reception in pilot deployments at STOC and ICML. Its proposed four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review (from author tool to full automation) offers a shared vocabulary for a debate that recurs across this literature: how much epistemic authority should be ceded to automated systems embedded in scholarly gatekeeping. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] complements this by testing AI coding agents' ability to *reproduce* published social science findings, introducing SocSci-Repro-Bench across four disciplines. Its findings sharpen the human-in-the-loop question: agents perform impressively on execution and even on abstracting research questions, but their accuracy degrades in ways that matter for trust—paper PDFs improve reproduction but bias agents toward fabricating results on non-reproducible tasks, and confirmatory prompting induces sycophantic specification search. Both papers thus converge on a cautionary point echoed elsewhere in this topic: automation gains in review and reproduction are real but come bundled with new failure modes (hallucinated grounding, sycophancy) that require deliberate evaluation design rather than acceptance at face value.

## Reflexivity, Critique, and the Limits of Adoption

A more skeptical set of papers pushes back against uncritical enthusiasm for LLM-driven workflows. [[Balluff2026-if]] is the most direct intervention, cataloguing epistemic, environmental, infrastructural, and ethical trade-offs of LLM adoption in communication research—reproducibility risks from opaque corporate updates, bias in synthetic data, and the often-overlooked case for smaller, open, task-appropriate models. Its call for a "trade-off mindset" resonates with [[Freelon2018-ao]]'s earlier warning about the fragility of platform-dependent methods in the "post-API age," where reliance on any single infrastructural affordance (API access then, LLM providers now) leaves research vulnerable to unilateral corporate decisions. [[Matias2025-px]] broadens the critique from technical to social infrastructure, arguing that public and lived-experience involvement is not a political concession but a scientific improvement to AI evaluation, addressing failure modes—like the Allegheny Family Screening Tool's ranking bias—that purely technical validation missed. [[Allen2025-ot]] occupies adjacent territory methodologically: reviewing a browser-extension-based, LLM-powered approach to studying platform effects without corporate cooperation, it frames such "platform-independent" tools as a necessary adaptation to closing data access, while cautioning that no single study (or single platform) can settle questions about social media's political effects. Read as a group, these four papers insist that the "workflow" question is never purely technical: it is entangled with corporate power, infrastructural contingency, and whose expertise counts as evidence.

## LLMs as Research Instruments: Studying Belief, Persuasion, and Trust

A distinct but related cluster uses LLMs not to code or classify existing data but as experimental instruments for generating new evidence about belief change—raising human-in-the-loop and evaluation questions of their own, since the "coding" here is the design and interpretation of AI-mediated dialogue. [[Costello2024-bg]] established that personalized, evidence-based AI dialogue durably reduces conspiracy beliefs, challenging motivational accounts of belief resistance and setting a template that later work extends and complicates. [[Kotz2026-lk]] generalizes this to contested policy domains (climate, vaccination, inequality), finding effects concentrated among skeptics and moderated by trust in science, while showing that belief change does not automatically transfer to policy support. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] introduces a crucial boundary condition: perceived political bias in the model itself can suppress persuasive uptake by up to 28%, operating through increased argumentative pushback rather than disengagement—a finding that bears directly on how researchers should design and report AI-mediated interventions. [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Dubey2026-bl]] extend the instrument-design question into inoculation and trust research: the former shows that conversational, adaptive inoculation against misinformation can outperform static reading/writing formats when individual baseline susceptibility is accounted for, while the latter finds, counterintuitively, that users with stronger conspiracy beliefs trust "balanced" news chatbots more than skeptics do, complicating assumptions about who technological interventions can reach. Collectively, this cluster demonstrates that LLMs are becoming not just tools for processing scholarly data but a novel experimental apparatus in their own right—one whose evaluation (durability, generalization, perceived neutrality, differential trust) requires the same methodological scrutiny urged elsewhere in this collection.

## Methodological Frontiers: Trace Data, Access, and the Nature of the Medium

Rounding out the topic are papers that expand the toolkit and its self-understanding. [[Fan2026-af]] argues for a "temporal turn" in communication research, reviewing six computational approaches (from sequence analysis to language-based embedding models) for analyzing time-stamped digital trace data as evolving user-sequences rather than aggregated cross-sections—directly relevant to the workflow question of which computational method best preserves theoretically meaningful structure in large behavioral datasets. Finally, [[Manovich2026-ih]] steps back from application to ask what kind of medium generative AI actually is, identifying built-in "media cognition" and structural conservatism as properties that condition everything researchers might do with these tools. Read against the rest of the collection, Manovich's account offers a theoretical undercurrent to the more applied papers' empirical observations—for instance, the aesthetic and semantic biases documented in classification and clustering work ([[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]], [[Arminio2025-tw]]) or the demographic skew in synthetic data flagged by [[Balluff2026-if]] can be read as specific instances of the medium's general tendency toward familiarity and convention rather than as isolated technical bugs. Across the full set, the throughline is a discipline in the process of building not just new tools but new standards for evaluating them: human oversight, transparency about trade-offs, and attention to where the model's affordances—rather than the researcher's intentions—quietly shape the resulting science.
