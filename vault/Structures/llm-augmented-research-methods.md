# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The turn toward LLM-augmented scholarly workflows

The papers filed here trace an arc from cautious methodological proposal to increasingly confident, agentic deployment of LLMs across the research pipeline—qualitative coding, event classification, survey analysis, annotation, and now peer review and even reproduction of findings. Read together, they suggest a field working out, task by task, what "human-in-the-loop" should actually mean, and a countercurrent (Balluff2026-if) warning that the whole enterprise is scaling faster than its epistemic justification.

### Coding and codebooks: LLMs as collaborators, not classifiers

Three papers converge on qualitative content analysis and share a strikingly similar normative stance: LLMs should refine human interpretive frameworks rather than replace them. [[Ober2026-vd]] builds a pipeline that pairs topic modeling's mathematical transparency with LLM-generated topic labels, explicitly choosing topic modeling as an interpretable substrate precisely because it can constrain and audit LLM outputs; the result is not a fully automated classifier but a revised, more structured codebook for analyzing teacher discourse on communication and digital literacy. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] pushes this framing further, rejecting the classifier metaphor outright in favor of the LLM as "analytical collaborator" or "augmentative interlocutor" in deductive framing analysis: divergences between LLM and human codes become diagnostic of latent theoretical assumptions (e.g., what "morality" framing tacitly requires) rather than simple errors to be corrected away. Both papers treat borderline cases and label disagreement as generative rather than merely noisy—a methodological commitment that distinguishes this cluster from more accuracy-maximizing uses of LLMs elsewhere in the topic. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extends the same impulse to survey methodology, proposing LLM-paired comparisons to scale open-ended response coding into continuous latent measures, positioned explicitly as an improvement over both closed-ended instruments and cruder existing LLM coding approaches—though, notably, it remains a methods proposal without reported empirical validation, leaving it more programmatic than the codebook papers.

### Classification and annotation: performance, bias, and the annotator question

A second cluster asks a blunter question: can LLMs replace human annotators or coders in domain-specific classification, and if so, at what cost? [[Meher2025-qb]] offers the most affirmative answer, fine-tuning Llama 3.1 via QLoRA for conflict event classification and showing dramatic gains over zero-shot baselines, especially for rare event categories—explicitly framing efficient fine-tuning as a democratizing methodological roadmap for resource-constrained political scientists, and as a successor to both manual coding and BERT-era tools like ConfliBERT. [[Brown2025-jk]] complicates the bias story that often accompanies such optimism: rather than finding systematic demographic bias in LLM annotation, it finds that item difficulty (human label entropy) swamps annotator demographics as a predictor of LLM-human agreement, and that whatever bias exists is dataset-specific, not model-specific—a finding that pushes against blanket claims of LLM unreliability as an annotator. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys this entire annotation-and-synthesis literature, organizing it around generation, assessment, and downstream use of LLM labels, and functions here as a structural bridge between the fine-tuning optimism of Meher2025-qb and the bias-sensitivity of Brown2025-jk—both of which instantiate exactly the "challenges and ethical issues" the survey flags as underattended.

### Agentic escalation: from annotation to verification and reproduction

A more recent and more ambitious strand extends LLM involvement beyond labeling individual texts into evaluating entire papers and entire literatures. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] describes Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an orchestrated agentic system for deep scientific review that substantially outperforms zero-shot models on proof-error detection and has been piloted at scale in real conferences; its four-level taxonomy of AI roles in peer review (author tool through total automation) offers a framework that could retroactively organize much of this topic's other papers along a human-oversight gradient. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] pushes agentic capability even further upstream, asking whether coding agents can reproduce published social-science findings outright—reporting high accuracy for frontier agents while carefully documenting failure modes strikingly consonant with the human-in-the-loop concerns raised elsewhere: agents exhibit sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting and become biased validators when given original paper PDFs, echoing Gomez-Zara2026-as's insistence that human interpretive authority must be actively exercised rather than assumed. Both papers mark a qualitative shift from LLMs as tools that assist a step in analysis to LLMs as agents evaluating or reproducing entire scholarly artifacts, raising the stakes of human oversight design considerably.

### The critical counterweight

[[Balluff2026-if]] functions as this topic's methodological conscience, arguing that the rapid adoption on display across the other eight papers has generally outpaced reflection on trade-offs: reproducibility risks from opaque corporate model updates, environmental costs rarely factored into method choice, and the availability of smaller, task-appropriate alternatives (encoder-only models, SVMs) that perform comparably to LLMs for many classification tasks at a fraction of the cost. This critique lands with particular force against Meher2025-qb's framing of LLM fine-tuning as inherently more promising than BERT-style approaches, and against any paper treating LLM-based classification as a default rather than a considered choice. Read as a set, the topic thus traces not a single trajectory of increasing automation but a genuine methodological contest: between LLMs as interpretive collaborators that preserve theoretical authority (Ober2026-vd, Gomez-Zara2026-as), LLMs as high-performing but bias-scrutinized classifiers (Meher2025-qb, Brown2025-jk), LLMs as increasingly autonomous agents verifying and reproducing science itself (Jayaram2026-wd, Alizadeh2026-es), and a standing challenge to ask, in each case, whether the LLM was ever the right tool at all (Balluff2026-if).
