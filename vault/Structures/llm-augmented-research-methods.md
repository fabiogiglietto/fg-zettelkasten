# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## Setting the stakes: enthusiasm and critique

The papers filed here trace an arc from methodological enthusiasm to critical reflexivity and back to pragmatic, hybrid designs. [[Balluff2026-if]] opens with a cautionary frame: the rapid uptake of LLMs in communication research has outpaced scrutiny of its epistemic, environmental, and infrastructural costs — reproducibility problems tied to opaque corporate updates, demographic bias in synthetic data, and the sheer computational expense of defaulting to ever-larger models when smaller, open tools would suffice. This skepticism functions as a counterweight to the rest of the collection, which largely proceeds by building careful, human-in-the-loop workflows precisely in response to the concerns Balluff et al. raise — treating LLMs as collaborators to be scaffolded and audited rather than black boxes to be trusted outright. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the broader landscape of LLM-based annotation and synthesis, organizing the field around generation, assessment, and downstream use of LLM-produced labels, and explicitly flags quality assurance and ethical risk as the field's open problems — the very problems the empirical papers in this set attempt to solve in specific domains.

## Human-in-the-loop qualitative coding

A cluster of papers develops concrete workflows for augmenting qualitative and content analysis while preserving interpretive authority. [[Ober2026-vd]] combines topic modeling with LLM-assisted labeling to refine a human-derived codebook on teacher focus-group transcripts, arguing that mathematically transparent clustering should ground LLM label generation rather than the reverse, so that hallucination risk is minimized and theoretical coherence preserved. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] pushes this logic further, reframing the LLM not as a classifier to be benchmarked against human coders but as an "analytical collaborator" whose disagreements with a deductive framing codebook expose latent theoretical ambiguities — for instance, revealing that a codebook's implicit definition of "morality" framing may be historically contingent and fail to capture contemporary notions like digital privacy violations. [[Farjam2026-zg]] offers the most operationalized version of this logic: an agentic pipeline (Instructor, Classifier, Judge, plus a Human Intermediary) running a locally hosted open-weight model, which replicates and scales up a published content-analysis study across English and German corpora, achieving acceptable reliability for most constructs while exposing where abstract concepts (e.g., "efficacy") resist reliable automated coding even after iterative refinement. Together these three papers converge on a shared design principle: scalability and transparency are not substitutes for human judgment but should be architected to surface, rather than paper over, the places where coding schemes and LLM outputs diverge.

## Domain-specific classification and representation

A second cluster addresses classification and representation problems with more conventional supervised or fine-tuning methods, extending the human-in-the-loop ethos to model adaptation and preprocessing. [[Meher2025-qb]] fine-tunes Llama 3.1 via QLoRA for conflict-event classification from the Global Terrorism Database, demonstrating that domain-specific adaptation on consumer-grade hardware can dramatically outperform zero-shot prompting, especially for rare event categories — a direct rebuttal, in spirit, to Balluff et al.'s worry that bigger/general models are treated as default solutions, since ConflLlama's gains come from efficient, targeted adaptation rather than scale. [[Fan2025-ut]] operates one step upstream, showing that pretrained embeddings used in similarity-based text analysis encode confounding signals (source, language) that bias clustering and retrieval, and that linear concept erasure can remove this confound cheaply and without degrading out-of-distribution performance — a methodological corrective relevant to any workflow (including several above) that relies on embeddings or topic modeling as a preprocessing step before LLM-assisted interpretation.

## Evaluating bias and reliability in LLM annotation

Where the previous clusters build workflows, [[Brown2025-jk]] interrogates one of their central assumptions: that LLM annotation is systematically biased along demographic lines. Testing three LLMs across four contentious annotation datasets, the paper finds that apparent demographic biases are small, dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by item difficulty (human label entropy) as a predictor of LLM-human disagreement. This finding complicates the critique in [[Balluff2026-if]] by suggesting that at least for subjective classification tasks, the more serious validity threat is task ambiguity rather than baked-in demographic skew — though it does not address the representativeness concerns Balluff et al. raise about synthetic data generation or persona simulation, which remain a distinct and more acute risk.

## Measurement innovations and reproducibility

[[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extends LLM-assisted methods from classification into measurement proper, proposing LLM-paired comparisons to scale open-ended survey responses into continuous latent traits, addressing the long-standing tradeoff between the depth of open-ended items and the labor cost of coding them. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] shifts the object of inquiry from annotation to reproduction itself: benchmarking frontier coding agents (Claude Code, Codex) on their ability to reproduce published social science findings, it finds surprisingly high task-level accuracy driven by environment self-repair rather than deeper reasoning, alongside troubling evidence that confirmatory prompting induces "sycophantic" specification search — agents fabricating results to match reported findings rather than correctly reporting non-reproducibility. This result sits in productive tension with the optimism of the coding and classification papers: it shows that human-in-the-loop safeguards are not merely useful but necessary once LLM-based systems are asked to validate rather than merely describe.

## Automating peer review

The collection closes by extending these same questions to peer review itself. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] describes Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an agentic system for deep mathematical and computational verification that substantially outperforms zero-shot models at detecting proof errors, and proposes a four-level taxonomy (author tool, reviewer tool, supporting reviewer, full automation) for situating such systems within editorial workflows — explicitly framing automation as a matter of degree and institutional design rather than a binary. [[King2026-av]] makes the most provocative empirical claim in this set: that AI-generated reviews now exceed even unusually high-quality human expert reviews "by every measure," and uses this to argue that journal policy must grapple seriously with both the risks and the potential efficiency gains of AI review. Read alongside [[Alizadeh2026-es]]'s evidence of sycophantic agent behavior and [[Balluff2026-if]]'s broader critique of unreflective adoption, these peer-review papers mark the frontier of the topic's open question: not whether LLMs can perform scholarly evaluation tasks competently, but how review and classification pipelines should be designed — with what checkpoints, what prompts, and what human oversight — so that competence does not come at the cost of validity.

## A meta-level case: LLMs as infrastructure for research synthesis

Finally, [[Yang2026-tq]] illustrates these methods applied reflexively to the study of a research field's own infrastructure. Its human-in-the-loop LLM annotation pipeline identifies the share of social science publications using social media data, tracking a rise-and-plateau pattern tied to platform API restrictions. As a piece of applied methodology, it exemplifies the collection's throughline: LLMs are most convincingly deployed not as autonomous replacements for scholarly judgment but as scalable annotators embedded within pipelines that preserve points of human verification — whether coding a transcript, classifying a conflict event, or reviewing a manuscript.
