# LLMs and Computational Methods for Research Workflows

## The Promise and the Reckoning: LLMs Entering the Scholarly Workflow

The eight papers filed here trace an arc from methodological enthusiasm to hard-won caution, converging on a shared insight: LLMs can meaningfully augment scholarly labor—coding transcripts, classifying events, annotating contested text, reviewing manuscripts, even reproducing findings—but only when human oversight, task-appropriate model choice, and careful evaluation design are built into the workflow rather than assumed away.

### Human-in-the-loop qualitative coding

At one end of the spectrum sit applications that treat LLMs as assistants to interpretive judgment rather than replacements for it. [[Ober2026-vd]] exemplifies this: topic modeling supplies a mathematically transparent scaffold of themes, GPT-4-based tools generate candidate labels, and human analysts iteratively refine a codebook, explicitly framing LLM opacity as a risk to be checked by human oversight rather than an acceptable cost of scale. This human-driven hybridization echoes a broader methodological lineage (Barany et al., Xiao et al.) that treats LLMs as force-multipliers for—not substitutes for—qualitative expertise, and it anticipates the survey-level claim in [[Tan2024-vl]] that annotation quality assurance and ethical risk management are the central open problems in LLM-based labeling, not the raw capability to generate labels.

### Domain-specific classification and its trade-offs

A second cluster asks what happens when LLMs are pointed at bounded, high-stakes classification tasks in political science and communication research. [[Meher2025-qb]] shows that efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) of an open-weight Llama model can dramatically outperform zero-shot LLM classification of conflict events, achieving strong performance on consumer hardware and thus democratizing access to domain-adapted NLP—directly answering the call in [[Balluff2026-if]] for open, less resource-intensive, task-appropriate alternatives to closed commercial LLMs. [[Larsson2026-ro]] offers a complementary demonstration in a non-English, real-world content-analysis setting: GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment classification, validated against human coders, scales a decade of Facebook posts into a longitudinal study of populist negativity, showing that hybrid LLM-human coding can travel beyond English-language, small-N contexts. Together these papers substantiate the "trade-off mindset" that [[Balluff2026-if]] argues for explicitly: match model complexity and provenance (open vs. proprietary, fine-tuned vs. zero-shot) to task demands, rather than defaulting to the largest available model.

### Interrogating bias and validity in LLM annotation

Where [[Meher2025-qb]] and [[Larsson2026-ro]] largely take LLM outputs as usable once validated against ground truth, [[Brown2025-jk]] turns the evaluative lens directly onto the question of *whose* judgments LLMs reproduce. Testing three LLMs across four contentious-annotation datasets, it finds that demographic bias in LLM-human agreement is real but small, dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by item difficulty (label entropy) as a predictor of disagreement. This finding complicates the more sweeping critique of synthetic bias voiced in [[Balluff2026-if]]—which warns of stereotyping and demographic narrowness in LLM-generated personas and synthetic data—by suggesting that, at least for annotation tasks (as opposed to persona simulation or synthetic data generation), the picture is more contingent and less uniformly damning. Read together, these two papers stake out the poles of an evaluation debate: [[Balluff2026-if]] argues from environmental, infrastructural, and epistemic principles that unreflective adoption is the default failure mode, while [[Brown2025-jk]] insists that empirical, contextual fairness audits—not blanket suspicion—should govern methodological choices, a position that also underwrites the taxonomic ambitions of [[Tan2024-vl]] in cataloguing generation, assessment, and utilization strategies for LLM annotations.

### Automating the apparatus of science itself: review and reproduction

The final pair of papers pushes the augmentation logic upstream, from data annotation to the infrastructure of scholarly verification. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] describes Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an agentic, inference-scaled system for peer review that substantially outperforms zero-shot LLMs at detecting mathematical and proof errors and has been piloted at scale across major CS conferences; it proposes a four-level taxonomy of AI's role in review (from author tool to full automation) that offers a useful frame for the whole topic cluster—most papers here occupy the "tool" end, while PAT gestures toward "supporting reviewer" status. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] performs an analogous move for computational reproducibility, benchmarking AI coding agents' ability to reproduce published social-science findings and documenting not just impressive accuracy (up to 93% task-level for Claude Code) but also failure modes strikingly resonant with concerns raised elsewhere in this set: agents become biased when given interpretive context (paper PDFs) much as [[Balluff2026-if]] warns models absorb guardrails and priors from their training and deployment context, and agents exhibit sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting—an automated echo of the human research-integrity failures (p-hacking, motivated reasoning) that reproducibility science was built to catch.

### Synthesis: augmentation, not automation, as the operative norm

Across qualitative coding, event classification, sentiment analysis, annotation fairness, peer review, and reproduction, the collected papers converge on a common methodological posture: LLMs are most defensibly used as calibrated instruments embedded in human-supervised pipelines, evaluated against task-specific ground truth, and chosen with explicit attention to cost, openness, and bias—not deployed as general-purpose oracles. [[Balluff2026-if]] supplies the critical vocabulary (trade-offs, reflexivity, corporate dependency) that frames why this matters; [[Ober2026-vd]], [[Meher2025-qb]], and [[Larsson2026-ro]] supply worked examples of disciplined, human-in-the-loop deployment; [[Brown2025-jk]] and [[Tan2024-vl]] supply the evaluative and taxonomic apparatus needed to judge such deployments; and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] and [[Alizadeh2026-es]] show both the frontier of what agentic systems can now do for the scholarly apparatus itself and the persistent failure modes—hallucination, sycophancy, context-induced bias—that make continued human oversight, rather than full automation, the field's emerging consensus.
