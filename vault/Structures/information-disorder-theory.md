# Information Disorder & Hybrid Media Ecologies

## Redefining the Object: From "Fake News" to Information Disorder

The starting move in this literature is a refusal of the term that names it. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] argues that "fake news" is a rhetorical invention that smuggles in an untenable assumption—that intent at the point of creation determines the epistemic status of a piece of content as it circulates. Drawing on second-order cybernetics and Chadwick's hybrid media system, the paper relocates the analytical centre of gravity from injectors to propagators, proposing a micro–meso–macro model in which truthfulness judgments cascade and recombine as content moves through the system. This move—from a noun ("fake news") to a process (disinformation-as-circulation)—sets the conceptual agenda for nearly everything that follows in this corpus. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] show empirically what happens when the discredited term persists in public discourse anyway: both find that "fake news" functions in practice as a delegitimization weapon rather than a description of falsity, redrawing Hallin-style boundaries of consensus and deviance (Yoo) or serving as a tool of strategic negative campaigning selectively amplified by aligned media (Mosca). [[Frischlich2025-vn]] extends the definitional critique to the field's own metaphors, arguing that "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" analogies each illuminate only one level of what is actually a multilevel complex adaptive system, and that single-analogy thinking produces single-lever interventions doomed to backfire.

## The Narrative Turn

A second, closely related current pushes disinformation studies away from claim-level truth/falsity binaries toward narrative as the proper unit of analysis. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the strongest programmatic case: successful disinformation behaves like cross-platform folklore, with recognizable characters, catchphrases, and cathartic payoffs that satisfy identity needs independent of, and sometimes despite, factual scrutiny—an argument that directly explains why fact-checking and domain-based detection struggle to gain traction. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes this insight in the U.S. context, showing survey evidence that cumulative, cross-outlet exposure to a recurring narrative—not exposure to any single false claim—predicts belief. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the missing theoretical apparatus, criticizing the field's "naïve" use of "narrative" as self-evident and proposing a hermeneutic-realist, narratological toolkit (Ricoeur, Phelan) capable of showing how content can be referentially and ethically suspect without being straightforwardly false. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] extend the narrative/framing logic into empirical sensemaking: Starbird's evidence-frame framework demonstrates that election-integrity rumors are typically built from accurate evidence wrapped in escalating partisan frames rather than fabricated facts, while Rohrbach traces how journalistic reporting strategies can themselves seed the formation of new conspiracy beliefs. Together these papers reorient the field's causal story: the problem is less what is stated than how what is stated is storied, framed, and made to recur.

## Coordinating Behavior: Toward an Actor- and Action-Centred Paradigm

A parallel and eventually convergent line of work shifts attention from content to coordinated action. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] is foundational here, arguing that disinformation research should detect coordinated collective behavior rather than bad content or bad actors in isolation, and operationalizing this via coordinated link-sharing networks around Italian elections. [[Mannocci2026-kc]]'s survey consolidates the resulting decade of scholarship into a general theory: coordinated online behavior is defined by actors, synergic actions, and intent, and characterized along four orthogonal dimensions—authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, time-variance—deliberately broader than Meta's threat-focused "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" (CIB) framing. [[Thiele2025-ol]] pushes further into the "attribution problem," proposing a rational-choice model that infers a campaign's covert principal from observable scale, elaborateness, and disguise, and explicitly critiquing CIB as conflating inauthenticity with manipulative intent. These conceptual advances are tested against hard cases: [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] shows how Serbia's ruling party mimics authentic grassroots support in ways that evade detection frameworks built for centralized bot farms, while [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] uses OSINT methods on leaked CVs to reconstruct the Internet Research Agency not as a black-box troll farm but as an ordinary, labor-market-embedded media organization—complicating simple state-actor narratives. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] and [[Scalco2026-bd]] round out this cluster by examining, respectively, the temporal persistence and event-appropriation strategies of manipulation campaigns, and a quantitative method for detecting "information voids"—demand/supply imbalances that correlate with misinformation prevalence, offering a structural, ecosystem-level diagnostic rather than an actor-level one.

## Platform Power, Data Access, and the Politics of Studying Disinformation

Underpinning all empirical work on coordination and narrative is the question of who controls the evidence. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] independently argue that platforms' monopoly over the data needed to study their own products creates industry-influence risks structurally analogous to tobacco and pharma, with subtle effects operating through funding, framing, and selective access rather than crude interference. [[Munger2025-cz]] supplies a pointed case study, arguing that even the most ambitious academic-industry collaboration (Meta2020) produced narrow, temporally fragile estimates that cannot keep pace with platform change, concluding that proactive regulation—not reactive research—is the only viable governance mechanism. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] historicizes this crisis, tracing platform data access through "Wild West," "Golden Age," and post-Musk "Walled Garden" phases, and assessing the EU Digital Services Act's Article 40 as a fragile, unevenly implemented attempt to convert access from platform philanthropy into regulatory right. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] draws the conceptual consequence for the field: causal-effects research is no longer an adequate lens, and platform collective behaviour should instead be theorized as democratic infrastructure in its own right—a framing that dovetails with the coordination-behavior literature above.

## Fact-Checking, Moderation, and Their Discontents

A more applied cluster interrogates the institutions built to counter disinformation. [[Farkas2026-lr]] shows how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend their platform funding through apologia strategies of differentiation and transcendence, revealing an emergent and contested professional identity. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically dismantles Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors, finding that content removal after verification is rare and that fact-checkers themselves favor transparency and counter-speech over deletion—while expressing sharp skepticism toward Community Notes as a substitute. [[Ramos2026-qo]] argues from frontline practice (Maldita.es, covering the 2024 Valencia floods) that claim-level fact-checking cannot keep pace with evolving conspiratorial narratives, proposing narrative monitoring and argumentation-theory-based "argument-checking" as necessary complements—an applied echo of the narrative-turn papers above. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study synthesizes expert consensus across this entire intervention landscape, finding that platform- and journalism-level governance are rated more important than individual-level literacy or debunking, and that the field remains fragmented and insufficiently anticipatory (notably having failed to foresee generative AI's impact).

## Mapping the Wider Ecology: Polarization, Radicalization, and Global Equity

Because disinformation, radicalization, and polarization scholarship share diagnostic vocabulary and often the same data infrastructures, several papers in this collection map adjacent terrain to sharpen the topic's boundaries. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of online radicalization research parallels the hybrid-media argument directly, concluding that treating "online" and "offline" radicalization as separable domains is no longer tenable. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] performs an analogous audit for polarization scholarship, finding that depolarization recommendations are largely disconnected from the evidence scholars themselves produce and instead reflect implicit deliberative-democratic norms—a caution equally applicable to disinformation-intervention research. [[Esau2025-tf]] offers a normative counterpoint, proposing deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening as antidotes to "destructive" (rather than all) polarization. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] both press a geographic critique that recurs across this whole topic: research is disproportionately WEIRD- and U.S.-centric, and genuinely comparative, cross-platform, globally equitable approaches remain the exception rather than the rule.

## Toward Empirical Grounding: Elections, Trust, and Strategic Actors

The topic closes on papers that ground these conceptual and taxonomic advances in concrete political stakes, previewing the researcher's later empirical turn. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] shows across 26 countries that misinformation-sharing by political elites is not a generic populist or right-wing pathology but specifically a radical-right-populist strategy tied to institutional-legitimacy crises and clickbait media symbiosis. [[Rossini2026-mj]] demonstrates in the Brazilian case that mainstream news use bolsters electoral trust even among supporters of an illiberal incumbent, while belief in elite-driven electoral misinformation erodes it—reframing disinformation's effects through the lens of democratic resilience rather than individual deception. Read together with the coordination, narrative, and platform-governance strands above, these studies close the loop: disinformation is conceptualized not as an aberrant category of false content but as an emergent property of a hybrid, coordinated, and structurally under-observed media ecology—precisely the premise the researcher's subsequent empirical work on coordinated behaviour takes as its point of departure.
