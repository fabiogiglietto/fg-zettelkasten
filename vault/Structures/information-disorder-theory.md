# Information Disorder and Fake News Conceptualization

## From intent to circulation: the founding definitional quarrel

The topic's anchor text, [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], stakes out the position that would structure much subsequent work in this collection: the post-2016 obsession with classifying "fake news" by the *intent* of its creator is analytically dead-ended, because in a hybrid news system falsehood is made and remade at every step of propagation. This is less a rejection of taxonomy than a relocation of it — from the injector to the chain of propagators, from the genesis of a claim to its cybernetic life. [[Lazer2018-mm]] and [[Freelon2020-yp]] represent the more conventional definitional move this critiques: both insist that "fake news" be abandoned as an analytic term (too politically contaminated, too narrow) in favor of "disinformation," precisely defined by deception, harm, and intent — the EU High Level Expert Group formula. [[Bennett2018-ys]] pushes further, arguing that what looks like scattered incidents of fake news is really a systemic "disinformation order" rooted in declining institutional legitimacy, a thesis [[Benkler2018-lw]] operationalizes empirically by showing that the crisis is asymmetric — a radicalized right-wing media sphere insulated from truth-correcting norms — rather than a general, symmetric polarization. Together these pieces mark the field's shift from "fake news" as a folk category to "information disorder" as a structural diagnosis.

## Hybrid media systems as the structural scaffold

Underpinning nearly all the conceptual work here is Andrew Chadwick's hybrid media system framework, represented directly by [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]], and by the comparative-media-systems tradition of [[Hallin2004-kg]]. Chadwick's insistence that older and newer media logics interpenetrate rather than supersede one another gives [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] its institutional vocabulary and supplies [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] with its central move: disinformation should be studied as *narratives* recurring across the hybrid ecosystem rather than as discrete false claims, since cumulative cross-outlet exposure — not single-source falsity — is what drives belief. [[Hallin2004-kg]]'s three-model typology, meanwhile, becomes the comparative backbone for later cross-national work ([[Humprecht2020-gd]], [[Luhring2025-od]]), reminding the field that "misinformation" is not a context-free phenomenon but one refracted through distinct political-media configurations.

## Scale, psychology, and the limits of individual-level explanation

A parallel empirical strand interrogates how much "fake news" actually matters and why people fall for it. [[Allcott2017-yz]] and [[Allen2020-nj]] both puncture alarmism from the supply side — fake news was a tiny fraction of the 2016 media diet, concentrated in a narrow and already-partisan audience — while [[Vosoughi2018-at]] complicates the picture by showing that falsehood genuinely outpaces truth in depth, breadth, and speed of diffusion, driven by human novelty-seeking rather than bots. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] relocate the explanatory burden from motivated partisan reasoning to inattention, heuristic processing, and the cognitive stickiness of the "continued influence effect," while [[Tsfati2020-uo]] closes the loop by showing that mainstream media, in the act of debunking, are often the primary *amplifiers* of fake news to the mass public. [[Starbird2019-qv]] adds a crucial sociotechnical correction to all of this: disinformation campaigns are not things done *to* passive audiences by bots and trolls but collaborative work, co-produced by authentic participants whose organic engagement is inseparable from orchestrated effort — a point later formalized by [[Thiele2025-ol]]'s rational-choice model of attributing coordinated campaigns to covert principals, and surveyed comprehensively by [[Mannocci2026-kc]], who treats coordination itself as an orthogonal analytic dimension cutting across authentic/inauthentic and harmful/benign behavior.

## Narrative, rhetoric, and the weaponization of the label

A more recent cluster pushes conceptualization further still, away from true/false binaries toward narrative and discourse. [[Marwick2026-qd]] and [[Sadler2025-vu]] converge on the claim that disinformation functions as *story* — identity-affirming, cathartic, narratively coherent — such that fact-checking and domain-level moderation miss the mechanism of spread entirely; [[Ramos2026-qo]] operationalizes this insight for practice, proposing "argument-checking" and narrative monitoring as supplements to claim-level fact-checking during crisis events. Running alongside this narrative turn is a rhetorical-pragmatic one: [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] both show that "fake news" accusations are themselves strategic weapons of delegitimization wielded by partisan media and political actors, reshaping Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance rather than describing any actual falsehood. [[Gaber2022-bk]] extends this logic to "strategic lying" in the Brexit and 2019 UK election campaigns, arguing that deliberate falsehood is less a post-truth rupture than an intensification of political spin, turbocharged by social media's rebuttal dynamics. [[Guo2026-fi]]'s conceptual review of the "non-political," though not about misinformation per se, offers a methodological lesson that resonates throughout this cluster: contested boundary categories (political/non-political, true/false, real/fake) are best studied relationally, as sites of power, rather than as fixed ontological kinds.

## The technological frontier: synthetic media and emergent facts

Two papers push the conceptual apparatus toward generative AI. [[Emilio2026-ik]] argues that the real risk is not isolated deepfakes but "synthetic realities" — coherent, interactive, fabricated information environments spanning content, identity, interaction, and institutions — culminating in a "Generative AI Paradox" whereby ubiquitous synthetic media licenses wholesale discounting of digital evidence. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] approaches the same rupture epistemologically, showing that none of fact-checking's three traditional categories of fact (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) can accommodate GenAI outputs, and proposing "emergent facts" as a new category demanding relational rather than binary verification.

## De-Westernizing and comparative resilience

A recurring self-critique across the collection is that misinformation research has been parochially WEIRD. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around this corrective; [[Nenno2025-xa]] operationalizes it by showing that news-value signatures of flagged misinformation differ, if modestly, between WEIRD and non-WEIRD contexts, with the US and Brazil as outliers; [[Humprecht2020-gd]] builds a structural resilience framework showing the US to be a sui generis vulnerable case rather than a template; [[Fletcher2026-lv]] extends the lens historically, showing that global trust in news has been declining since the 1980s — predating the internet — but diverges sharply by press-freedom regime; and [[Hourigan2026-oc]] grounds the whole conversation in lived audience experience, finding that everyday perceived misinformation is textual, mainstream-sourced, and topically much broader than the "hot button" issues the literature fixates on. [[Luhring2025-od]]'s methodological audit of NewsGuard — showing how binary trustworthiness cutoffs can badly distort source-based findings — is the infrastructural counterpart to this comparative turn, a reminder that cross-national claims are only as good as the measurement instruments underneath them.

## Platforms, data, and the political economy of researching disorder

A substantial and increasingly self-reflexive thread concerns the conditions of possibility for studying any of this. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] trace the "post-API age" and the shift from data philanthropy to commercial paywalls, with the EU's DSA Article 40 offered as a fragile regulatory corrective; [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[Annabell2026-ev]] examine that provision from the inside, showing respectively the practical bottlenecks facing vetted researchers and how platforms strategically narrow the very definition of "data" to disadvantage qualitative and humanities-informed research. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] generalize the warning into an argument about structural industry capture of the evidence base itself, drawing explicit parallels to tobacco and pharma; [[Crosset2026-mq]] reframes content moderation regulation as a form of "security bureaucratisation" that governs circulation rather than content; and [[Bechmann2026-dr]] calls for reconceiving platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure, beyond the causal-effects paradigm that this whole data-access struggle has been organized around. [[Donovan2025-ws]] supplies the historical account that ties these governance debates together, narrating how "misinformation-at-scale" was socially constructed through platforms' own moderation choices between 2016 and 2021, and why those platforms have since retreated from the problem their business model creates.

## The field talking to itself

Finally, several papers function as meta-commentary on where the field should go next, and how its core concept relates to adjacent ones. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts finds near-consensus that no single intervention will work and that terminological debates over "misinformation" versus "conspiracy theory" may be a distraction from more urgent multi-level, multi-stakeholder work. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] makes a structurally similar point about analogy rather than definition, showing that "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" each illuminate one level of a multilevel complex system while obscuring the others. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] imports lessons from the more mature field of climate psychology, warning against overreliance on cognitive proxies and underscoring the risk that industry collaboration — the same concern raised by [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] — can distort misinformation research itself. Rounding out this reflexive turn, [[Rothut2026-wt]], [[van-Eck2026-xg]], [[Esau2025-tf]], and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] survey cognate literatures — online radicalization, depolarization, destructive polarization, and polarization research's own Western skew — each diagnosing the same pathology this topic keeps returning to: concepts (radicalization, polarization, misinformation, fake news) that circulate widely in both scholarship and public discourse while remaining under-defined, unevenly operationalized, and prone to normative assumptions smuggled in as empirical findings. Read together, the 49 papers trace an arc from a blunt post-2016 alarm about "fake news" toward a far more structural, comparative, and self-critical understanding of information disorder as an emergent property of hybrid media systems, platform political economies, and the research infrastructures used to study them.
