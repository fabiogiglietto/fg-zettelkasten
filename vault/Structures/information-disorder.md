# Information Disorder & Disinformation

# Information Disorder & Disinformation: Structure Note

## From discrete falsehoods to narrative and cultural systems

The most consequential arc running through this corpus is a shift away from treating disinformation as discrete false claims amenable to verification, toward treating it as *narrative* — culturally embedded, recurring, and often resistant to true/false adjudication altogether. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the foundational argument: disinformation succeeds as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic storytelling, not as isolated facts, which is why fact-checking and domain-level detection repeatedly fail to contain it. This is echoed and extended by [[Sadler2025-vu]], who develops a hermeneutic-realist, narratological apparatus for assessing content that is referentially and ethically troubling without being straightforwardly false, and by [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]], whose survey evidence shows that cumulative exposure to recurring narratives (not single claims) drives belief. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] push this further methodologically, developing an "evidence-frame" framework and a "deep storytelling" account of election rumors in which the *how* of telling — cueing, allusion, implicit frame activation — is as important as factual content; [[Prochaska2026-pr]] then formalizes this into a "grammar" of recurring Actor-Object-Action tropes spanning the 2020–2026 U.S. election cycles. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] anticipates this shift from a cybernetics angle, arguing analytic attention should move from creators' intent to propagators' judgments and cascades. Together these papers constitute a sustained critique of "infocentric" disinformation studies and argue for narrative-, frame-, and ritual-based alternatives — a critique sharpened by [[Frischlich2025-vn]], who shows that even our *meta*-analogies (virus, warfare, pollution) are each partial and can misdirect intervention design.

## Radicalization, identity, and the far right

A parallel strand examines disinformation's entanglement with identity formation and partisan asymmetry. [[Marwick2025-vx]] reframes Far-Right "redpilling" as a gradual socio-technical socialization process in which disinformation functions as self-validating "evidence," complicating simple exposure-equals-belief models. At the macro level, [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds across 26 countries that misinformation sharing by political elites is not explained by populism or right-wing ideology alone but by their *interaction* — radical-right populism specifically. This is consonant with [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]], foundational accounts arguing the post-2016 epistemic crisis is asymmetric, institutional, and rooted in a radicalized right-wing media ecosystem rather than symmetric polarization or technology per se. Case studies substantiate this: [[Copland2025-em]] traces Sky News Australia's transformation into a transnational right-wing propaganda node; [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual disinformation to TikTok's younger audience; [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate media visibility for Radical Right parties across the 2024 EU elections, independent of electoral performance; and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral success to shifts in voter disinformation concern. [[Yang2023-cg]] quantifies the asymmetry directly, finding image-based political misinformation on Facebook 5–8 times more prevalent on the right. Complicating any simple partisan story, [[Renault2025-uh]] shows Republicans are flagged 2.3x more than Democrats even under X's crowdsourced Community Notes system, while [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" demonstrates that *perceptions* of being targeted by misinformation are themselves partisan and identity-driven, independent of actual asymmetries.

## Elections, trust, and democratic erosion

A dense cluster examines disinformation's effects on electoral trust and intolerance, concentrated on Brazil, the U.S., and Europe. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use the same Brazilian panel infrastructure to show that belief in electoral misinformation increases political intolerance and decreases trust in results, with messaging apps (WhatsApp) operating as an indirect pathway — complemented by [[Ventura2025-sw]]'s deactivation experiment isolating WhatsApp's distinct causal role. In the U.S. context, [[Berlinski2023-rj]] shows unsubstantiated voter-fraud claims erode election confidence (concentrated among Republicans/Trump approvers) with fact-checks failing to mitigate harm, while [[Kim2026-wg]] documents geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising with measurable turnout effects — a methodological corrective to null findings from prior IRA studies. [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorizes "strategic lying" in Brexit/2019 UK election campaigning as an evolution of spin rather than a rupture, where even rebuttals amplify the original lie. [[Tai2026-qk]] examines institutional and ideological checks on elected officials' own misinformation sharing. Underpinning much of this work is [[Humprecht2020-gd]]'s comparative framework showing that societal resilience to disinformation is structurally conditioned (polarization, trust, media systems), with the U.S. a genuine outlier — a caution against over-generalizing from any single national context that [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue introduction makes explicit as a field-wide concern about WEIRD bias, further evidenced empirically by [[Nenno2025-xa]]'s 24-country study of news values in flagged misinformation.

## State and coordinated influence operations

Several papers examine disinformation as organized, state-adjacent production rather than organic circulation. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT analysis of IRA employee CVs reframes the "troll factory" as a labor-market institution embedded in Russia's media economy, recruited around Crimea (2014) rather than primarily for 2016. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes a "dual objective" model of Russian wartime propaganda serving domestic legitimacy and foreign perception management simultaneously, while [[Graham2025-gp]] shows how #IStandWithPutin exploited rather than evaded platforms' truth-infrastructure. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] documents coordinated amplification among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus. On detection and attribution, [[Keller2019-nk]] uses rare ground-truth data (South Korea's NIS scandal) to show coordination patterns — not bot-like features — best reveal astroturfing, while finding the campaign's actual influence was modest; [[Thiele2025-ol]] builds a general rational-choice typology for inferring covert principals from campaign structure; [[Starbird2019-qv]] reframes such operations as "collaborative work" blending orchestration and organic crowd participation; [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] and [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] extend detection to emerging platforms (Bluesky) and the adaptive persistence of campaigns over time; and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a broader category than CIB, spanning propaganda, gambling, and adult-content networks united by structural (not motivational) logic. [[Waight2025-al]] offers a methodological bridge, using LLMs to measure genuine narrative diffusion (e.g., Russian "biolabs" claims reaching U.S. outlets) where exact-text-reuse methods fail entirely.

## The war in Ukraine as a monitoring testbed

Several papers converge specifically on Ukraine-war disinformation as an EU-monitoring priority. [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an under-studied vector for TikTok war disinformation at massive scale (#Ukraine: 36.9 billion views); [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds video-based decontextualization — not AI-generated imagery — is most credible precisely because ample authentic Ukraine-war footage exists for recontextualization, suggesting low-tech manipulation may rival generative AI as a threat; [[Rogers2026-cy]], explicitly part of the EU-funded vera.ai project, maps "post-truth spaces" in Moldovan Facebook discourse to surface fact-checking leads; and [[Sadler2025-vu]]'s case study of NATO-blame narratives for the invasion exemplifies the narrative-ethics approach discussed above.

## Health misinformation and the infodemic

The COVID-19 domain anchors a cluster examining both circulation dynamics and real-world harm. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] provides a rare causal estimate linking antivaccine Twitter content to reduced vaccination and measurable excess cases/deaths via an epidemic-compartmental model. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" — demand-supply imbalances correlating with reduced credible content and increased misinformation — across six European countries' vaccine discourse. [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] offers an early, influential account of echo-chamber-driven cascade dynamics comparing science and conspiracy content on Facebook. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] complicates the misinformation frame by showing UK media's politically polarized citation of mainstream (not fringe) COVID scientists reflects political *use* of legitimate science rather than misinformation per se — a useful boundary case. [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] extends platform-governance analysis to climate misinformation, finding structurally weaker, more discursively evasive rules than for COVID or elections.

## Fact-checking: epistemics, institutions, and automation

A cluster interrogates fact-checking itself — its epistemological foundations, institutional legitimacy, and technological augmentation. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] proposes "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category needed for generative-AI outputs that evade evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based fact frameworks. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines the rhetorical "apologia" European fact-checkers deploy to defend platform-funding dependencies, while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically rebuts Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors, showing removal is rare and most verified content remains online. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written by Maldita.es practitioners, argues fact-checking must be supplemented with "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" given the Valencia DANA flood conspiracy's narrative mutation. On automation, [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows LLMs fact-check poorly without curated retrieval context (and exhibit left-leaning citation patterns even with web search), while [[Altoe2026-wu]] builds an end-to-end pipeline for narrative-level virality triage to close the gap between rapid misinformation peaks and slow fact-checker response, and [[Xue2025-bp]] examines the tension between fact-checking's objectivity norms and its strategic use of emotional framing. [[Luhring2025-od]] provides a crucial methodological audit of NewsGuard, the infrastructure underlying much of this research, cautioning against binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs.

## Platform governance and the politics of moderation

A substantial cluster interrogates how platforms discursively and procedurally govern (mis)information. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows Meta's Newsroom frames problematic content as originating from "outside" actors while positioning Meta as capable solver — a discursive move paralleled in [[Donovan2025-ws]]'s history of "misinformation-at-scale" as a structural feature of engagement-driven business models, and in [[Gillespie2022-jx]]'s account of algorithmic "reduction" as an under-scrutinized moderation form hidden behind the more visible politics of removal. [[Crosset2026-mq]] offers a comparative security-governance reading of EU/US legislation, identifying convergent "bureaucratisation" logics beneath divergent regulatory philosophies. [[Holt2026-zq]] and [[Miller2026-cp]] examine the post-professional-fact-checking turn concretely: Holt finds user-flagged "False News" on Facebook mostly reflects political disagreement rather than falsity, while Miller documents X's AI Note Writer API as additive to (not a replacement for) human Community Notes raters. [[Mosca2026-yh]] theorizes "fake news" accusations themselves as strategic rhetorical weapons (SDSA model) rather than misinformation responses, complementing [[Yoo2026-ev]]'s finding that "fake news" functions as a delegitimizing label across the U.S. ideological spectrum. [[Balluff2026-bv]] documents covert media capture via government advertising in Austria, showing democratic-corporatist systems are not immune to "journalism for sale" dynamics long associated with hybrid regimes.

## Measurement, scale, and contested harms

Running beneath all of the above is an unresolved empirical dispute about how much misinformation matters and to whom. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] document the disproportionate virality and reach of false content on Twitter and Facebook respectively, while [[Allen2020-nj]], [[Budak2024-ef]], and [[Tsfati2020-uo]] push back: fake news is a vanishingly small share of the whole media diet, exposure is concentrated among a tiny minority, and (per Tsfati) mainstream media may disseminate fake news more widely than fake-news sites themselves through correction and coverage. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] ground this debate psychologically, showing poor truth discernment stems more from inattention and reasoning failures than motivated partisanship, and that corrections are strikingly fragile (continued influence effect) absent alternative causal explanations. [[Lazer2018-mm]] and [[Freelon2020-yp]] serve as early field-defining statements calling for interdisciplinary, platform-collaborative research and conceptual precision (fake news vs. misinformation vs. disinformation vs. propaganda) — a conceptual project that [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts revisits at a mature stage, concluding that no single intervention suffices and that platform/journalism governance now outranks individual-level literacy and fact-checking in expert priority. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] grounds this debate empirically in everyday user experience, finding perceived misinformation is overwhelmingly textual, mainstream-sourced, and topically diffuse — a corrective to both alarmist and dismissive narratives about scale and source.

## Emergent technologies: AI, deepfakes, and synthetic reality

A final, forward-looking cluster addresses generative AI's reshaping of the field. [[Emilio2026-ik]] proposes "synthetic realities" (a four-layer content/identity/interaction/institution stack) as the successor concern to isolated deepfakes, formalizing a "Generative AI Paradox" whereby ubiquitous synthetic media causes rational discounting of all digital evidence. [[Choi2026-bz]] demonstrates a "modality-congruent carryover effect" whereby difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes susceptibility to subsequent same-modality fakes. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s large Kenyan field experiment shows deepfake impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated through audience comments — extending [[Starbird2019-qv]]'s participatory framework to AI-generated content and to a Global South context largely absent from this literature otherwise. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] (discussed above) provides the crucial empirical counterpoint that low-tech video decontextualization can rival or exceed AI-generated imagery in persuasive power, tempering some of the generative-AI alarm found elsewhere in the corpus. Finally, [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that misinformation's *source* (influencers vs. regular users) independently shapes downstream toxicity dynamics — a reminder that as the technological substrate of disinformation evolves, the social and parasocial infrastructures through which it is legitimized remain decisive.
