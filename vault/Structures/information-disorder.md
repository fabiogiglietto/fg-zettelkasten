# Information Disorder

# Information Disorder

## From facts to narratives: the conceptual pivot

If there is a single organizing move that runs through this shelf, it is the shift away from treating "fake news" as a discrete, falsifiable proposition and toward treating disinformation as *narrative*. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] lays foundational groundwork by relocating analytical attention from injectors' intent to propagators' judgments, proposing a micro-meso-macro model of how falsehoods cycle through a hybrid news system whose actors rarely converge on a single truth. [[Marwick2026-qd]] pushes this furthest, arguing that successful disinformation behaves as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic story — with characters, catchphrases, and moral arcs — such that domain-blacklisting or fact-checking approaches necessarily miss what makes it spread. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes this empirically, showing that cumulative exposure to recurring disinformation narratives (not isolated claims) predicts belief. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the missing theoretical apparatus, borrowing narratology and hermeneutic realism to show that narratives can be referentially and ethically suspect without containing any single false statement — a direct challenge to the true/false binary that still structures most fact-checking work. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] extend the narrative turn into collective sensemaking: election-fraud "deep stories" are co-produced by influencers, elites, and audiences through evidence-sharing and framing moves visible in quote-tweet chains, meaning individual posts cannot be assessed in isolation. [[Waight2025-al]] then offers the methodological payoff of this whole cluster — an LLM-based pipeline for measuring narrative similarity across languages that finally makes claims like these tractable at scale, revealing narrative overlap between Russian state media and mainstream U.S. outlets invisible to lexical reuse metrics. [[Goel2025-iq]] closes the loop empirically: source-based misinformation metrics miss the substantial repurposing of true mainstream content into misleading narratives, reinforcing the narrative-over-source thesis from a different angle. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] provides a meta-level corrective, arguing that the field's own governing analogies (infodemic, warfare, pollution) each capture only one level of this multilevel, complex-adaptive-system reality.

## Elites, parties, and the strategic weaponization of "fake news"

A second cluster examines disinformation as elite political strategy rather than grassroots contagion. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary analysis pinpoints radical-right populism specifically — not populism or right-ideology alone — as the strongest predictor of elite misinformation sharing, a finding echoed in [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s account of how "connective factions" within parties forge symbiotic ties to hyper-partisan media ecosystems around triggering issues like Critical Race Theory. [[Tai2026-qk]] examines institutional and ideological checks on officials' sharing behavior, while [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents Brazilian parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during a health crisis through distinct rhetorical postures (objectivity, authority, proximity). [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] both show "fake news" itself becoming a rhetorical weapon: hyperpartisan outlets and politicians deploy the accusation to delegitimize opponents and reshape Hallin's spheres of legitimacy, largely divorced from any descriptive concern with actual fabrication. [[Kim2026-wg]] demonstrates the sharpest edge of elite-driven disinformation — geo-racially targeted digital voter-suppression advertising with measurable turnout effects — while [[Balluff2026-bv]] traces covert media capture via government advertising, showing that even consolidated democracies are vulnerable to "pay positive to go negative" dynamics. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] turn to citizens' own polarized perceptions of disinformation, the latter proposing a "hostile misinformation effect" — a partisan-perception mirror of the hostile media effect — showing that who counts as a misinformation *target* is itself contested along identity lines. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] rounds out this theme by showing that mere media narratives *about* election interference — regardless of whether interference worked — can themselves produce asymmetric polarization.

## Platforms as infrastructure, gatekeeper, and battleground

A substantial cross-cutting concern is how platform architecture and governance shape disorder. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects Meta's own discursive self-positioning, showing how "coordinated inauthentic behavior" functions as a floating signifier that externalizes blame while eliding platform affordances like Groups. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] extends this into the post-2025 moment, empirically testing Zuckerberg's censorship narrative against fact-checkers' actual (limited) removal powers, while [[Renault2025-uh]] shows Community Notes itself reproduces (rather than eliminates) partisan asymmetries in flagged misinformation. [[Farkas2026-lr]] captures fact-checkers' own precarious rhetorical position, defending platform partnerships through apologia strategies of differentiation and transcendence. Measurement-focused studies chart platform-level prevalence and diffusion: [[Vincent_undated-re]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] (a European extension of Guess et al.'s Facebook newsfeed work), and [[noauthor_undated-bm]]'s large-scale study of Meta-identified deceptive networks, which found concentrated reach heavily amplified by non-network resharers rather than the networks themselves — and crucially, that naive associations between exposure and downstream political attitudes evaporate under adjustment. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids," showing these supply-demand imbalances correlate with lower-credibility content circulation. Platform-specific studies extend the picture to newer terrain: [[Bosch2024-hj]] on TikTok's sonic propaganda around Ukraine, [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] on TikTok immigration discourse in Canada, [[Copland2025-em]]'s account of Sky News Australia as a transnational "network propaganda" node bridging Australian and US right-wing ecosystems, and [[Ventura2025-sw]]'s deactivation experiment isolating WhatsApp's distinct role beyond feed-based platforms. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] and [[Marino2023-9137f448]] examine remediation dynamics specifically in Italian health and pandemic contexts, showing legacy media and "Alternative Influence Networks" jointly shape what circulates. Undergirding all of this, [[Heiss2026-qv]] and [[Pierri2025-hm]] step back to interrogate the research infrastructure itself: platforms' exclusive control of data creates industry-influence risks structurally similar to tobacco or pharma research, and the EU's DSA Article 40 — meant to remedy this — remains hobbled by resource asymmetries and implementation bottlenecks.

## Coordinated manipulation, bots, and information operations

Several papers turn to deliberately orchestrated manipulation. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers a theoretical corrective to the field's reliance on platform-derived categories like CIB, proposing a rational-choice attribution model keyed to scale, elaborateness, and disguise. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT analysis of the IRA's CVs revises received wisdom about troll-factory demographics and timing, showing recruitment tracked Crimea rather than the 2016 US election and revealing deep integration into Russia's ordinary media labor market. [[Kim2026-br]] extends this cross-nationally with an explainable-ML framework detecting two decades of suspected state-linked trolling in Korean news comments, finding morally condemning rhetoric — rather than direct foreign-state praise — as the dominant and most-amplified strategy. [[Graham2025-gp]] examines how #IStandWithPutin exploited rather than evaded platforms' "infrastructure of truth," and [[FitzGerald2025-nv]], [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]], [[Zhao2025-ny]], and [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] variously address the persistence/adaptability of manipulation campaigns, real-time bot detection on Bluesky, hashtag hijacking, and social bots as agenda-builders competing with human organizations for narrative control — collectively arguing that agenda-building and propaganda theories built for human actors need revision for machine-inflected ecosystems.

## Belief formation: conspiracy, radicalization, and everyday encounters

A cognitive/audience-facing thread examines how people actually come to hold false beliefs. [[Adam2026-tz]]'s panel-plus-tracking design demonstrates a "marriage" of media exposure and predispositions in COVID conspiracy belief formation, isolating both contagion (alternative media) and mitigation (mainstream media) effects alongside backfire dynamics among populists. [[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Marwick2025-vx]] together reframe radicalization: ConspiracyTok's demographically diverse creators build "populist knowledge production" through a "generous epistemology," while redpilling narratives reveal gradual socialization rather than sudden conversion, with disinformation functioning as "evidence" that legitimizes extremist belief as rational inquiry. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] implicates journalism itself in conspiracy formation via specific reporting strategies. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] broadens the empirical lens beyond "hot-button" topics, finding via digital diaries that everyday misinformation encounters center on business/economics and mainstream/alternative news clickbait rather than fringe sources — a useful corrective to topic-narrow research design. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] trace downstream consequences: electoral misinformation belief predicting political intolerance in Brazil, and antivaccine tweets causally linked (via a novel SIRVA epidemic model) to measurable vaccination declines and excess COVID cases and deaths.

## Interventions, corrections, and the fact-checking apparatus

Countermeasures form their own arc. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates prebunking can scale cheaply and durably via Instagram ads, with a persistent five-month effect. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] and [[Choi2026-bz]] probe visual/AI disinformation specifically, finding effects are issue-contingent (video decontextualization outperforms text only for salient, well-documented events) and that authentication difficulty carries over across encounters in modality-congruent ways. [[Xue2025-bp]] examines fact-checkers' own use of emotional framing, complicating fact-checking's presumed objectivity. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] theorizes a new epistemic category — "emergent facts" — needed once generative AI outputs no longer fit evidence-based, interpretative, or rule-based fact regimes. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] and [[Balluff2026-if]] jointly temper enthusiasm about LLMs as fact-checking tools: curated retrieval context dramatically outperforms raw model knowledge or web search, and communication researchers more broadly are urged toward reflexive, less resource-intensive methodological choices given reproducibility and bias risks. [[Emilio2026-ik]] and [[Triedman2025-uy]] extend the generative-AI concern to systemic scale — "synthetic realities" eroding institutional trust, and Grokipedia's derivative-but-degraded sourcing relative to Wikipedia, respectively — while [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes "AI hype" itself as a structuring force in journalism that requires "breakdown and repair" rather than mere debunking.

## Field-level reflexivity

Finally, several pieces step outside individual studies to interrogate the field's own health. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts finds pessimism dominant and platform/journalism-ecosystem interventions rated above individual-level fixes like fact-checking. [[Budak2024-ef]] argues public discourse has diverged from the empirical evidence on prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] imports lessons from climate psychology on intervention design and vested-interest resistance, and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] and [[Nenno2025-xa]] jointly press the field to escape its WEIRD-country default, the latter showing news-value expressions in flagged misinformation vary meaningfully by cultural context. [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of NewsGuard is essential methodological housekeeping for this entire literature, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs (rather than continuous scores) can substantially distort downstream findings — a caution that should be read alongside nearly every source-based study catalogued above. Together with [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] on adjacent measurement questions (politicized science sourcing, Facebook Reactions as engagement proxies) and older touchstones like [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] on Charlie Hebdo counter-hashtags, this reflexive layer signals a maturing field turning its own tools — narrative analysis, platform critique, epistemic skepticism — back on itself.
