# Information Disorder & Misinformation

## The shape of the problem: from "fake news" to narrative infrastructure

The corpus opens with a conceptual reckoning that recurs across three decades of scholarship gathered here: what, exactly, is being studied when we study "misinformation"? [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] stages this reckoning directly, arguing that creator-centric typologies of "fake news" cannot capture how falsity actually circulates in a hybrid news system; attention must shift to propagators and the layered judgments that convert a claim into a cascade. [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]] supply the underlying systems theory this move depends on — politics conducted through the interaction, not displacement, of old and new media logics, with power distributed unevenly across an increasingly polycentric "political information cycle." [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] extend this into an explicitly political register, arguing that disinformation is a symptom of declining institutional trust and should be seen as a "disinformation order" rather than a scatter of isolated hoaxes — a framing that refuses to let platforms or individual liars bear sole explanatory weight. [[Lazer2018-mm]] crystallizes the field's early self-diagnosis: fake news is a problem of eroded journalistic process and institutional safeguards, demanding interdisciplinary, platform-collaborative science rather than moralizing about individual gullibility.

A second wave of papers pushes back against treating "narrative" as a loose metaphor and insists it be theorized rigorously. [[Sadler2025-vu]] indicts the field for using "narrative" naively, proposing a hermeneutic-realist, narratological framework (Ricoeur, Phelan) that can assess content as referentially and ethically problematic without reducing to true/false. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the parallel move empirically, showing that successful disinformation behaves as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic story — with catchphrases, characters, and moral stakes — rather than as a discrete false fact amenable to domain-level detection. [[Starbird2025-jj]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]], and [[Prochaska2026-pr]] develop this into a full research program on U.S. election rumors: evidence-frame analysis shows misleadingness often lives in the *framing* applied to true evidence rather than in fabricated facts; "deep storytelling" analysis shows the *how* of telling (style, allusion, implicit cueing) is as constitutive as content; and the trope-grammar paper formalizes recurring Actor-Object-Action structures that let audiences slot novel events into a stable fraud mythology. [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[Waight2025-al]] supply complementary methodological infrastructure: Goel shows mainstream, factually accurate articles are routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives (undermining domain-blocklist measurement), while Waight's LLM-based narrative-similarity pipeline operationalizes "same claim about same event" at scale, demonstrating empirically that lexical-overlap methods miss most actual narrative diffusion (including Russian state narratives migrating into low-quality and even mainstream U.S. outlets). [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]] bring this narrative turn to policy practice: U.S. survey evidence that cumulative cross-outlet exposure to a narrative predicts belief, and a fact-checker's first-person account (Valencia floods) of why argument-checking and narrative monitoring must supplement claim-level correction — directly naming the PROMPT-style narrative-monitoring infrastructure this hub note is organized around. [[Altoe2026-wu]] pushes this furthest technically, reframing the fact-checking unit of analysis as the evolving narrative cluster and building a pre-peak virality-triage pipeline explicitly designed around fact-checkers' actual temporal bottleneck.

## Measuring the problem: prevalence, diffusion, and its limits

A long empirical lineage asks the blunter question of how much misinformation actually circulates and to whom. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Grinberg2019-ua]] established the foundational Twitter findings — falsehood travels farther/faster/deeper, but exposure/sharing is extremely concentrated among a small, older, more conservative minority — while [[Allcott2017-yz]] modeled fake news as a market equilibrium and estimated its 2016 electoral impact as plausibly modest. [[Allen2020-nj]] radically reframes scale by placing online fake news within the *whole* media diet (television still dominates), concluding fake news is roughly 0.15% of daily consumption — a corrective echoed by [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]], which finds untrustworthy-source exposure on Facebook/Instagram concentrated and largely without attitudinal effect, and by [[Allcott2019-gn]], which tracks a post-2016 Facebook-specific decline in misinformation diffusion relative to Twitter. [[Guess2021-ym]] and its European extension [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] use Meta's URL-shares infrastructure to show credible content vastly outweighs low-credibility content in aggregate, though exposure/sharing skew older and more ideologically congenial — a pattern [[Vincent_undated-re]] updates for TikTok (rising to ~25% exposure-weighted prevalence) and [[Rogers2026-zz]] extends through 2024, tracing how Meta's shrinking data access and shifting terminology ("fake news" → "misinformation" → "borderline content") make the underlying phenomenon harder to see even as right-wing commentary remains the disproportionate beneficiary of what engagement remains. Countering the link-centric paradigm entirely, [[Yang2023-cg]] shows image-based misinformation is far more prevalent (~20%) and partisan-skewed than text-based estimates suggest, while [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds users perceive misinformation mostly as clickbait-inflected mainstream/alternative news text, not fringe or audiovisual content — a reminder that "prevalence" depends enormously on what counts as the unit of measurement. [[Luhring2025-od]] interrogates the measurement infrastructure itself, auditing NewsGuard's stability and showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs (rather than continuous scores) can single-handedly distort cross-national findings. [[Nenno2025-xa]] globalizes the lens further, showing that news-value patterns in flagged misinformation across 24 countries only modestly generalize from WEIRD contexts — a point [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue introduction makes programmatically, and [[Humprecht2020-gd]] operationalizes structurally via a cross-national resilience framework identifying the U.S. as a uniquely vulnerable outlier.

## Psychology, belief formation, and the limits of correction

A parallel strand asks why people believe and share misinformation at all. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] reframes the field around a discernment/bias distinction, showing inattention and lack of reflective reasoning — not partisan motivated cognition — best explain poor truth discernment and the sharing-belief gap, with accuracy-nudge interventions as the resulting policy upshot. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] complicates the partisanship story at the sharing stage specifically: out-party animus, not ignorance, predicts fake-news sharing, which follows the same logic as ordinary partisan news sharing. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] remains the touchstone on why corrections fail (the continued-influence effect, backfire risk, and the narrow set of techniques — alternative causal accounts, repeated retraction, prebunking — that actually work), a foundation visible beneath [[Ramos2026-qo]]'s argument-checking proposal and [[Berlinski2023-rj]]'s finding that fact-check tweets fail to mitigate the confidence-eroding effects of elite voter-fraud claims, concentrated among Republicans and Trump approvers. [[Choi2026-bz]] extends this into the deepfake era, identifying a modality-congruent carryover effect in which difficulty authenticating one fake reshapes confidence and susceptibility toward subsequent same-format fakes. [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] supplies the network-structural complement: confirmation bias produces homogeneous echo chambers whose polarization, not raw virality, determines cascade size. [[Xue2025-bp]] closes a loop by showing the "objective" genre of fact-checking is itself saturated with emotional language, and [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that the *source* of misinformation (influencer vs. ordinary user) independently shapes the toxicity it provokes, converting credibility and parasocial bonds into collective antagonism.

## Political actors, elites, and strategic deployment

A substantial cluster treats misinformation/disinformation as elite political strategy rather than diffuse social pathology. [[Gaber2022-bk]] names this "strategic lying" in the Brexit/2019 UK election context — deliberate falsehood as agenda-priming device, impervious to fact-check rebuttal, which often amplifies rather than neutralizes the lie. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] generalizes this cross-nationally across 26 countries' parliamentary tweets, finding that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing, but their conjunction — radical-right populism — robustly does. [[Tai2026-qk]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] extend the elite-centered lens to institutional/ideological checks on U.S. officials and to how far-right electoral performance shapes European voters' disinformation concerns, respectively. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] turn to the *label* itself as a weapon: "fake news" and fake-news accusations function rhetorically to delegitimize opponents and reshape Hallin's spheres of legitimate debate, largely detached from actual veracity claims. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents a related phenomenon during Brazil's Manaus crisis — parliamentarians competing for epistemic authority via derivative reporting, direct authority claims, or local brokerage, effectively contesting journalistic gatekeeping itself. [[Balluff2026-bv]] and [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] show institutional capture operating from the supply side: covert advertising-for-coverage arrangements in Austria, and Meta's own strategic Newsroom framing that externalizes blame for problematic content onto "inauthentic" outsiders while eliding platform affordances like Facebook Groups.

The Brazilian case studies form their own dense sub-network on electoral misinformation's downstream democratic costs: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use panel data to show belief in electoral fraud claims erodes both political tolerance and trust in results over time, with messaging-app news use operating indirectly through misinformation belief; [[Kansaon2025-id]] traces how WhatsApp coordination translated fraud narratives into real-world mobilization including the January 8 attacks; and [[Ventura2025-sw]] uses a deactivation experiment to show WhatsApp's distinct causal role beyond feed-based platforms. [[Marwick2025-vx]] complements this with a qualitative account of how disinformation-as-"evidence" is woven into the gradual, socially embedded process of Far-Right "redpilling," challenging simple exposure-equals-radicalization models.

## Coordination, propaganda, and platform infrastructure

Several papers interrogate the organizational and infrastructural machinery beneath circulation. [[Starbird2019-qv]] offers the foundational CSCW reframing of information operations as *collaborative work* performed by and through crowds, blurring orchestrated and organic participation — a lens later cases apply directly: [[Copland2025-em]] to Sky News Australia's dual-strategy transnational propaganda pipeline, [[Gaw2025-ru]] to Philippine influence operations as political brokerage, [[Graham2025-gp]] to the #IStandWithPutin campaign's parasitism on trust infrastructure, [[Bosch2024-hj]] to TikTok's sonic propaganda techniques around the Ukraine invasion, and [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] to the adaptive persistence of manipulation campaigns across emerging events. [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] and [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] bring this to bot detection specifically — real-time coordinated-bot identification on Bluesky, and bots functioning as agenda-builders that most strongly shape campaign-level sentiment rather than press coverage. [[Rogers2026-cy]] formalizes "post-truth spaces" as a mapping technique for fact-checkers, demonstrating in a Moldovan case that borderline-content clusters, not core post-truth nodes, often carry the greatest network influence. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] traces an analogous architecture in COVID science discourse: a coordinated, overwhelmingly contrarian retweet network amplifying credentialed anti-consensus experts, with news coverage following rather than leading superspreader activity. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" as measurable demand-supply imbalances that empirically correlate with reduced credible content and increased misinformation — a structural account of *when* the information ecosystem becomes vulnerable, complementing [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]]'s finding that algorithmic news aggregation (Google News Italia) shapes which health topics gain engagement independent of source.

## Institutions, governance, and the fact-checking apparatus under strain

A closing cluster examines the institutions meant to manage this ecosystem and their growing fragility. [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] give fact-checkers' own voice: European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform-funded partnerships via differentiation/transcendence apologia, while post-2025 interviews reveal fact-checkers overwhelmingly favor labeling and transparency over removal and view Community Notes skeptically as vulnerable to coordinated manipulation. [[Holt2026-zq]] empirically tests the Community Notes/crowd-report premise directly, finding user-flagged "false news" mostly reflects political disagreement rather than detectable falsity — a wisdom-of-crowds failure with direct bearing on Meta's 2025 policy pivot, which [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes as the latest swing in a cycle where misinformation-at-scale is structurally built into engagement-driven business models, not an externality to be moderated away. [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] documents the same governance dynamic in the climate domain: platforms favor soft interventions and discursively offload responsibility to creators and external experts rather than adopting binding rules. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] step back to the research infrastructure itself, tracing the collapse of open API access (the "walled garden") and the structural conflicts of interest that compromise independent study of platform harms — concerns that bear directly on new frontiers opened by generative AI: [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows LLM fact-checkers are unreliable without curated retrieval and exhibit citation bias; [[Triedman2025-uy]] documents Grokipedia's derivative but ideologically skewed divergence from Wikipedia; and [[Emilio2026-ik]] theorizes the furthest horizon — a "synthetic reality" stack whose proliferation may rationally lead societies to discount all digital evidence, the GenAI paradox. [[Mahl2026-hc]] and [[Frischlich2025-vn]] serve as capstones to this whole arc: a Delphi panel converging on the need for coordinated, multi-level intervention portfolios rather than silver bullets, and a complexity-science argument that no single analogy (virus, weapon, pollutant) — and by extension no single narrative-monitoring or fact-checking fix — can capture a problem that is, at bottom, an irreducibly multilevel, adaptive system.
