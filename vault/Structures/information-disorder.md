# Information Disorder & Disinformation Narratives

## The Shape of the Problem: From "Fake News" to Information Disorder

The topic opens with a conceptual reckoning. [[Lazer2018-mm]] and [[Allcott2017-yz]] mark the moment "fake news" entered scholarly vocabulary as a tractable object, defined by fabrication and economic incentive; but [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] quickly argue the term should be abandoned, proposing instead a "disinformation order" rooted in institutional legitimacy crisis rather than discrete false stories. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] pushes this further with a cybernetic argument: fake news is observer-dependent, constituted at the point of propagation rather than creation, and any typology fixated on the liar's intent misses how falsehood actually cycles through a hybrid system. [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]] supply the structural vocabulary — the hybrid media system, the political information cycle — that much of this corpus takes for granted. [[Thiele2025-ol]] later formalizes the attribution problem this creates: distinguishing manipulative intent from mere inauthenticity remains unresolved even at the level of platform-released "coordinated inauthentic behavior" categories, a critique [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] extends by showing how Meta's own discourse strategically exploits this ambiguity as a floating signifier. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] caps this conceptual strand by arguing that no single root metaphor — virus, warfare, pollution — captures a genuinely multilevel complex system, a diagnosis echoed in [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study, which finds expert consensus converging on "no silver bullet" and multi-stakeholder governance.

## Measuring the Problem: Prevalence, Concentration, and the Ecosystem Baseline

A parallel, more empirical lineage asks simply: how much misinformation is there, and who actually encounters it? [[Vosoughi2018-at]] establishes that falsehood diffuses faster and deeper than truth on Twitter, driven by humans rather than bots. But a strong corrective tradition — [[Allen2020-nj]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Allcott2019-gn]], [[Guess2021-ym]], [[Eady2023-xg]] — insists that at the scale of the whole media ecosystem, fake news and foreign influence operations are a vanishingly small, heavily concentrated phenomenon, dwarfed by television and mainstream news consumption, with exposure skewed toward a tiny, already-committed conservative minority. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] extends this into 2020, finding concentrated exposure but no attitudinal effects from Facebook/Instagram untrustworthy-source content. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] continue this measurement tradition into contemporary Europe, tracking platform-level prevalence (TikTok's rising share) and cross-national trends in untrustworthy URL exposure. Yet this reassuring "small and concentrated" consensus is contested from several directions: [[Yang2023-cg]] shows that link-based measures radically undercount image-based misinformation on Facebook (20%+ of political images), [[Mosleh2024-op]] shows single-platform (Twitter-centric) conclusions don't generalize across seven platforms, and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] revisits Facebook diffusion patterns during the 2020 election at billion-post scale. Methodologically, [[Luhring2025-od]] audits the NewsGuard infrastructure underlying much of this work, warning that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can distort findings that continuous scores would not. [[Allen2020-nj]] and [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue introduction both push toward a structural, comparative, non-WEIRD-centric research agenda — a call substantively answered by [[Humprecht2020-gd]]'s cross-national resilience framework and [[Nenno2025-xa]]'s 24-country study of news values in flagged misinformation.

## The Narrative Turn: Beyond True/False

A major fault line in the corpus runs between content-centric measurement and a "narrative turn" that insists the true unit of analysis is not the discrete false claim but the story. [[Marwick2026-qd]] crystallizes this: disinformation succeeds as identity-affirming, cathartic cross-platform narrative, not as a factual error awaiting correction. [[Sadler2025-vu]] radicalizes the point theoretically, arguing disinformation studies' naïve realism about "narrative" obscures how content can be referentially and ethically troubling without being false — demonstrated through a NATO-blame case that resists the true/false binary entirely. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] operationalize this empirically for US election rumors, showing that misleading frames attach to (often accurate) evidence through collaborative sensemaking, with 2022 audiences interpreting ambiguous events through a "deep story" of fraud established in 2020. [[Goel2025-iq]] extends the mechanism to mainstream news itself, showing true articles from reputable outlets are disproportionately co-shared with fake news to support misleading narratives — a finding with real teeth against source-level blocklist methodologies. [[Waight2025-al]] supplies a methodological bridge, using LLMs to measure narrative similarity (rather than lexical or topical similarity) across languages, applied to the Russian "biolabs" narrative's diffusion into low-quality US outlets. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Rossini2026-jn]]/[[Rossini2026-mj]] show narrative-exposure and misinformation-belief frameworks translating into real effects on political tolerance and electoral trust in the US and Brazil. [[Ramos2026-qo]] turns this into practitioner method, proposing "argument-checking" and narrative monitoring as necessary supplements to claim-level fact-checking, illustrated through the Valencia flood "hidden bodies" conspiracy. [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Yoo2026-ev]] close the loop by showing "fake news" itself has become a rhetorical weapon — a delegitimizing label deployed by politicians and partisan outlets rather than a description of content quality, a dynamic also visible in [[Rodarte2026-dk]]'s account of Brazilian parliamentarians contesting journalistic epistemic authority.

## Platforms, Coordination, and Infrastructure

A substantial cluster examines the infrastructural and organizational machinery that produces and propagates disinformation. [[Benkler2018-lw]] remains the touchstone account of an asymmetric, radicalized right-wing media ecosystem insulated from truth-correcting norms; [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Knupfer2025-vt]] extend this network-propaganda lens to Sky News Australia and congressional "connective faction" dynamics respectively. On the covert-influence side, [[Keller2019-nk]] and [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] provide rare ground-truthed accounts of astroturfing organization (South Korea's NIS, the St Petersburg IRA), both emphasizing that coordination, not automation, is the diagnostic signal — a claim [[Thiele2025-ol]] and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] generalize into typological and real-time detection frameworks, and [[Kansaon2025-id]] demonstrates concretely for Brazilian WhatsApp networks feeding into real-world protest mobilization. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations as political brokerage infrastructure in the Philippines; [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] notes such campaigns persist by appropriating emerging events. [[Scalco2026-bd]] offers a structural-supply account via "information voids," linking demand-supply imbalance to misinformation prevalence. Platform accountability itself is contested: [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes "misinformation-at-scale" as a built-in feature of engagement-driven business models and documents platforms' retreat from moderation; [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] examine the fallout for fact-checkers after Meta's 2025 rupture with third-party verification; [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] indicts platforms directly for democratic backsliding; and [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s Grokipedia analysis extends this to AI-generated reference infrastructure, finding ideological slant and reliance on blacklisted sources baked into an ostensibly neutral encyclopedia.

## War, Elections, and Identity-Group Narratives

The PROMPT project's substantive monitoring concerns — Ukraine, elections, LGBTQI+ and other identity politics — recur as applied terrain for the above frameworks. On Ukraine: [[Graham2025-gp]] examines #IStandWithPutin's exploitation of platform trust infrastructure, [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as a TikTok-specific propaganda vector, and [[Waight2025-al]]'s biolabs case and [[Hameleers2026-mc]]'s comparative study of textual/AI/video disinformation (finding video outperforms text specifically for the high-salience, well-documented Ukraine case) both use the war as empirical ground. On elections: [[Starbird2025-jj]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]], [[Rossini2026-jn]], [[Rossini2026-mj]], [[Ventura2025-sw]], and [[Kansaon2025-id]] collectively trace how electoral fraud narratives form, persist, and erode democratic trust across the US and Brazil, while [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows UK media narratives *about* interference can themselves asymmetrically polarize audiences, and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" demonstrates citizens perceive their own side as disproportionately targeted. [[Gattermann2025-yx]], [[Iris2026-pg]], [[Tornberg2025-ir]], and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] turn to the far-right specifically: disproportionate media visibility for radical-right parties, radical-right populism (not populism or right-ideology alone) as the strongest predictor of elite misinformation-sharing, and platform-tailored securitizing visual campaigns (Sweden Democrats) explicitly using out-of-context footage. [[Gaber2022-bk]] historicizes "strategic lying" through Brexit and the 2019 UK election as continuous with, rather than a rupture from, political spin. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offers an earlier case of discursive contestation (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie) that anticipates the narrative-contest dynamics seen in later election studies. [[Much2026-gu]] and [[Marwick2025-vx]] extend the identity/narrative lens to radicalization pathways — gendered podcast ecosystems and "redpilling" as gradual socialization rather than sudden conversion — showing how disinformation operates as part of broader identity formation rather than discrete persuasion events.

## Psychological and Behavioral Mechanisms

Underpinning the corpus is a persistent psychological question: why do corrections fail and why do people share what they share? [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] remains foundational on the continued-influence effect and the narrow conditions under which debunking succeeds. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] and [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] locate sharing behavior in partisan motivation and echo-chamber homogeneity rather than ignorance, a finding [[Tsfati2020-uo]] complicates by showing mainstream media's own amplification of fake news stories may reach more people than fake sites directly. [[Choi2026-bz]] and [[Wack2026-bt]] bring this into the generative-AI era, showing deepfake impact is modality-dependent and socially negotiated through audience comments rather than fixed at production. [[Xue2025-bp]] examines emotionality as a fact-checking strategy, [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] traces how influencer-sourced misinformation generates qualitatively distinct toxicity, and [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] closes the causal loop by linking antivaccine Twitter content to measurable reductions in vaccination and increased mortality. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] steps back to recommend misinformation research import methodological lessons — behavioral over cognitive outcomes, systems thinking, organized resilience — from the more mature field of climate psychology, a fitting bookend to a field still negotiating its own foundational categories.

## Science, Journalism, and the LLM Frontier

A final cluster examines misinformation's intersection with science communication, journalism, and emerging AI tools. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]], [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]], and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] examine how scientific and health content is filtered, politicized, and amplified across coordinated networks, partisan press, and algorithmic gatekeepers. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] implicates journalistic strategies themselves in conspiracy belief formation. On the newest frontier, [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows LLMs are poor fact-checkers without curated retrieval augmentation and exhibit citation bias toward left-leaning credible sources, while [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s Grokipedia study and [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s "post-truth spaces" concept (developed for the vera.ai verification project) point toward the next generation of applied, practitioner-facing tools for locating and attributing influence within an increasingly AI-mediated information environment — bringing the structure note full circle to the practical monitoring work the PROMPT project itself undertakes.
