# Cross-National Influence Operations

## The comparative turn in influence-operations research

The papers filed here mark a shift away from single-country, single-platform case studies toward genuinely comparative accounts of deceptive coordination. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] provides the programmatic statement: the field has been disproportionately anchored in WEIRD contexts, and understanding misinformation as a global democratic problem requires cross-platform, cross-cultural inquiry. The rest of the corpus can be read as responses to that call, working outward from Russia and the United States toward Nigeria, India, Brazil, the Philippines, Korea, Serbia, and China — while also probing whether the concepts inherited from the US/Russia-centric literature (bots, troll farms, "coordinated inauthentic behavior") travel well.

## National case studies and the diversity of tactics

Several papers supply thick description of a single national ecosystem, and together they demonstrate that "influence operation" is not one thing. [[Kansaon2025-id]] shows how Brazilian WhatsApp groups turned coordinated messaging into street mobilization — doxxing Supreme Court justices and helping organize the January 8 uprising — revealing how encrypted, closed platforms escape detection regimes built for Twitter. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes Philippine influence operations not as content campaigns but as *brokerage*: covert firms mediating between political clients and voters through durable political-economic infrastructures, a lens that resonates with Poliakoff2026-zk's organizational history of the St Petersburg IRA, which likewise recasts a "troll factory" as a labor-market actor embedded in Russia's media economy rather than a purely covert cell. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] adds Serbia, arguing that SNS-linked networks represent a subtler, harder-to-detect form of digital authoritarianism than centralized bot farms because they mimic authentic grassroots support — a claim that anticipates the methodological worries raised later in the corpus. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] documents Telegram-based amplification networks in Russia and Belarus, while [[Kim2026-br]] offers the longest lens of all: two decades of Korean troll activity, showing that suspected foreign-linked accounts rely less on praising favored actors than on morally condemning domestic leaders across the ideological spectrum, a polarization-first strategy rewarded by engagement-based ranking. [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] extends the comparative reach into China, showing how the same platform infrastructure simultaneously enabled COVID-protest mobilization and state-aligned coordinated repression — underscoring that coordination is dual-use, not intrinsically oppositional or regime-aligned. [[Beacken2026-zb]] is the pivot point for the topic's explicit geographic remit (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey), arguing against technologically deterministic accounts: generative-AI propaganda adoption is shaped by local political, economic, and social structure rather than following one global trajectory — a structuralist counterweight to swarm-threat narratives elsewhere in the set.

## Ukraine as a shared proving ground

The war in Ukraine functions as a natural experiment that recurs across otherwise disparate methodological traditions. [[Waight2025-al]] uses the Russian "US-funded biolabs" narrative to demonstrate that narrative similarity — shared claims about shared events — is a distinct estimand from lexical or topical overlap, and shows that low-quality US outlets, not mainstream ones, were the primary conduits for Russian-aligned claims. [[Bosch2024-hj]] turns to TikTok's sonic affordances as an underexamined vector for war-related mis/disinformation, extending the platform-specificity argument beyond text and image. [[Graham2025-gp]]'s #IStandWithPutin case study makes an infrastructural claim that complements both: propaganda succeeds not by evading verification systems but by parasitizing them — hashtags, algorithmic amplification, and trust signals become the delivery mechanism. [[Makeev2026-ma]] supplies the strategic frame tying these together, theorizing a dual-objective propaganda model in which Russian domestic messaging (high stakes, low costs) diverges deliberately from foreign-facing messaging designed to shape international audiences' cost-benefit perceptions — a claim that helps explain why the same war generates such stylistically different narrative streams across the other Ukraine-focused papers.

## Transnational media networks and connective action

A parallel strand traces how ideologically aligned outlets and audiences fuse across borders without necessarily being "operations" in the covert sense. [[Copland2025-em]] shows Sky News Australia running a bifurcated strategy — parochial content for domestic Facebook audiences, US-coded conspiracy content for global YouTube reach — effectively inserting itself into the American right-wing propaganda pipeline. [[Groebner2026-pc]] generalizes this finding across four Anglophone far-right channels (Sky News Australia, Fox News, Rebel News, GB News), documenting substantial transnational commenter overlap with US content occupying the structural center. [[Bennett2025-xs]] supplies the theoretical scaffolding for why such networks matter politically: a "communication-as-organization" account in which digitally networked publics become surrogate organizations pulling conservative parties toward illiberalism, a dynamic the authors explicitly extend beyond the US case to European parallels. Read together, these three papers argue that transnational influence need not be orchestrated by a covert principal at all — audience-driven, platform-enabled connective action can produce comparable effects to deliberate coordination.

## Attribution, definition, and the limits of detection

A methodologically self-critical cluster interrogates whether the field's core concepts and detection tools are adequate to this comparative ambition. [[Thiele2025-ol]] argues that "coordinated inauthentic behavior" conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent and proposes instead a rational-choice typology keyed to principals' resources and stakes, inferred from scale, elaborateness, and disguise. [[Pante2025-pq]] applies this skepticism directly, re-examining claims of inter-state collaboration on Twitter/X and finding that earlier findings do not survive rigorous coordination detection with proper control datasets — a cautionary note against over-attributing coordination. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] contributes the temporal dimension implicit in the topic's "spaghetti-throwing" framing: manipulation campaigns are not static releases but persistent, adaptive processes that latch onto emerging events to sustain relevance, iteratively throwing narratives at the wall to see what sticks across news cycles and platforms.

## The next frontier: generative AI as an amplifier and a target

The corpus closes by looking forward. [[Schroeder2026-im]] warns that agentic, LLM-powered "swarms" could fuse the adaptive, iterative logic already visible in human-run operations with autonomous, round-the-clock coordination — manufacturing consensus, poisoning training data ("LLM grooming"), and outpacing detection-based defenses. [[Waight2026-ts]] provides an empirical demonstration of exactly this training-data pathway, showing that state-coordinated Chinese media is memorized by commercial LLMs and shapes model outputs toward pro-regime valence, with the effect generalizing across 37 countries in proportion to media-freedom levels. Together with [[Beacken2026-zb]]'s structural caution about uneven GenAI adoption, these papers suggest the comparative logic that organizes this whole topic — attending to how tactics are adapted, not merely transplanted, across national contexts — will need to be extended from human-run "spaghetti-throwing" operations to the AI systems now positioned as both instruments and targets of influence.
