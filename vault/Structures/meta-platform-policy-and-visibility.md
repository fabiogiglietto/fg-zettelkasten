# Meta Platform Policy and Political Visibility

## The political content reduction policy as empirical anchor

The centre of gravity for this topic is [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], which supplies the first systematic evidence that Meta's political content reduction policy materially reduced the Facebook reach of Italian parliamentarians — roughly 72% at trough for re-elected MPs — and that this suppression began nearly a year before Meta's own announced global rollout, with only partial recovery after the January 2025 reversal. Crucially, the paper's discovery of an asymmetric effect, where extremist accounts compensated for per-post declines by posting far more frequently and ended up gaining aggregate reach, reframes "de-politicization" policies not as neutral dampeners of political noise but as interventions with differential, potentially counterproductive, consequences for who becomes visible. This finding resonates directly with [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], which examines Facebook's URL-sharing ecosystem at national scale and shows that partisan-audience content is systematically penalised in the shares-to-views conversion while professionally sourced journalism is rewarded — and, more importantly, that both effects fluctuate sharply around known governance moments (the 2020 "break the glass" measures), evidence that Facebook is an active, temporally variable curator rather than a stable conduit. Read together, these two papers establish the empirical throughline of the topic: Meta's visibility architecture is not fixed policy but a moving target whose effects are detectable mainly through inference from outcomes, since the company does not disclose the interventions themselves — a transparency deficit both papers explicitly flag.

## From cooperative to conflictual platform governance

A second cluster traces the institutional and discursive shift underlying these policy moves. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows how Meta's Newsroom, in the 2016–2021 period, discursively externalised the "problem" of problematic content onto inauthentic actors and advertisers while presenting Meta itself as capable technocratic solver — a rhetorical strategy that arguably paved the way for policies later justified as protecting users from "too much politics." [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] picks up the story at its 2025 inflection point, empirically testing Zuckerberg's claim that fact-checkers functioned as censors and finding it largely unsupported: removal following fact-check verification was rare (~30%), and fact-checkers themselves favour labelling and counter-speech over deletion, framing Meta's shift as a move from "preemptive cooperation" to "conflictual cooperation" with epistemic institutions. [[De2026-ld]] supplies the broader discursive vocabulary for this era — "changecraft" — showing how Meta (alongside TikTok, YouTube, and X) narrates policy pivots such as the end of third-party fact-checking as continuity ("updating our approach") rather than rupture, aestheticizing infrastructural change while obscuring the deeper reallocation of power it enacts. [[Moran2025-qn]] and [[Donovan2025-ws]] zoom out further, situating Meta's retrenchment within an industry-wide unravelling of trust-and-safety infrastructure after 2021, driven by political backlash, layoffs, and the recognition that "misinformation-at-scale" is structurally endogenous to engagement-based business models rather than an external contaminant platforms can fully excise. Together this cluster narrates a coherent arc: rhetorical externalization of blame (Hurcombe) → real institutional retreat (Moran, Donovan) → policy reversal justified through continuity narratives (De2026) → empirical unmasking of the gap between justification and effect (Cazzamatta, Giglietto).

## Comparative pressure from adjacent platforms

Because Meta's 2025 pivot toward Community Notes explicitly borrows legitimacy from X's model, the topic is illuminated by papers auditing that model directly. [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] both stress-test the crowdsourced, bridging-based logic that Meta is now adopting: Renault shows Republican-authored misinformation is still flagged more than Democrat-authored misinformation even under Community Notes, refuting the debiasing rationale for abandoning professional fact-checking, while Bouchaud demonstrates that bridging algorithms structurally undermoderate the most polarizing content by design — a finding with direct import for what Meta's Community Notes rollout should be expected to (fail to) catch. The broader X literature also offers a methodological and substantive mirror for Meta research: [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds that X's algorithmic feed causes a persistent rightward attitudinal shift via changed following behaviour, explicitly reconciling its results with the null findings of "the 2020 Meta study" ([[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Bakshy2015-rn]]) by showing that algorithmic effects operate through durable changes in network composition rather than transient exposure — a mechanism Meta's own collaborative research design may have been unable to detect. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] extend this critical-audit tradition to ownership-driven algorithmic favoritism and inadvertent ideological profiling in recommender embeddings, both cautionary analogues for scrutinizing Meta's own opaque ranking systems. [[Ventura2026-yc]] adds a state-intervention counterpoint: Brazil's ban of X shows how platform-level political events (bans, policy shifts) can produce durable partisan "sorting ratchets," a dynamic structurally similar to what Giglietto's Italian data suggests may be happening more subtly through Meta's reach throttling. Cross-platform moderation studies — [[Rieder2026-pp]] on YouTube's inability to contain "ambient" Tate content, [[Karo2026-dn]] on jihadist evasion of TikTok moderation, [[Jurg2025-ur]] on YouTube's inconsistent authority-signalling during EU elections — collectively demonstrate that visibility governance failures are not Meta-specific but symptomatic of engagement-optimized architecture generally, reinforcing Donovan's structural argument. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment is the most constructive counterpoint in the set, showing that alternative, "diversified extremity" ranking can reduce norm-distorting amplification without users' feed enjoyment, implicitly indicting the default engagement logic Meta and its peers still deploy.

## Visibility beyond text: images, ads, and cross-national scale

Two papers extend the visibility question beyond feed ranking to other modalities of political presence. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs for auditing visual political communication on Instagram, opening a methodological path for scaling up studies of "concentrated visibility" of candidates versus parties — directly complementary to reach-based analyses like Giglietto's. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Allcott2025-jb]] address paid visibility: the former flags that ad-delivery algorithms (on Meta and Google) shape political ad reach and cost in ways opaque to campaigners, while the latter's null experimental findings on 2020 US political ads represent the "old" cooperative-research baseline against which more recent, non-cooperative studies (Giglietto, Renault) implicitly argue for methodological independence. [[Iris2026-pg]] and [[Balluff2026-bv]] push the visibility question to the level of media systems rather than platforms per se — disproportionate radical-right coverage across European news outlets, and covert advertising-driven favouritism in Austrian tabloid coverage of Kurz — both useful comparative baselines for distinguishing platform-specific from media-system-wide visibility distortions, and both implicitly asking whether Meta's algorithmic penalties or rewards for "partisan" and "quality" content (per Giglietto2026-632ef967) interact with or offset these traditional-media biases.

## Infrastructure, data access, and the conditions of knowing

A final cluster addresses why so much of this literature must proceed by inference rather than direct disclosure. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that Meta was one of only two platforms to engage explicitly with "data quality" in DSA consultations — and did so by inverting the argument, claiming its own data was too poor to justify researcher access, a strategic move that helps explain the transparency deficits Giglietto and Cazzamatta document empirically. [[Pierri2025-hm]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] chart the institutional terrain of Article 40 access more broadly, arguing that voluntary "independence by permission" research (the 2020 Meta collaborations) is structurally compromised, and that the DSA's shift toward access-as-regulatory-right, however uneven in implementation, is what makes newer, more adversarial analyses (like the Meta Content Library-based Giglietto studies) possible at all. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] adds a linguistic-equity dimension to this infrastructure critique, showing Meta's moderator allocation still disadvantages non-English languages, while [[Crosset2026-mq]] theorizes the whole regulatory turn — DSA, NetzDG, US state laws — as a convergence on "security bureaucratisation," in which transparency and auditing requirements, rather than direct content control, become the mechanism by which platforms like Meta are governed from a distance. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] offer historical ballast for this claim: McNally's decade-long Guardian dataset shows Facebook's News Feed algorithm is empirically not a "black box" but a system whose interventions leave detectable, lagged traces, precisely the auditing logic the DSA now seeks to institutionalize; Rossi's European URL-sharing analysis, building on Meta's own transparency data, shows how fragile and geographically uneven "trustworthy news" exposure already was well before the 2025 policy reversal. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review of Facebook Reactions research rounds out the picture as a reminder that even Meta's smallest affective-engagement affordances have long been implicated in amplifying negative, politically charged content — the granular mechanism underlying the coarser reach effects this whole literature now documents at scale.

*(Three filed papers — [[Volpe2026-um]] on micro-influencer labour, [[Swartz2026-zb]] on scam culture, and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] on health-news gatekeeping via Google News — sit at the topic's periphery: useful for thinking about platform culture and gatekeeping generally, but not directly engaged with Meta's political-visibility policy regime.)*
