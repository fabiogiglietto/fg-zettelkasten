# Platform Engagement & Algorithmic Curation

## The dissolution of "social media" as a category

A striking thread running through the most recent entries in this collection is the claim that the object of study itself—"social media"—may no longer exist in the form that gave rise to two decades of platform research. [[Boyd2026-op]] argues that platforms once organized around reciprocal peer exchange have become "parasocial media," dominated by scrolling, influencer content, and one-sided attention. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] extends this into a full periodization, arguing that algorithmic recommendation, generative AI, and user retreat into closed spaces are splitting "social media" into three post-social formations: algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the morphological vocabulary for the first of these: a shift from "networked publics" built on explicit interpersonal ties to "clustered publics" built on inferred behavioral similarity, with TikTok as the paradigm case and other platforms undergoing "TikTokification." Together these three papers reframe everything else in this topic cluster—they suggest that studies of reactions, engagement, and curation documented below are measuring a system already in transition to something else, where the "user" becomes a spectator fed by watch-time rather than a node in a social graph.

## What the algorithm does and doesn't do: the causal evidence

A second cluster directly tests the causal power of feed algorithms, often with contradictory results that resist easy synthesis. The large Meta-collaboration studies—[[Guess2023-ur]], [[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]], and [[Bakshy2015-rn]]—largely find that algorithmic curation reshapes on-platform experience (engagement, content mix, time spent) without producing measurable shifts in polarization, knowledge, or behavior, while individual choice and network composition do much of the sorting work. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] directly complicates this null-effects consensus: a platform-independent field experiment on X finds that the algorithmic feed *does* shift political attitudes rightward, and reconciles the discrepancy with Meta's findings by showing that algorithmic exposure changes which accounts people *follow*—a persistence mechanism invisible to studies that only toggle the algorithm on and off within existing networks. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] add mechanism-level detail from the X/Twitter case: algorithmic visibility gains for right-leaning accounts are explained less by partisanship than by proximity to Elon Musk and "agitating" content, while a reconstructed embedding of X's recommender shows that ideology is linearly encoded even without being an explicit design target. [[Brown2026-br]] applies similar supply/demand decomposition logic to YouTube, finding strong "rabbit hole" effects (content-to-content momentum) but little support for algorithmically driven echo chambers or radicalization—user choice, not the algorithm, drives most ideological patterning, though the system does nudge all users toward a baseline conservative tilt. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment reframes the mechanism again: engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social *norms* of political dialogue (not directly measured attitudes), and a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without killing engagement—suggesting algorithmic effects may operate through misperception of the discourse climate rather than direct persuasion, a pathway orthogonal to what the Meta studies tested.

## Curation beyond toggling: platforms as active, fluctuating gatekeepers

A related but distinct line of work insists that "the algorithm" is not a fixed treatment condition but a continuously shifting governance apparatus. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] supplies the conceptual anchor, arguing that "reduction"—demotion short of removal—constitutes a core, underexamined form of content moderation that blurs curation and governance. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] operationalize this empirically on Facebook: both find that algorithmic treatment of news is detectable through time-lagged, event-sensitive interventions rather than a stable background process. McNally shows hard news specifically (not opinion, lifestyle, sport) responds to ranking changes with a consistent ~3-week lag; Giglietto shows that the "partisan penalty" and "quality reward" built into the shares-to-views relationship intensified sharply around the 2020 election and COVID, implying active governance recalibration rather than stable network structure. [[Votta2025-xz]] extends this to political advertising, where ad delivery algorithms—not just advertiser targeting—shape reach and cost. Read together with [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s account of the "Tate-space" on YouTube—where deplatforming is symbolically significant but structurally outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation—this cluster argues that curation is an ongoing, asymmetric, and often invisible exercise of platform power that static audits or one-time experiments can mischaracterize as neutral or stable.

## Partisan structure of exposure and sharing

Several papers map the ideological architecture of what circulates, often pushing back against simple filter-bubble narratives while documenting real, asymmetric segregation. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] is the foundational finding that individual choice narrows cross-cutting exposure more than Facebook's algorithm does; [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] revisits this with URL-level granularity in 2020 and finds segregation is far higher than domain-level web-browsing studies suggested, intensifies along the "funnel of engagement," and is sharply asymmetric, with a homogeneous conservative corner of the ecosystem housing most fact-checked misinformation. [[Green2025-ap]] offers a methodological corrective underlying both: domain-level partisanship measures mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, meaning "curation bubbles" form through networked re-bundling of individual stories rather than wholesale source selection—a finding that also helps explain why [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]]'s European replication of Guess et al.'s newsfeed-quality approach finds relatively stable *View* shares for untrustworthy content even as *Share* percentages rise around elections. [[Messing2014-jc]], the earliest paper in the set, anticipates much of this by showing social endorsement cues can override partisan source cues in selection experiments—a optimistic counterpoint that the later, larger-scale studies complicate but do not fully overturn. [[Schulz2026-ts]] adds a further twist: much apparent polarization is an artifact of self-selection into posting by ideologically extreme users, meaning the visible discourse systematically misrepresents the underlying opinion distribution—a mechanism that dovetails with [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s Reddit study, which finds minimal cross-ideological overlap even among discursively similar subreddits and argues polarization is "sedimented" as a structural precondition of platform design, moderation, and user agency rather than a measurable discourse outcome. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] proposes a methodological "political cartography" addressing exactly the oversimplifications (unidimensional leaning, outlet-level focus, national parochialism) that this cluster has been working around.

## Reactions, affect, and the engagement logic of partisan communities

A substantial empirical thread examines Facebook's Reactions feature and affective engagement as the proximate mechanism linking content to visibility. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review synthesizes this literature, establishing that anger reliably drives engagement in political contexts while lifestyle content skews positive—a pattern [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] confirms comparatively across Norwegian, Swedish, and Danish elections, finding Angry reactions predict sharing while Love reactions suppress it, and that populist parties are disproportionately effective at mobilizing anger. [[Larsson2026-ro]]'s decade-long study of Norway's Progress Party shows this dynamic is not static: negative sentiment has grown over time, particularly under a new leader, and shares/comments follow a different logic than likes. [[Marino2026-he]] pushes this temporal sensitivity further and furthest from the Global North, showing that a hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network's affective engagement is highly unstable over three years, with a sharp 2023 inflection around Lula's inauguration and the coup attempt—directly undercutting the assumption, implicit in cross-sectional echo-chamber research, that hyperpartisan communities behave as stable, impermeable units. [[Xue2025-bp]] complicates the normative picture further by showing that even fact-checking content, nominally aligned with objectivity, strategically deploys emotionality to drive engagement, while [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that the *source* of misinformation (influencer vs. ordinary user) shapes the toxicity of audience response via credibility legitimation and parasocial enmeshment—mechanisms that connect the Reactions literature to the platform-ecology work on influencers discussed below.

## Platform-specific vernaculars, memes, and the aesthetics of circulation

Where the Reactions literature treats affect quantitatively, a cluster of qualitative and multimodal studies examines how specific platform grammars shape what ideological content looks like. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]'s clustered-publics thesis undergirds [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s finding that far-right Instagram content relies on positive affect and humor—"multimodal softening"—to normalize extremism through algorithmic proximity rather than overt hostility, and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]]'s cross-platform study of the Sweden Democrats, which shows the same party producing argumentative text on Facebook/X but memetic, affectively charged, often geographically misleading content on TikTok aimed at younger voters. [[Cabbuag2024-me]]'s account of Filipino TikTok "dogshows" and [[Graham2026-fb]]'s study of Reddit "botting" as participatory culture both push against treating platform content simply as strategic messaging, instead reading incivility and repetitive posting as culturally negotiated, community-sanctioned practices enabled by platform affordances. [[Tuters2026-vo]]'s study of "Great Reset" conspiracism develops the most ambitious infrastructural account here, arguing that platform grammars (hashtag co-occurrence, modular remixing) generate conspiratorial alignment *before* coherent narrative forms, a "high-tech paranoid style" that reads patterned algorithmic visibility as intentional elite coordination—complementing [[Kasianenko2026-tn]]'s finding that Google's AI Overviews apply inconsistent debunking guardrails depending on how established a conspiracy theory is. [[Topinka2026-hb]] extends this logic into the generative-AI register, theorizing "slopaganda" as affectively resonant, obviously-fake AI content that hooks engagement through ambient attunement rather than deception—a direct bridge to [[Tang2026-gu]]'s ethnography of Chinese "AI slop" creators who treat virality itself as an engineerable, benchmarkable production target. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s and [[Lukito2026-il]]'s work on cross-platform curation by right-leaning media and political actors (Truth Social vs. Twitter, TikTok vs. Facebook) shows the same strategic logic operating at the level of institutional actors deliberately tailoring packaging to platform grammar, echoing [[Bouchafra2026-ts]].

## Creators, labor, and the human cost of algorithmic demand

A final thread turns from content and audiences to producers. [[Volpe2026-um]]'s ethnography of micro-influencers shows how platform demands for constant, algorithmically legible output shape even ostensibly ethical content niches like second-hand fashion, blending care-labor rhetoric with reputational strategy under conditions of precarity. This dovetails with [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s account of how YouTube's recommendation system rewards and sustains an entire decentralized ecosystem of reaction, remix, and interview channels around a deplatformed figure, and with [[Wan2026-ai]]'s and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s user-side literature on algorithm literacy and political efficacy, which asks how ordinary users understand and navigate—rather than simply suffer—algorithmic curation. [[Wedel2026-ix]]'s audience-centered concept of "informativeness" and [[Much2026-gu]]'s account of podcasts as "gendered media spaces" both extend this user-agency perspective into adjacent genres (short video, podcasting) where incidental, non-journalistic exposure increasingly substitutes for traditional news consumption, suggesting that the architecture of engagement and ideological sorting documented throughout this topic is migrating beyond the feed itself into the broader attention economy that [[Boyd2026-op]] and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argue is superseding "social media" as such.
