# Platform Data Access & Governance

## The Collapse and Reconstruction of Research Infrastructure

The topic's center of gravity is a historical rupture: the "post-API age" that Freelon first named in [[Freelon2018-ao]] and later periodized in [[Freelon2024-sc]], tracing data access across five eras from laissez-faire APIs through academic cooperation to today's pay-to-play and walled-garden regimes. [[Bruns2019-nr]] supplies the polemical name for the originating shock—the "APIcalypse" following Cambridge Analytica—arguing that platforms instrumentalized privacy panic to suppress critical, public-interest scrutiny rather than genuinely protect users. Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] eulogize the Twitter API specifically, with the latter offering hard bibliometric evidence: 25% annual growth in Twitter-based scholarship through 2022, stagnation in 2023, and a 13% decline in 2024 once Musk's $42,000/month Enterprise pricing took hold. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] extends this into a three-act structure—Wild West, Golden Age, Walled Garden—explicitly generalizing the Twitter/X trajectory to Meta, Reddit, and TikTok. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review corroborates this at the level of published social science: empirical social-media research tripled from 2010 but plateaued and declined in 2022–2024, with Twitter/X and Facebook disproportionately dominant relative to actual platform usage, and multi-platform work remaining rare (82.59% single-platform studies).

The infrastructure that replaced open APIs is itself a research object. [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "Seeing like an API" framework shows how CrowdTangle shaped not just what could be seen but what counted as competent methodology, with researchers' adaptive workarounds (optimizing, extending, verifying) simultaneously contesting and entrenching platform epistemics—while privileging well-resourced, Global North institutions. [[Bruns2026-pn]] picks up the successor infrastructure, the Meta Content Library's "clean room," arguing its design embeds distrust of researchers, privileges quantitative single-platform work, and widens North-South and discipline-based inequities—echoing [[Lukito2026-nb]]'s "price, proficiency, or permission" typology of 72 access tools, which finds consumption-side data almost entirely missing (6 of 72 tools) and documents Meta's serial churn through Social Science One, CrowdTangle, FORT, and MCL. [[Zheng2026-bi]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]] attack the problem from the tool-auditing side: TubeStats/TokStats builds defensible random-sample denominators against YouTube and TikTok's opportunistic-sample bias, while Rieder's audit of YouTube's search API reveals a "forgetful by design" temporal-decay pattern that undermines retrospective research entirely. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's DSA-mandated Research API similarly inadequate despite its 2023 expansion.

## The DSA as Contested Remedy

A second cluster treats the EU Digital Services Act, especially Article 40, as the regulatory answer to platform-controlled access—while documenting how contested and incomplete that answer remains. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], and [[Pierri2026-ib]] offer complementary stock-takes of what Article 40 practically changes for election and political-communication research, emphasizing legal mandate as a genuine shift from philanthropy to right, but also cataloguing implementation bottlenecks: slow vetting, circular funding problems, and platform resistance. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] reframes this friction not as regulatory failure but as evidence the law is finally constraining real power—a "moving targets, moving politics" argument for staying the course rather than retreating. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys the resulting tool landscape directly. Several papers interrogate the regulation's conceptual foundations: [[Annabell2026-ev]] shows "data" itself is an undefined floating signifier that platforms strategically narrow to aggregated, quantified datasets in their mandated catalogs, while [[Peters2026-mo]] finds "data quality" similarly absent from official DSA text yet contested by academic/NGO stakeholders who partially won inclusion in the Delegated Act's recitals—with Meta notably inverting researchers' own arguments to claim access would be futile. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] extends DSA analysis to Article 25's dark-patterns provisions, mapping design taxonomies onto autonomy-violation categories and applying the framework to the live X blue-checkmark case. [[Schulte2026-df]] grounds these abstract debates empirically, using the 2025 German election to show that constrained, asymmetric access across X and TikTok directly shapes which electoral dynamics can even be studied. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes for a conceptual reorientation beyond causal-effects paradigms toward platform behavior as democratic infrastructure, a framing [[Crosset2026-mq]] complements from a security-governance angle, showing how EU/US legislation converges on "bureaucratisation" (transparency, auditing, reporting) regardless of underlying political philosophy.

## Industry Capture of the Evidence Base

A third strand interrogates whether research conducted under these access regimes can be trusted at all. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] are the anchor texts here, arguing—drawing explicit analogy to tobacco and fossil-fuel science—that tech industry influence over high-profile social-media research is pervasive, concentrated, and systematically undisclosed (roughly 80% "industrial saturation" once authors, editors, and reviewers are combined). [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates this within a broader "research on research" agenda, while [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers a focused meta-scientific critique of the Meta2020 partnership itself, arguing that one-off collaborations lack temporal and poetic validity and that platforms' rapid evolution makes reactive social science structurally inadequate—implying proactive regulatory disclosure mandates are necessary. [[Allen2025-ot]] responds by showcasing platform-independent experimentation (LLM-based feed reranking via browser extension) as a methodological escape route from platform gatekeeping. Methodologically, [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] demonstrate how even "generous" platform-provided datasets (Social Science One's URL Shares data) carry hidden artifacts—the 100-share censoring threshold inflates apparent fake-news prevalence by ~4x and masks a platform-wide algorithmic force behind seemingly local cross-country trends—cautionary tales for any research built atop platform-curated data. [[Park2026-tr]] rounds out this cluster by documenting the chilling legal risks (CFAA, CMA) researchers face when official access fails and they turn to scraping, underscoring that data-access precarity has real personal and professional costs.

## Content Moderation, Visibility, and the Politics of Reduction

Running alongside data-access debates is substantive scrutiny of what platforms actually do with content once access permits study. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] provides the conceptual keystone: "reduction" (demotion without removal) is a pervasive, under-theorized form of moderation that concentrates curatorial power while evading transparency and measurement entirely. This frame illuminates the empirical core of the topic—Meta's political content reduction policy. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses the DSA-enabled Meta Content Library itself to detect the policy's effects on Italian parliamentarians, finding a 72% reach decline that began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout, a transparency deficit Meta never disclosed, and an asymmetric rebound where extremist accounts compensated via posting volume. [[Rogers2026-zz]] complements this with a longitudinal replication of Craig Silverman's "fake news" methodology across 2016–2024, tracing how Meta's redefinitions (fake news → false news → misinformation → borderline) progressively depoliticized and narrowed the moderation problem, even as borderline content that does perform well remains almost exclusively right-wing. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] shows algorithmic governance is dynamic rather than stable background, with partisan-reach penalties and quality rewards fluctuating sharply around known interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures. [[McNally2025-dn]] makes a parallel "black box is not opaque" argument, detecting Facebook's News Feed ranking changes through lagged engagement effects on Guardian content, explicitly nominating DSA Article 40 as the legal basis for systemic algorithmic audits. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] remains the benchmark empirical finding that Facebook's ecosystem is asymmetrically segregated, with misinformation concentrated in a homogeneously conservative corner—a result in tension with Meta's self-presented moderation narrative as examined discursively in [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]], both showing how platforms craft legitimizing language ("coordinated inauthentic behavior," "changecraft") that externalizes blame and stages change as continuity.

The endgame of US content moderation—Meta's 2025 abandonment of third-party fact-checking for Community Notes—generates its own sub-literature. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] surveys fact-checkers' own assessment, finding Zuckerberg's censorship framing empirically unfounded (removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases) while fact-checkers express skepticism toward Community Notes' vulnerability to coordinated manipulation. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines the rhetorical self-defense fact-checkers mount to justify continued platform funding despite these pressures. [[Holt2026-zq]] empirically tests whether user reports could substitute for professional verification, finding reported links reflect political disagreement rather than factual falsity. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] and [[Miller2026-cp]] turn directly to Community Notes' mechanics: the former shows its bridging-based design structurally undermoderates polarizing content across 13 countries with electoral-integrity implications, while the latter (an industry-adjacent account) documents how AI "Note Writers" now generate a majority of helpful notes while nominally preserving human rating authority.

## Algorithmic Power, Advertising, and Structural Platform Critique

A final set of papers extends beyond moderation narrowly construed to the broader architecture of algorithmic curation and platform power. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] documents how X's recommender system rewarded "agitating" content and centralized visibility around Elon Musk himself post-acquisition, independent of political leaning per se. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] shows X's recommender inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear representation of users' ideological position, raising GDPR-adjacent profiling concerns. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] examine political-advertising delivery and independent auditing (Brazil 2018) respectively, both pointing to opacity in targeted ad systems as a persistent governance gap, a concern [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] had already flagged structurally in its account of micro-targeting's methodological challenges for political communication research. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] use DSA transparency mandates themselves as audit data, finding systematic linguistic moderation disparities (Global South languages chronically under-resourced) and inconsistent algorithmic promotion of "authoritative" sources around EU elections. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] extend scrutiny to substantive harm domains—disinformation prevalence (TikTok highest, rising) and climate misinformation governance (rarely formalized, dominated by "soft" interventions and governance-washing rhetoric). [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] broadens the frame further still to deepfake-enabled financial fraud, arguing for gatekeeper liability rather than victim-focused regulation. Undergirding all of this is the foundational theoretical work of [[Gillespie2010-as]] and [[van-Dijck2018-up]], whose accounts of "platform" as strategic, depoliticizing rhetoric and of the "platform society" as a contested terrain between private and public value remain the conceptual scaffolding against which every contemporary governance intervention—DSA, Community Notes, content reduction, API closure—is implicitly judged. [[Allcott2019-gn]] offers an early empirical data point in this longer arc, showing Facebook/Twitter misinformation diffusion trends diverging post-2016, a result now revisited and complicated by the post-API, post-fact-checking landscape mapped across this entire collection.
