# Platform Data Access & Governance

## The Shape of an Emerging Field

Platform data access has become, over the last decade, less a technical footnote to social media research than its central methodological and political problem. The papers gathered here trace an arc: from an early "Wild West" of loosely governed APIs, through the traumatic contraction Bruns christened the "APIcalypse" [[Bruns2019-nr]] and Freelon named the "post-API age" [[Freelon2018-ao]], to the current, still-unsettled moment in which the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA) attempts to re-found data access as a legal right rather than a platform favor. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes this history most fully—prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation—showing that today's four "official" access regimes (laissez-faire API, academic API, walled garden, pay-to-play) are themselves unstable equilibria. Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] eulogize the specific collapse of Twitter's open API, the latter quantifying a 13% drop in Twitter-based publications in 2024 and framing API closure as an exercise of platform power with measurable epistemic cost. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review corroborates this at the level of the whole field: the share of social-science papers using social media data tripled from 2010 to the early 2020s, then plateaued and declined from 2022-2024, a pattern too widespread across disciplines to be coincidental. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] synthesizes this trajectory as a movement "from the Wild West to the Walled Garden," arguing that each phase—data philanthropy, academic APIs, commercial paywalls—was reversible by a change in platform ownership or strategy, and that the DSA marks an attempt to convert access from discretionary gift to enforceable obligation, however unevenly implemented.

## The DSA as Regulatory Experiment

A cluster of papers treats Article 40 of the DSA as the pivotal, still-live experiment in converting platform transparency from voluntary gesture to legal mandate. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frame the current turbulence—platform resistance, procedural friction, slow vetting—not as evidence of regulatory failure but as proof that binding law is finally constraining entrenched platform power; de Vreese and Tromble's "moving targets, moving politics" argues explicitly against retreat from hard-won frameworks. [[Pierri2026-ib]], written from inside EU policy discussions on the Article 40 Delegated Act, catalogs concrete implementation bottlenecks: misaligned incentives between researchers, regulators, and platforms; a "circular problem of resources" whereby data access is needed to win grants that are needed to afford data access; and the looming blind spot of large language models as unregulated systemic-risk vectors. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows how even a seemingly technical concept—data quality—became a site of political contestation during the Delegated Regulation's consultation process, with academics and NGOs pushing (partially successfully) to enshrine quality standards that platforms, especially Meta, sought to invert as an argument against access itself. [[Schulte2026-df]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], and [[Lukito2026-nb]] apply this regulatory lens to election research specifically, with Lukito's "price, proficiency, or permission" framework cataloging 72 access tools and showing how platform-permission-based access remains inherently ephemeral—"independence by permission"—while consumption-side data remains starkly underprovided relative to production-side data. [[Crosset2026-mq]] widens the aperture, situating the DSA alongside US state laws and Germany's NetzDG as one of three competing "circulation regimes" (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation) that converge on a shared logic of security bureaucratisation—transparency as a distributed governance apparatus rather than mere accountability.

## Auditing the Infrastructure Itself

A second cluster turns empirical scrutiny onto the very tools the DSA and platforms provide, finding them wanting. [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s audit of YouTube's Data API documents a "forgetful by design" search endpoint whose temporal decay and inconsistent ranking undermine any claim to serve DSA-mandated systemic-risk research. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained limitations despite its DSA-driven European rollout. [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's content moderation during EU elections, finding inconsistent application of publisher-context labels and vague removal communications, and argues for reconceiving "transparency" as "observability." [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA-mandated transparency data itself to expose systemic underinvestment in non-English, Global-South-language moderation. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this critique to the infrastructure of access itself: Meta's Content Library "clean room" model, while addressing genuine privacy concerns, structurally privileges quantitative, code-fluent, well-resourced Global North researchers over qualitative, mixed-methods, and Majority World scholarship—raising the pointed question of whether mandated access is genuinely *useful* access. [[Zheng2026-bi]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] approach the same problem from opposite ends: Zheng's TubeStats/TokStats build independent random-sampling infrastructure to escape platform-controlled denominators, while Cullen's "Seeing like an API" framework theorizes, via interviews with CrowdTangle users, how technical design and governance rules co-produce what researchers can even conceive of asking.

## Data Artifacts and the Limits of What's Measured

Even where access exists, the data itself carries hidden distortions that several papers dissect forensically. The Meta/Social Science One URL Shares Dataset—arguably the most heavily used industry-provided dataset in the field—receives sustained critical attention: [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] shows the 100-public-shares anonymization threshold produces spurious cross-country convergence driven by algorithmic ranking changes rather than genuine social signal; [[Allen2021-ai]] traces bias introduced by differential-privacy noise and the same censoring threshold; [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends the dataset's use comparatively to Germany, France, and Italy while flagging its US-skewed NewsGuard pairing as a limit on cross-national inference; and [[Holt2026-zq]] mines the same dataset to show user "False News" reports track polarized disagreement more than falsity, with direct implications for Meta's 2025 shift toward Community Notes. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], using the newer Meta Content Library, demonstrate that Facebook's algorithmic governance is neither neutral nor stable—reach penalties for partisan content and rewards for journalistic quality fluctuate sharply in step with unannounced platform interventions like 2020's "break the glass" measures, and Meta's political-content-reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' reach by 72% without corresponding disclosure in DSA transparency reports. Complementing this, [[McNally2025-dn]] shows Facebook's News Feed algorithm's effects on hard news can be statistically detected from public engagement data alone, challenging the "black box" framing and building an evidentiary case for Article 40(4) systemic audits. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] and [[Luhring2025-od]] extend the data-quality critique beyond platform-provided datasets to the auxiliary infrastructures researchers rely on—web-scraping methodology and NewsGuard trustworthiness ratings, respectively—showing that measurement error in each is systematic rather than random, and thus consequential for downstream misinformation research.

## Industry Entanglement and the Politics of Knowledge Production

A final, more institutionally reflexive cluster interrogates not just what data is available but who is allowed to produce authoritative knowledge from it, and under what conditions of dependency. [[Munger2025-cz]] and [[Freelon2020-yp]] offer parallel metascientific critiques of the Meta2020 election partnership, arguing that even the most methodologically ambitious platform-academic collaboration produced narrow, rapidly decaying estimates incapable of supporting generalizable claims—evidence, they argue, that reactive empirical social science is structurally inadequate to govern fast-changing platforms, and that proactive regulation is therefore necessary. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]], and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] escalate this concern into a systematic argument, drawing explicit analogies to tobacco, pharma, and fossil-fuel science: roughly half of high-profile social media research carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small recurring set of scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, with industry-tied work skewing toward user-blame topics (misinformation sharing) and away from platform-dynamics scrutiny. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines the analogous dependency of fact-checking organizations on platform funding, finding fact-checkers deploy rhetorical strategies of differentiation and transcendence to defend their legitimacy while acknowledging the risk. [[Allen2025-ot]] considers a methodological response to these dependencies—platform-independent browser-extension experiments—as a viable alternative when platform cooperation is unavailable, while [[Park2026-tr]] documents the chilling legal risks (under laws like the CFAA) that independent, non-collaborative researchers face precisely because they operate outside platform sanction. Together with conceptual interventions like [[Gillespie2022-jx]] on reduction as an under-studied moderation technique, [[Bechmann2026-dr]] on platform behavior as democratic infrastructure, [[Helmond2026-ll]] on platformization's AI-era mutation, and earlier scene-setting work like [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]], this cluster makes the throughline of the whole topic explicit: data access is never merely a technical question of API availability but a governance question about who gets to know things about platforms, on what terms, and in whose interest—precisely the terrain that Article 40 and its expert apparatus (ECAT, the JRC) were built to contest. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]]'s survey of deepfake-fraud regulation and [[Ahuja2025-ku]]'s dark-patterns/DSA Article 25 framework round out the picture by showing how the same regulatory logic—shifting accountability from users to platform-gatekeepers—extends beyond data access narrowly construed into adjacent domains of platform harm and design governance, and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] offers a snapshot inventory of the tools landscape this whole body of work is racing to keep pace with.
