# Platform Data Access & Governance

## The arc from open APIs to regulated access

A dominant narrative running through this collection traces a historical arc: from the "Wild West" of largely unrestricted APIs, through a brief "Golden Age" of academic-tier access, to the current era of commercialized paywalls and regulator-mandated but heavily constrained data pipelines. [[Freelon2024-sc]] offers the canonical periodization — API prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation — and argues that access is now permanently contingent on platform decisions rather than settled infrastructure. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] extends this into a "Wild West to Walled Garden" framing specifically for Twitter/X, showing how voluntary "data philanthropy" (Data Grants, the Academic API) gave way to Musk-era pay-to-play pricing, and arguing that the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA) represents an attempt to convert access from platform discretion into legal right. Bastos2025-ya eulogizes the same trajectory from within "Twitter studies," while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies its consequences bibliometrically: a median 25% annual growth in Twitter-based scholarship through 2022, stagnating in 2023 and declining 13% in 2024 after Enterprise API pricing hit $42,000/month. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review of social-science publishing corroborates this at a broader scale, showing that empirical social-media research tripled across disciplines before plateauing and declining in 2022–2024 — a shift concentrated in Twitter/X and Facebook, the two platforms most affected by the closures these papers document. Together these pieces establish the empirical baseline against which the DSA's promise of researcher access must be judged, while [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] and [[Helmond2026-ll]] supply longer-range conceptual framing — the former on how big data and micro-targeting reshaped political-communication methodology even before the API closures, the latter on how "platformization" itself is now shifting toward an AI-centered infrastructural regime that may render API-based access frameworks increasingly moot.

## The DSA as regulatory turn: Article 40 in practice

A cluster of papers assesses what Article 40 of the DSA actually changes. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] examine the mechanism in the context of political-communication and election research, cataloguing new APIs and portals and noting their uneven scope and eligibility rules. [[Pierri2026-ib]], written from direct involvement in the Delegated Act negotiations, is more explicitly programmatic: it frames Article 40 as a milestone that nonetheless faces legal, technical, and organizational bottlenecks — a "circular problem of resources" in which data access is needed to win grants that are needed to fund the labor of requesting data access — and flags the emerging blind spot of LLMs as systemic-risk-inducing intermediary services. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] takes the most explicitly normative stance, arguing that current turbulence (platform resistance, procedural friction) should be read not as regulatory failure but as evidence that binding regulation is finally constraining platform power, and warning against abandoning the framework at the first sign of friction. [[Peters2026-mo]] complicates this optimism by showing, through a mixed-methods analysis of consultation feedback, that "data quality" was almost entirely absent from official EU documents and had to be forced into the Delegated Regulation by academic and NGO advocacy against platform resistance (Meta notably inverted the argument, claiming its own data was too poor-quality for access to be worthwhile). [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] survey the resulting landscape of tools more empirically: Lukito's audit of 72 access programs finds production data vastly outweighs consumption data (66 vs. 6 tools) and identifies "price, proficiency, or permission" as the three structural barriers that continue to exclude non-Western and under-resourced researchers even under a DSA-mandated regime.

## Auditing the new infrastructure

Several papers move from policy analysis to direct empirical audit of what DSA-mandated or platform-provided tools actually deliver, and the verdict is consistently sobering. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API still riddled with limitations "without explanations." [[Rieder2025-ju]] conducts the most granular technical audit, showing YouTube's Data API search endpoint to be "forgetful by design" — prioritizing freshness over completeness, with findable videos dropping sharply after 20–60 days, undermining any claim that the API can support systemic-risk research under the DSA. [[Jurg2025-ur]] complements this with a substantive audit of YouTube's content moderation during the 2024 EU elections, finding inconsistent application of "authoritative source" labels and vague removal communications, and explicitly calls for expanded researcher access via the YouTube Research Program. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data itself as a research object, finding systematic underinvestment in moderator allocation for Global South languages across six platforms — evidence that transparency reporting, even where mandated, remains too coarse for independent verification. On the Meta side, [[Cullen2026-cb]] retrospectively theorizes CrowdTangle through a "seeing like an API" framework, showing how its technical design and governance rules constrained researchers' very capacity to formulate questions, while [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this critique to CrowdTangle's DSA-era successor, the Meta Content Library, arguing that "clean room" environments — while addressing legitimate privacy concerns — structurally privilege quantitative, code-based, Global-North research and foreclose qualitative and cross-platform work. [[Zheng2026-bi]] offers a rare constructive counterpoint, describing TubeStats and TokStats as independently built random-sampling infrastructures that supply defensible denominators absent from any platform-sanctioned API, explicitly framed as unpermissioned but ethically necessary research infrastructure in the "post-API age." [[Schulte2026-df]] arrives at a parallel diagnosis from an application-driven angle, arguing via a 2025 German-election case study that the central bottleneck is not scholarly attention but the absence of reusable, standardized research infrastructure for multi-platform observability.

## The URL Shares Dataset as recurring case study

A tight sub-thread uses Meta's URL Shares Dataset (and its successor, the Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset / Meta Content Library) as a running methodological case study, illustrating both the promise and the artifacts of platform-provided data. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] identifies a striking cross-country synchrony in monthly URL trends that turns out to be an artifact of the dataset's 100-public-shares anonymization threshold interacting with feed-ranking changes, warning that platform-side data engineering choices can overwhelm the social signal researchers intend to measure. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] applies the dataset comparatively across Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising shares of untrustworthy URLs in election years but stable *views* of untrustworthy content — evidence, again, of platform-side dampening rather than pure audience behavior. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] pushes this further with the successor dataset, showing that Facebook's share-to-view amplification is dynamically modulated by partisan alignment and journalistic quality, and that this modulation fluctuates in lockstep with known governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures) — direct evidence against treating Facebook as a neutral conduit. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses the DSA-mandated Meta Content Library itself to detect an undisclosed political-content-reduction policy that suppressed Italian parliamentarians' reach by 72%, beginning ten months before Meta's announced rollout — simultaneously a substantive finding about platform governance and a methodological demonstration that DSA-enabled infrastructure, imperfect as it is, can support real oversight. [[Holt2026-zq]] closes this thread by using the same dataset lineage to interrogate Meta's 2025 shift toward Community-Notes-style user reporting, finding that user-flagged "False News" links skew toward polarized-but-not-strictly-false content, raising doubts about the epistemic reliability of the crowd-based moderation now replacing professional fact-checking.

## Industry influence and the politics of independence

A parallel line of inquiry treats platform control over data not just as a methodological constraint but as a structural conflict of interest. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] together mount the most systematic case: platforms' exclusive control of the data needed to study them creates dependency dynamics analogous to tobacco, pharma, and food-industry science, and empirically, roughly half of high-profile social-media research in top journals carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small recurring set of authors, editors, and reviewers, with industry-tied work skewing toward user-blame topics (misinformation sharing) and away from platform-dynamics research. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a companion critique of the Meta2020 partnership specifically, arguing that even the most methodologically ambitious platform-academic collaboration produced narrow, low-temporal-validity estimates that cannot outpace platform change, and concluding that reactive empirical social science is structurally inadequate — proactive regulatory mandates for platform self-disclosure are needed instead. [[Allen2025-ot]] responds from a more methodological angle, showcasing browser-extension-plus-LLM techniques as a "platform-independent" alternative that sidesteps the need for platform cooperation altogether. [[Farkas2026-lr]] extends the industry-entanglement theme to fact-checkers, showing how European fact-checking organizations rhetorically defend their platform funding (from Meta, Google, ByteDance) via differentiation and transcendence strategies that mirror journalism's traditional autonomy claims.

## Methodological consequences: what gets distorted, and how researchers respond

Several papers focus squarely on measurement validity given the imperfect data platforms make available. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that ex-situ web scraping (as opposed to in-situ tracking) introduces ~34% content disparity dominated by paywalls and login walls rather than by time delay, systematically biasing news-exposure research. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard, the trustworthiness-rating infrastructure underlying much of the URL Shares work above, finding continuous scores reasonably stable since 2022 but binary thresholds highly sensitive to a handful of borderline sources crossing a cutoff. [[McNally2025-dn]] pushes back on "black box" rhetoric around recommender systems, showing that Facebook's News Feed algorithm changes leave detectable, laggard fingerprints in engagement data — an argument for the feasibility of the systemic-risk audits Article 40 envisions. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] represents an earlier-generation methodological adaptation, using Facebook's ad platform itself (Pixel, conversion tracking) as a substitute recruitment infrastructure for hard-to-reach survey populations, prefiguring the donation- and ad-based workarounds now proliferating post-API-closure. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]], meanwhile, demonstrates via a data-donation study that X's recommender system inadvertently encodes users' political ideology in its embedding space — a finding with direct implications for DSA-mandated recommender-system audits and GDPR profiling concerns.

## The wider regulatory and legal field

Beyond the DSA proper, several papers situate platform data governance within broader legal and security frameworks. [[Crosset2026-mq]] compares EU and US content-moderation legislation as distinct "circulation regimes" (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation) that nonetheless converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" via transparency, auditing, and reporting mandates — a useful macro-frame for understanding *why* DSA-style transparency obligations take the administrative form they do. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] operationalizes DSA Article 25 (dark patterns) into a design-factor framework directly applicable to enforcement, including live proceedings against X. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extends platform-governance logic to AI-driven financial fraud, arguing for gatekeeper (rather than victim-focused) liability models. [[Park2026-tr]] documents the underappreciated legal risk researchers themselves face under computer-crime and IP law when conducting the very scraping, donation, and audit studies catalogued elsewhere in this collection, providing empirical grounding for why "unpermissioned" research methods ([[Zheng2026-bi]]) carry real professional and legal cost. Finally, [[Bechmann2026-dr]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] bookend the collection conceptually and historically: the former arguing that DSA-era research should move beyond narrow causal-effects paradigms toward treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure in its own right, the latter recalling that independent, volunteer-based auditing (of Facebook political ads in the 2018 Brazilian election) predates the DSA by years — a reminder that regulatory mandate is one route to observability, but not the only one researchers have historically pursued.
