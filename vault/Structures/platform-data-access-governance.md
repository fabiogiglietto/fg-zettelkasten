# Platform Data Access & Governance

## The arc: from open API to regulated access

The throughline across this topic is a historical arc, told and retold from different angles: an era of relatively open, ad hoc platform data access gave way to closure and precarity, which regulation is now attempting—only partially successfully—to convert into an enforceable right. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the hinge moment, arguing that Facebook's 2018 Pages API shutdown inaugurated a "post-API age" in which no amount of methodological investment in a platform's API can be trusted to survive corporate discretion. [[Bruns2019-nr]] radicalizes this into the "APIcalypse," reframing Cambridge-Analytica-era restrictions not as privacy protection but as a strategy to frustrate exactly the critical, public-interest research most threatening to platforms, with Social Science One as a case study in intransparent gatekeeping dressed as generosity. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] extend this periodization into the 2020s, cataloguing successive regimes (laissez-faire, academic API, walled garden, pay-to-play) across Facebook, Twitter/X, YouTube, Reddit, and TikTok, and showing that each "golden age" of access (Academic API, CrowdTangle) has proven reversible at the whim of ownership change or commercial strategy. The empirical costs of this precarity are quantified by [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] (a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024 following $42k/month API pricing), Bastos2025-ya (a eulogy for "Twitter studies" as a subfield), and [[Yang2026-tq]], whose systematic review of 2010–2024 social science publishing shows the share of social-media-based empirical research tripling and then plateauing/declining exactly when API restrictions bite—evidence that data-access policy, not researcher interest, has been the binding constraint on an entire mode of inquiry.

## The DSA as regulatory answer—and its friction

Against this backdrop, Article 40 of the EU Digital Services Act is treated across many papers as the field's central natural experiment in converting platform discretion into legal obligation. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frame current turbulence—platform stonewalling, procedural opacity, contested vetting—not as proof the regulation has failed but as evidence it is finally constraining an entrenched asymmetry of informational power; the correct posture, they argue, is to stay the course rather than retreat to voluntary self-regulation. [[Pierri2026-ib]], written from a position of direct involvement in the Article 40 Delegated Act process, is more clinical about the obstacles: misaligned incentives between researchers, platforms, and regulators, a "circular problem of resources" whereby data access is needed to win grants that are needed to secure data access, and platforms' capacity to use their own reach to frame transparency mandates as censorship. [[Lukito2026-nb]] operationalizes the same tension as "price, proficiency, or permission," cataloguing 72 access tools to show that platform-permission-based access remains inherently ephemeral ("independence by permission") and that consumption-side data is almost entirely absent from the landscape. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows how even a seemingly technical concept—data quality—became a site of stakeholder contestation during the Delegated Regulation's consultation, with platforms (notably Meta) inverting researchers' own arguments to claim access is futile because platform data is inherently poor quality. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] apply this regulatory lens to concrete research problems—election communication and multi-platform electoral discourse—arguing that observability itself should be treated as reusable research infrastructure rather than a one-off achievement, while [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys the resulting landscape of APIs and tools directly.

## Auditing the promise: what regulated access actually delivers

A cluster of papers empirically tests whether DSA-mandated or DSA-adjacent access mechanisms deliver on their promise, generally finding a gap between mandate and utility. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained inconsistencies; [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," with findable videos decaying sharply within 20–60 days post-publication in ways that undermine both historical research and DSA systemic-risk audits; [[Jurg2025-ur]] documents inconsistent application of YouTube's publisher-context labels and opaque removal notices during the 2024 EU elections, arguing for "observability" as the right normative target. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency reporting itself as data, revealing systematic underinvestment in Global South languages' moderation workforces across six platforms. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds to the representativeness gap by building independent random-sampling infrastructure (TubeStats/TokStats) precisely because neither YouTube nor TikTok's official tools can produce a defensible denominator. On the Meta side, [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] extend the critique from API to "clean room" infrastructure: CrowdTangle shaped what could be seen even before its shutdown, and its DSA-era successor, the Meta Content Library, embeds a philosophy that distrusts researchers, privileges quantitative code-based work, and widens resource gaps between well-funded and Majority-World teams—raising the point, echoed by [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], that mandating access is not the same as making it useful.

## The URL Shares Dataset lineage: artifacts, thresholds, and what platforms let researchers see

A tightly linked sub-thread traces the specific afterlife of Meta's URL Shares Dataset (the Social Science One/Condor lineage), treating it as both an unusually rich resource and a case study in how privacy engineering and platform governance distort findings. [[Guess2021-ym]] establishes the descriptive baseline of credible-versus-low-credibility news circulation; [[Allen2021-ai]] shows the dataset's 100-public-share censoring threshold—not differential-privacy noise—inflates apparent fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold relative to a representative panel; [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] finds the same threshold produces an uncanny cross-country convergence in trends, revealing a platform-wide algorithmic force (likely Feed/MSI ranking changes) that can swamp the very social signals researchers seek to measure. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] applies the dataset comparatively to Germany, France, and Italy, while [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] push furthest toward treating Facebook as an active curator whose "break the glass" interventions and undisclosed political-content-reduction policies—rather than stable background conditions—drive visibility, with the latter documenting a transparency failure (a policy affecting Italian MPs' reach that Meta never disclosed in DSA compliance reporting) alongside a qualified endorsement of the Meta Content Library as real, if imperfect, progress. [[Holt2026-zq]] extends this dataset into the post-fact-checking era, showing user "False News" reports track political disagreement more than falsity—directly relevant as Meta shifts toward Community Notes. Adjacent methodological cautions—[[Luhring2025-od]] on NewsGuard's stability and the distorting effect of binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs, and [[Ulloa2024-jm]] on how ex-situ web scraping (rather than time delay) is the dominant source of measurement error in news-exposure research—generalize the lesson: every platform-mediated or platform-adjacent dataset carries construction artifacts that must be modeled, not assumed away.

## Industry entanglement and the limits of platform-cooperative research

A further cluster interrogates not access mechanics but the structural dependence access creates. [[Heiss2026-qv]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] draw the tobacco/pharma/food analogy explicitly, arguing platforms' exclusive control of data, funding, and access creates opportunities for industry influence unmatched in other scientific domains; [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] operationalizes this empirically, finding roughly half of high-profile social-media research in top journals carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small recurring set of authors, editors, and reviewers, and skewed toward user-focused rather than platform-dynamics topics. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the sharpest single critique of the discipline's flagship cooperative project, the Meta 2020 election studies, arguing that one-off, heavily negotiated collaborations with rapidly changing platforms cannot achieve temporal or "poetic" validity, and that proactive regulation—platforms required to know and disclose their own effects—is the only viable alternative to reactive academic science. [[Allen2025-ot]] takes a more constructive methodological turn, situating Piccardi et al.'s LLM-based browser-extension reranking as a "platform-independent" experimental middle path precisely because platform cooperation has become so scarce. [[Farkas2026-lr]] rounds out this thread from the fact-checking side, showing how European fact-checkers rhetorically manage their own dependence on platform funding (mostly Meta) through apologia strategies that mirror journalism's traditional autonomy claims.

## Governance beyond data access: moderation, design, and platformization

Several papers widen the lens from data access narrowly construed to the broader regulatory apparatus now governing platforms, of which Article 40 is one instrument among several. [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates DSA transparency and auditing obligations within a comparative analysis of EU/US content-moderation law, arguing all regimes converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" that governs platforms as quasi-public infrastructures via reporting and audit rather than direct content control. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] extends DSA-adjacent regulatory reasoning to Article 25's dark-patterns provisions, building a law-to-design framework later applied to the Commission's case against X's paid checkmarks. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues the DSA era demands abandoning narrow causal-effects paradigms in favor of treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure in its own right. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] applies a parallel gatekeeper-liability logic to deepfake financial fraud, arguing regulation should target platforms and intermediaries rather than individual victims. [[Helmond2026-ll]] steps back furthest, arguing platformization itself is entering an AI-centered phase (Meta's abandonment of the Like button, Google's space-based AI infrastructure) that will reconfigure what "platform data" even means as behavioral inference supplants observable social-graph signals—a warning that the DSA's transparency architecture, built for the API era, may already be targeting a paradigm platforms are leaving behind. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] offers a technical complement here, showing X's recommender system inadvertently encodes users' political ideology in a linear embedding direction, which blurs the active/passive profiling distinction central to GDPR and DSA debates. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] provides earlier groundwork, identifying blurred organizational boundaries, microtargeting, and strategic amplification as the three big-data-era challenges that this whole regulatory apparatus was eventually built to address.

## Method, risk, and the researcher's position

Finally, a set of papers addresses the practical and legal conditions of doing this research at all. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] show early adaptive methods—ad-based recruitment, volunteer browser plugins—developed precisely because platform APIs were inadequate for survey and ad-transparency research respectively. [[McNally2025-dn]] demonstrates that algorithmic "black boxes" can be audited indirectly through longitudinal engagement time series, an approach explicitly framed as consonant with DSA Article 40(4)'s systemic-risk audit mandate. [[Park2026-tr]] closes the loop on the human cost of doing any of this work, documenting the chilling effects of overbroad computer-crime and IP law on researchers who must scrape, donate-collect, or otherwise route around platform-sanctioned access—a reminder that the DSA's legal channel is not the only, and often not even the primary, site of legal risk in this research program. Read together, these 48 papers trace a field moving from informal data philanthropy, through the APIcalypse and a decade of ad hoc workarounds, into a regulatory settlement that is real but fragile, unevenly implemented, and already straining against the next platform paradigm shift.
