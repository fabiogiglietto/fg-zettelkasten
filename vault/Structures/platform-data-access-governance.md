# Platform Data Access & Governance

## The Closing of the Open Web and the Rise of Regulated Access

The starting point for this literature is a historical rupture: the "post-API age" that Freelon first named in 2018 and that [[Freelon2024-sc]] now periodizes in full, tracing platform data access from prehistory through a brief "Golden Age" of academic APIs to today's fragmented regime of pay-to-play pricing, walled gardens, and unofficial workarounds. Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] write eulogies for the specific case of Twitter, with Murtfeldt's bibliometric analysis showing a 13% drop in Twitter-based scholarship in 2024 after two decades of exponential growth — hard evidence that API closures translate into measurable losses of empirical capacity. [[Yang2026-tq]] generalizes this pattern across the social sciences: the share of empirical papers using social media data tripled between 2010 and 2022 before plateauing and declining in 2022–2024, with Twitter/X and Facebook's dominance as data sources revealed to be an artifact of access rather than actual platform popularity. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] frames this arc as a movement "from the Wild West to the Walled Garden," arguing that platform-led data philanthropy (Social Science One, CrowdTangle, Data Grants) was always structurally fragile — a form of "independence by permission" (echoed in [[Pierri2025-hm]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]]) that a single ownership change (Musk's Twitter) or corporate decision (Meta's CrowdTangle shutdown, chronicled by [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]]) could dismantle overnight.

## The DSA as Regulatory Counter-Move — and Its Frictions

Against this backdrop, the EU's Digital Services Act, and specifically Article 40, emerges across many papers as the field's central hope for a durable, rights-based alternative to platform discretion. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] all treat Article 40 as a genuine paradigm shift — transforming data access from voluntary largesse into a legal entitlement — while insisting that current turbulence (platform stalling, ambiguous procedures, under-resourced Digital Services Coordinators) reflects growing pains rather than failure. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] is explicit that this friction is evidence the regulation is "targeting the right asymmetries of informational power," a framing shared by [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and [[Schulte2026-df]], the latter proposing that platform observability itself be treated as reusable research infrastructure. Yet the empirical papers filed alongside these more hopeful assessments complicate the story considerably. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that "data quality" — central to whether DSA access is actually usable — was nearly absent from official EU texts and had to be forced in by academic and NGO stakeholders against platform resistance (Meta notably inverted the argument, claiming poor data quality made access pointless). [[Pierri2025-hm]] documents a "circular resource problem" disadvantaging early-career researchers and identifies large language models as an emerging regulatory blind spot. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes further still, arguing the field should move past narrow causal-effects paradigms toward conceptualizing platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure — a conceptual reframing of what DSA-era research should even be for.

## Auditing the New Tools: Compliance Without Usability

A cluster of hands-on audits tests whether DSA-mandated tools deliver on their promise, and the consistent verdict is that formal compliance masks substantive inadequacy. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained limitations; [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," with findable videos decaying sharply within 20–60 days post-publication in ways unrelated to actual deletion — a serious problem for any DSA-mandated systemic-risk research relying on retrospective search. [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's moderation claims around the 2024 EU elections and finds publisher-context labels applied inconsistently across languages and broadcasters, while removal statements remain deliberately vague. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency reports themselves to expose stark linguistic inequities in human moderator allocation, with Global South languages receiving as little as 7.5% of English's moderator-to-content ratio on X. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] turn the critical lens on the "clean room" model exemplified by the Meta Content Library, arguing that its architecture — mandatory monthly data deletion, code-only Jupyter environments, no qualitative tools — structurally privileges well-resourced, code-fluent, Global North researchers and forecloses whole methodological traditions, even as it nominally satisfies legal access mandates. [[Lukito2026-nb]] synthesizes this into a "price, proficiency, or permission" framework across 72 access tools, finding consumption-side data almost entirely absent and platform-permission programs inherently ephemeral. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds pragmatically by building independent, unpermissioned sampling tools (TubeStats, TokStats) precisely because sanctioned APIs cannot produce representative samples — an infrastructural DIY response to the same problem [[Ulloa2024-jm]] identifies from another angle, showing that even web-scraping itself introduces systematic, non-random distortions in what "content exposure" appears to mean.

## Structural Dependency and Industry Influence

A parallel body of metascientific work argues that data-access asymmetry is not merely inconvenient but corrupting. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] extend the "merchants of doubt" framework from tobacco and pharma to platform research, documenting that roughly half of high-profile social media studies have undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small recurring set of scholars who also dominate editorial gatekeeping — yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the literature. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this as a problem unique to platform studies among the sciences: unlike food or pharma, there is no independent way to generate the underlying data at all. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers perhaps the sharpest single critique, arguing that even the most ambitious platform-cooperative project — the 2020 Meta election studies — was structurally incapable of producing durable knowledge, since platforms evolve too fast for one-off collaborations to have temporal validity, and concludes that proactive regulation compelling platforms to characterize their own effects is the only viable path forward. [[Allen2025-ot]] responds constructively by highlighting "platform-independent" experimental designs (browser-extension LLM reranking) as a promising middle path between full platform cooperation and pure lab artificiality. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] exemplify this independent-audit tradition empirically, respectively reverse-engineering X's recommender embeddings to reveal inadvertent political profiling, and showing that Community Notes structurally undermoderates polarizing content by design — both findings obtained without platform cooperation, underscoring why such independent capacity matters.

## Case Studies of Platform Discretion in Action

Several papers use whatever access remains to document specific instances of platforms exercising unaccountable discretion over political visibility, giving empirical teeth to the access debates above. The Political Content Reduction Policy is the paradigmatic case: [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] shows, using the Meta Content Library, that this policy suppressed Italian MPs' Facebook reach by 72% and began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout — while extremist accounts compensated by posting more frequently, actually gaining aggregate reach, and Meta never disclosed the policy in its DSA transparency filings. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends this temporal-sensitivity approach to the US, showing that Facebook's shares-to-views amplification function shifts sharply around known governance interventions like 2020's "break the glass" measures, evidence that the platform is an active curator rather than neutral conduit. The 100-public-shares threshold underlying much of this URL-based research is itself scrutinized by [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], who shows that near-identical monthly trend curves across 46 countries reflect a platform-wide algorithmic force (likely Meaningful Social Interactions optimization) rather than genuine local dynamics — a methodological warning later heeded by [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] in its European extension of Guess et al.'s newsfeed-quality work, and by [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]], which finds untrustworthy-source exposure on Facebook/Instagram in 2020 low on average but concentrated, with no detectable attitudinal effects of reduced exposure. Together these papers show how a single opaque platform parameter (a share threshold, an MSI weight, a "political content" classifier) can silently determine what researchers believe they are measuring. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] perform comparable algorithmic audits on X, the former via a large field experiment showing asymmetric, persistence-driven political effects of the "For You" feed, the latter documenting how Musk-era ranking rewarded "agitating" content and centralized visibility around Musk himself — both studies made possible only by creative, largely independent data collection rather than platform grants.

## Platforms as Discursive and Governance Actors

A final cluster examines how platforms narrate and manage their own moderation decisions rather than simply enact them. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows Meta's Newsroom systematically externalizes blame for problematic content onto "inauthentic" outside actors while erasing the role of its own affordances (e.g., Facebook Groups); [[De2026-ld]] generalizes this into the concept of "changecraft," the discursive management of platform change as continuous, aesthetic, and benevolent even amid substantive power consolidation. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] documents the collapse of Meta's Third-Party Fact-Checking Program and finds Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing empirically unfounded, while [[Farkas2026-lr]] shows European fact-checkers' own rhetorical labor to justify continued platform dependency. [[Donovan2025-ws]], [[Moran2025-qn]], and [[Crosset2026-mq]] step back to historicize and theorize this terrain: Donovan traces the invention of "misinformation-at-scale" as an artifact of engagement-driven business models; Moran documents the professional unraveling of trust-and-safety work amid political backlash; Crosset situates DSA-style transparency mandates within a broader logic of "security bureaucratisation" that governs platforms as quasi-public infrastructure without ever directly controlling content. Taken together with the broader Delphi-style horizon-scanning of [[Mahl2026-hc]] — which finds platform governance rated by international experts as the single most important lever for mitigating misinformation, ahead of individual-level literacy or fact-checking — this literature converges on a shared diagnosis: the central methodological problem of contemporary platform research is inseparable from the political problem of platform power, and neither the pre-DSA "Wild West" nor the current clean-room compliance regime has yet resolved it.
