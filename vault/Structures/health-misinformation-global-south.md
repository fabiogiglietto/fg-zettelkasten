# Health Misinformation in the Global South

## Mapping the Infodemic: From Detection to Causal Impact

This cluster of work, produced under the Media Ecosystems Analysis Group's Gates Foundation-funded agenda, traces an arc from *detecting* where and how problematic health content circulates to *quantifying* whether it actually matters for health outcomes. Although the empirical sites span Italy, the UK, and the US as much as India and Nigeria, the papers collectively build the methodological toolkit — information-void detection, coordinated-network mapping, nefarious-actor identification, and causal impact estimation — that the group's Global South mapping work draws upon.

### Locating the Gaps Where Misinformation Thrives

[[Scalco2026-bd]] opens the arc at the ecosystem level, asking not where misinformation *is* but where the conditions for it emerge. By formalizing "information voids" as measurable imbalances between demand (Wikipedia, Google Trends) and supply (Facebook, Twitter, GDELT), the paper shows that periods of informational scarcity — rather than being neutral — are systematically associated with a collapse in high-credibility content and a surge in unreliable sources. This supply-demand framing sets up a central question for the rest of the collection: misinformation does not simply "win" attention, it fills a vacuum left by failures of the legitimate information supply, often triggered by concrete events like vaccine authorizations.

[[Lyons2026-ca]] complements this macro-level diagnosis with a micro-level audit of actual exposure, using behavioral trace data rather than platform metadata. Its central finding — that low-credibility health content exposure is rare in aggregate but acutely concentrated among older, more conspiracist-minded, politically dubious-news-consuming users, and is driven by habitual cross-site referral rather than search or social media discovery — complicates any simple "void-fills-with-misinformation" story. It suggests that voids may not be filled indiscriminately across the population, but channeled toward specific audiences through pre-existing consumption habits, a nuance any India/Nigeria replication of void-detection methods would need to incorporate.

### Mapping Coordinated Networks and Nefarious Actors

The network-mapping strand is the methodological core of the group's "nefarious actor" detection program. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] documents the MINE-FACTS prototype, built on Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) detection via CooRnet, showing that content-agnostic behavioral signals can surface problematic Italian COVID-19 content at a substantially higher hit rate than routine fact-checking. Crucially, it catalogs the *tactics* of evasion — link laundering through reputable outlets, first-comment link placement, exploitation of religious and community groups — that any adaptation of this toolkit to Indian or Nigerian platforms would need to anticipate, since coordinated actors are shown to be adaptive rather than static targets.

[[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this network account by asking not just *who* coordinates but *whose voices* get amplified, identifying an "Alternative Influence Network" (particularly an Intellectual Dark Web cluster) whose COVID-skeptic content is disproportionately shareable, remediated through legacy media and journalist pages rather than circulating purely in fringe spaces. This insight — that hybridity between mainstream and alternative media, not isolation from it, drives problematic content amplification — echoes forward into [[Allen2024-av]]'s later finding that the most consequential vaccine-skeptical content came from *mainstream* outlets, not low-credibility fringe domains.

[[Song2025-yh]] broadens the comparative lens, showing that CLSB methodology is ideologically agnostic: both pro- and anti-vaccine communities coordinate, but with starkly different credibility profiles and national framings (UK anti-vaccine discourse emphasizing safety, US anti-vaccine discourse emphasizing freedom). This comparative, context-sensitive design is directly relevant to the India/Nigeria mapping work, since it demonstrates that coordination structures must be read against local political culture rather than treated as a universal signature of malicious intent.

### Polarization, Legitimacy, and the Limits of "Misinformation"

[[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] serves as a methodological caution within this collection: not all contested science circulation constitutes misinformation. Its analysis of UK newspapers' asymmetric citation of Great Barrington Declaration versus John Snow Memorandum signatories shows that mainstream, credentialed scientists can be sorted into politically polarized "bubbles" by media framing alone, without any of the hallmarks (fringe status, retraction, conspiratorial content) associated with the nefarious actors mapped elsewhere in this collection. This distinction matters for any coordinated-network methodology exported to new national contexts: density and partisanship of a cluster are not, by themselves, evidence of inauthenticity or harm.

### From Networks to Consequences: Does Any of This Matter?

The collection closes by confronting the hardest question — causal impact on health behavior and outcomes. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] and [[Allen2024-av]] arrive at this question from different angles and reach complementary, somewhat tension-filled conclusions. Bollenbacher's SIRVA epidemic model attributes a measurable, if modest, causal effect of antivaccine tweets on US vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths, using a compartmental model fit to geolocated Twitter exposure. Allen's survey-experiment-plus-crowdsourcing approach reaches a more skeptical verdict about fact-check-flagged misinformation specifically — its reach was too limited to matter much — while showing that *unflagged*, factually accurate but "vaccine-skeptical" content, often from mainstream news, had a far larger aggregate effect on hesitancy. Read together, these two papers suggest that the group's nefarious-actor detection and CLSB-mapping tools, optimized to find coordinated, low-credibility, fact-checkable falsehoods, may be systematically pointed at a smaller share of the problem than diffuse, legitimate-seeming, harm-implying content circulating through mainstream channels — a caution worth carrying into any extension of this toolkit to the health information ecosystems of India and Nigeria.
