# Information Disorder & Disinformation

## From discrete falsehoods to circulating narratives

A significant current within this collection pushes back against the dominant "true/false claim" paradigm that has organized disinformation research since 2016. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] opens this line by arguing, via second-order cybernetics, that attention should shift from the intent of content creators to the judgments of propagators who decide to share, reframing "fake news" cascades as emergent, observer-dependent phenomena rather than fixed objects. [[Marwick2026-qd]] radicalizes this move by treating disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *stories*—complete with characters, catchphrases, and moral arcs—that fact-checking and domain-blocklist methods are structurally unable to counter, a thesis echoed by [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]]'s survey evidence that cumulative, cross-outlet narrative exposure (not single claims) drives belief. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the missing theoretical apparatus, importing narratology and Ricoeurian hermeneutics to show how content can be referentially and ethically problematic—Manichean, monological, deceptively focalized—without being demonstrably false, using a pro-Kremlin Venezuelan influencer's NATO narrative as a test case. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] operationalize a cognate insight for U.S. election denial: rumors are less about fabricated facts than about the *frames* attached to (often accurate) evidence, visible in quote-tweet "call and response" dynamics and in the way 2022 audiences filled ambiguous events with the pre-built "deep story" of 2020 fraud. [[Goel2025-iq]] extends the argument methodologically, showing that mainstream, factually accurate articles are disproportionately co-shared with fake news to support the very narratives (anti-vax, voter fraud) that domain-based measures miss, while [[Waight2025-al]] builds an LLM pipeline explicitly to measure "narrative similarity" as distinct from lexical or topical overlap, revealing that low-quality (but not mainstream) U.S. outlets substantially echoed Russian "biolabs" claims. [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]] apply narrative-centric logics to practice: the former reframing "fake news" accusations themselves as a rhetorical weapon of strategic delegitimization in Italian politics, the latter proposing "argument-checking" and narrative monitoring as complements to claim-level debunking after Spain's 2024 Valencia floods. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] ties the cluster together conceptually, arguing that virus, warfare, and pollution metaphors for misinformation each illuminate only one level of a genuinely multi-level complex system, while [[Marwick2025-vx]] shows how far-right "redpilling" itself is less a sudden conversion than a processual narrative socialization built from cumulative "evidence."

## Measuring exposure: what the infodemic actually looks like

A second cluster—closely aligned with the EU-funded PROMPT monitoring tradition—asks how much problematic content people actually encounter. [[Vincent_undated-re]] reports repeated cross-platform European measurements showing TikTok's mis/disinformation share rising toward 25% and a persistent engagement advantage for low-credibility accounts. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s U.S. approach to Germany, France, and Italy, finding untrustworthy-URL shares spiking around national elections even as views from untrustworthy sources stay comparatively flat, with older cohorts dominating both exposure and sharing—a pattern [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] corroborates for Facebook/Instagram in the U.S. (low average exposure, heavily concentrated, no detectable attitudinal effects) and [[Lyons2026-ca]] replicates specifically for health content, where older, more conspiracist, politically dubious-news-consuming users drive a small but highly concentrated exposure tail sourced from other low-credibility sites rather than search or social referral. [[Nenno2025-xa]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] broaden the comparative lens across 24 countries and seven platforms respectively, showing that WEIRD-derived assumptions about news values and engagement patterns do not travel cleanly, while [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] uses billion-post diffusion trees to characterize the concentrated, actor-driven virality of misinformation on Facebook during the 2020 U.S. election. Methodologically, [[Luhring2025-od]] and [[Scalco2026-bd]] interrogate the infrastructure underlying such measurement itself: the former auditing NewsGuard's stability and showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs (rather than continuous scores) can distort cross-national findings, the latter proposing a demand–supply "information delta" to detect "information voids" that correlate empirically with reduced credible content and rising misinformation around COVID vaccine milestones. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] complicates the entire measurement enterprise from the audience side, showing via digital diaries that ordinary Australians flag mainstream/alternative news outlets—not fringe sources—as their dominant experience of "misinformation," driven by clickbait rather than topic (politics, health) alone.

## Coordination, bots, and the industrial infrastructure of manipulation

A distinct empirical program traces the organizational and network mechanics behind amplification. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] offers rare organizational detail on Russia's Internet Research Agency via leaked CVs, showing recruitment spiking with the Crimea annexation (not the 2016 U.S. election) and revealing a troll factory integrated into ordinary Russian media labor markets. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] documents parallel coordinated amplification among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus, while [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extends detection to the emerging decentralized platform Bluesky. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] and [[Marino2023-9137f448]] apply Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior methods to Italian COVID-skeptic Facebook networks, uncovering adaptive evasion tactics (link laundering, first-comment placement) and showing how "Alternative Influence Network" and "Intellectual Dark Web" figures achieve outsized share-driven engagement partly through remediation of legacy-media appearances. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] generalizes this into a "Deceptive Information Operations" framework spanning pro-Putin propaganda, gambling promotion, and adult-content networks, arguing that Meta's coordination-plus-inauthenticity categories are too narrow to capture the shared structural logic of a "Zombie Internet." [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] adds that such campaigns persist over time precisely by appropriating new emerging events, while [[Song2025-yh]] and [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] show coordination operating on the "legitimate" science-adjacent fringe: UK/US anti-vaccine networks and COVID-contrarian expert-amplification networks respectively, the latter demonstrating that coordinated retweeting elevates a narrow set of credentialed anti-consensus voices who then feed—rather than follow—subsequent low-trust news coverage.

## Health misinformation, vaccines, and journalistic contagion

Health-specific findings recur across the corpus's COVID lens. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] provides a causal, epidemiologically-modeled estimate that antivaccine tweets produced thousands of vaccine refusals and measurable excess cases and deaths in 2021 U.S. counties. [[Adam2026-tz]] complements this with panel-and-tracking evidence that alternative media exposure produces "contagion" of conspiracy belief while mainstream media exposure produces "mitigation"—except among populists, for whom even debunking backfires. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] turn the lens onto journalism itself: the former arguing that specific reporting strategies can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs, the latter showing UK newspapers' politically polarized selective citation of Great Barrington versus John Snow Memorandum scientists during COVID, evidencing scientific "bubbles" that mirror but do not equal misinformation. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] rounds out the health cluster by showing how Google News' algorithmic gatekeeping and Facebook engagement jointly privilege emotionally charged (celebrity, malpractice) health topics over medical-scientific ones, a dynamic with clear consequences for infodemic management.

## Elites, elections, and democratic erosion

The largest thematic vein concerns how disinformation reshapes democratic legitimacy through elite behavior and citizen belief. In Brazil, [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during the Manaus COVID collapse via derivative reporting, direct authority claims, or local brokerage; [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use panel data to show electoral misinformation beliefs corrosively increasing political intolerance and eroding trust in the 2022 election results, even as mainstream news use bolsters trust and messaging-app news use operates as an indirect misinformation-amplifying pathway; [[Ventura2025-sw]] adds a WhatsApp-deactivation field experiment underscoring the distinct informational role of encrypted messaging beyond public feeds. Cross-nationally, [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds across 26 countries' parliamentary tweets that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation sharing, but their *combination*—radical-right populism—does; [[Tai2026-qk]] similarly probes how institutional role and ideology check or enable U.S. officials' misinformation sharing. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a novel "hostile misinformation effect," a partisan-motivated perception that one's own side is disproportionately targeted by misinformation, moderated by political interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] and [[Iris2026-pg]] connect these dynamics to the 2024 European Parliament elections, the former linking far-right electoral performance to voter disinformation concern, the latter documenting systematic, structural media overexposure of Radical Right parties relative to their actual polling and electoral strength across five countries. [[Balluff2026-bv]] and [[Gaber2022-bk]] historicize elite media manipulation outside the platform frame entirely: Austria's "Inseratenaffäre" advertising-for-coverage scandal and the UK's Brexit/2019-election "strategic lying," respectively, both showing that disinformation dynamics long predate—and persist alongside—social media. [[Yoo2026-ev]] closes this cluster by showing how "fake news" itself becomes a weaponized, largely content-free rhetorical label in hyperpartisan U.S. outlets, reshaping Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance.

## Geopolitical propaganda and information warfare

A tightly linked set of papers examines state-aligned and transnational propaganda around Ukraine and the broader right-wing media ecosystem. [[Copland2025-em]] traces how Sky News Australia became a node bridging Australian and U.S. right-wing propaganda pipelines, with a bifurcated Facebook/YouTube strategy for domestic versus international conspiracist audiences. [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Bosch2024-hj]] examine, respectively, how the #IStandWithPutin campaign exploited platform "infrastructures of truth" and how TikTok's sonic affordances became an under-examined vector for Ukraine-war disinformation, complementing [[Sadler2025-vu]] and [[Waight2025-al]]'s narrative-level analyses of Russian framing strategies discussed above. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] and [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] provide the organizational backbone (IRA structure; Telegram coordination) underpinning such operations.

## Generative AI and synthetic media

A newer strand assesses how AI reshapes both the production and correction of disinformation. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds experimentally that visual disinformation—AI-generated stills or decontextualized video—outperforms text only for highly salient, footage-rich issues like the Ukraine war, complicating alarm about generative AI's unique persuasive power. [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies a "modality-congruent carryover effect" whereby difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes confidence and susceptibility toward subsequent same-modality fakes. [[Wack2026-bt]] shows, in a large Kenyan experiment, that deepfake impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated through visible peer commentary, with partisan motivated reasoning shaping whether audiences credit "credulous" or "incredulous" framing. [[Triedman2025-uy]] examines a structural case of AI-generated knowledge production—Grokipedia—finding it derivative of Wikipedia but citing far more low-quality and blacklisted sources, especially on controversial and political topics. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] evaluates LLMs themselves as fact-checkers, showing that curated retrieval context, not model scale or reasoning, is the decisive bottleneck for reliable political fact-checking, with search-enabled models also citing sources with a discernible left-leaning skew.

## Institutions of correction: fact-checkers, platforms, and moderation politics

Finally, a cluster examines the embattled institutions meant to counter disinformation. [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform funding partnerships against censorship accusations, while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically debunks Meta's 2025 framing of fact-checkers as censors, showing removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases and that fact-checkers themselves favor transparency and counter-speech over deletion, distrusting Community Notes as a replacement. [[Holt2026-zq]] and [[Renault2025-uh]] interrogate that Community Notes/user-reporting alternative directly: the former showing user "False News" reports track political disagreement more than falsity, the latter showing Republicans are flagged 2.3 times more often than Democrats even under the crowdsourced bridging algorithm, undercutting claims of anti-conservative fact-checker bias. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] performs discourse analysis on Meta's own Newsroom communications, showing systematic externalization of blame onto "inauthentic" outside actors while eliding platform affordances and hyperpartisan accounts. [[Xue2025-bp]] and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] probe under-examined equity and craft dimensions of correction—emotional framing in fact-checks, and stark linguistic disparities in human moderation staffing revealed by DSA transparency data, respectively. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes the whole arc, arguing that "misinformation-at-scale" is structurally endogenous to engagement-driven platform business models, such that the post-2021 retreat from trust-and-safety investment was less a policy failure than a return to platforms' underlying incentive structure. [[Rogers2026-cy]] offers a practical countermeasure from the EU's vera.ai project, mapping "post-truth spaces" for fact-checkers via network proximity to mainstream discourse, demonstrated on Moldovan Russia-Ukraine war discourse.

## Toward integrative and comparative synthesis

Several papers explicitly step back to assess the field itself. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts finds near-consensus that no single intervention suffices, with platform governance and journalism ecosystem reform rated above individual-level literacy or fact-checking—a judgment that resonates with [[Frischlich2025-vn]]'s complexity-systems critique of single-analogy thinking. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around correcting the field's WEIRD-centric bias, a concern echoed by [[Nenno2025-xa]]'s cross-24-country evidence and by this collection's relative dearth of Global South cases beyond [[Wack2026-bt]]'s Kenyan study—underscoring that health-misinformation mapping in contexts like India and Nigeria, flagged in this topic's original scope, remains an open frontier relative to the heavily North Atlantic and Brazilian empirical base assembled here. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]], finally, extends the disorder concept into consumer behavior, showing that influencer-sourced brand misinformation generates qualitatively distinct, more homogeneous toxicity than identical claims from ordinary users—a reminder that the source-and-network dynamics dissected throughout this literature extend well beyond the explicitly political and health domains that dominate the field's attention.
