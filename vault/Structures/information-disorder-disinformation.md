# Information Disorder & Disinformation Narratives

# Structure Note: Information Disorder & Disinformation Narratives

## From claims to stories: the narrative turn

A dominant thread running through this cluster is a deliberate move away from treating disinformation as a matter of discrete false claims, toward treating it as *narrative* — circulating, mutating, cross-platform story-work. [[Marwick2026-qd]] states the manifesto most directly: disinformation succeeds as identity-affirming, cathartic story, with recognizable characters, catchphrases, and moral arcs that travel across genres (satire, memes, late-night comedy) in ways that defeat domain-level and fact-checking countermeasures. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes this further into narrative theory proper, arguing that the true/false binary cannot capture content that is referentially and ethically suspect without being outright false, and proposing a hermeneutic-realist, narrative-ethics toolkit as an alternative. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] operationalize this shift empirically for U.S. election rumors: misleading content is produced less by false facts than by the interaction of (often accurate) evidence with partisan interpretive frames, visible in the "call and response" structure of quote-tweets where influencers supply evidence and audiences supply frames. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] extends the narrative frame to belief formation directly, showing that cumulative cross-outlet exposure to a narrative — not single claims — predicts belief. [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[Waight2025-al]] supply methodological infrastructure for this agenda: Goel shows that mainstream outlets' *true* content is routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives (evading source-list detection), while Waight develops an LLM-based pipeline to measure narrative similarity at scale, demonstrating that lexical/topical methods miss most of the diffusion between Russian state media and low-quality U.S. outlets. Zooming out, [[Frischlich2025-vn]] critiques the dominant metaphors (infodemic, information warfare, information pollution) for each obscuring parts of this multilevel, complex-adaptive dynamic, while [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Vincent_undated-re]] anchor the narrative-level story empirically in platform-exposure measurement, and [[Nenno2025-xa]] shows that the news-value structure of flagged misinformation (conflict above all) generalizes only weakly beyond WEIRD/US-Brazil contexts.

## Elites, parties, and the strategic production of disinformation

A second cluster treats disinformation as elite political strategy rather than viral accident. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds across 26 countries that neither populism nor right ideology alone predicts low-factuality sharing by parliamentarians — only their combination (radical-right populism) does, tying misinformation to a specific ideological formation rather than a generic platform pathology. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] shows the mechanism at a finer grain: intra-party factions (e.g., "CRT Republicans") build "connective faction" ties to hyperpartisan media that reward ideological extremity with engagement and coverage, radicalizing the party from within. [[Tai2026-qk]] examines institutional and ideological checks on elected officials' misinformation sharing, while [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents Brazilian parliamentarians waging narrative contests for epistemic authority during crisis, splitting into derivative-reporting, direct-authority, and brokerage modes. [[Mosca2026-yh]] reframes "fake news" accusations themselves as a rhetorical weapon of negative campaigning (the SDSA model), a claim echoed by [[Yoo2026-ev]], which shows both left- and right-wing U.S. alternative outlets weaponizing "fake news" as an empty delegitimizing label rather than a descriptor. State-level strategic production appears in [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s organizational history of the IRA troll factory (revealing it as a labor-market entity tied to Crimea rather than primarily the 2016 U.S. election), [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s dual-audience wartime propaganda model, [[Graham2025-gp]]'s account of #IStandWithPutin exploiting platform trust infrastructure, and [[Kim2026-br]]'s two-decade machine-learning analysis of Korean state-linked trolling, which finds moral condemnation — not praise — as the dominant, most-amplified rhetorical strategy. [[Copland2025-em]] traces how Sky News Australia became a node bridging Australian and U.S. right-wing propaganda ecosystems via a dual Facebook/YouTube strategy. [[Thiele2025-ol]] and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] contribute analytic/detection tools (a rational-choice attribution typology; real-time bot detection on Bluesky) for identifying the hidden principals behind such coordinated campaigns, complemented by [[Zhao2025-ny]]'s study of hashtag hijacking as a contest of attention, narrative, and bots, and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]]'s "Deceptive Information Operations" framework, which argues structurally similar operational logics (state propaganda, gambling promotion, unmoderated adult-content networks) underlie a "Zombie Internet" that existing CIB-style taxonomies cannot capture.

## Elections, trust, and democratic consequences

Several papers connect disinformation narratives directly to democratic erosion, especially around elections. The Brazilian pair [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use panel data to show that belief in electoral fraud misinformation increases political intolerance and decreases trust in results, with messaging apps (not social media broadly) as an indirect pathway — while mainstream news use, notably, bolsters trust even among Bolsonaro voters, positioning journalism as a resilience factor. [[Ventura2025-sw]] complements this by isolating WhatsApp's distinct causal role via a deactivation experiment. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows that mere media *narratives about* election interference (independent of actual interference) can itself polarize — asymmetrically, depending on which party is portrayed as beneficiary. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a "hostile misinformation effect" (parallel to the hostile media effect): citizens across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland believe their in-group is disproportionately targeted by misinformation, an effect amplified by political interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation, with downstream costs to media trust and perceived electoral integrity. At the level of media visibility, [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate radical-right coverage across five European election contexts, and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance to shifts in voter concern about disinformation — together suggesting a feedback loop between media attention, electoral success, and public perception of information disorder. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] offers a partial counter-case, finding pro-immigration content outweighs anti-immigration content on Canadian TikTok, complicating assumptions drawn from X/Twitter-centric studies, though flagging generative AI as an emerging vector for racist "speculative worldbuilding."

## Health, conspiracy, and belief formation

A substantial subset addresses COVID-19 and health misinformation as a proving ground for belief-formation theory. [[Adam2026-tz]] combines panel and web-tracking data to show both "contagion" (alternative media) and "mitigation" (mainstream media) effects on conspiracy belief, mediated by selective engagement/avoidance among populists and the politically mistrustful. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] causally links county-level antivaccine Twitter exposure to reduced vaccination and excess COVID cases/deaths via a novel epidemiological-causal model. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] traces how a small, non-automated but coordinated contrarian network amplifies credentialed anti-consensus experts, with news coverage typically following rather than leading superspreader activity. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] documents UK political polarization in which scientists are cited, showing this reflects strategic media use of legitimate scientific disagreement rather than fringe misinformation per se. [[Scalco2026-bd]] proposes and validates a method for detecting "information voids" — periods of demand-supply imbalance correlating with reduced credible content and rising misinformation — using COVID vaccine discourse as its testbed. On conspiracy specifically, [[Marwick2025-ov]] examines "ConspiracyTok" as populist knowledge production with a "generous epistemology" enabling both marginalized standpoint claims and disinformation, while its companion [[Marwick2025-vx]] reframes far-right "redpilling" as gradual socialization via disinformation-as-evidence rather than sudden conversion. [[Gardam2025-er]] proposes a visual-first methodology for Instagram climate-denial discourse, and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] examines how journalistic strategies themselves may inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs. Bridging domains, [[Spampatti2026-kx]] imports six methodological and political-economy lessons from climate psychology (behavioral outcome measurement, systems thinking, vested-interest resistance) into misinformation research, and [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts converges on a similar integrative conclusion: no silver bullet exists, and platform governance and journalism ecosystem reform outrank individual-level literacy/debunking interventions.

## Everyday misinformation and cross-platform measurement

Several papers push back against topic- and platform-narrow framings. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds Australians most often flag business/economics and celebrity content, mostly attributed to mainstream/alternative news (not fringe sources) via clickbait — a picture at odds with politics- and health-centric research agendas. [[Budak2024-ef]] argues public discourse has diverged from evidence on prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm, a corrective echoed by [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue introduction calling for de-WEIRDing the field. Cross-platform measurement papers — [[Mosleh2024-op]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Bosch2024-hj]] (on TikTok's distinctive use of *sound* for Ukraine-war disinformation), and [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] (on influencer-sourced misinformation driving distinct toxicity dynamics) — collectively argue that Twitter/Facebook-centric findings do not generalize. Methodologically, [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard itself, showing continuous scores are stable but binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can distort findings — a caution relevant to much of this literature's source-based measurement. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] offers an early theoretical reframing (still influential across this corpus): shifting attention from creator intent to propagator judgment via a second-order-cybernetics, micro/meso/macro model of circulation — a direct ancestor of the narrative-level, ecosystem-oriented approaches above. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] is an earlier case study of dissenting hashtag discourse (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie) illustrating discursive contestation within crisis communication, prefiguring the epistemic-authority contests seen later in [[Rodarte2026-dk]].

## Fact-checking, platform governance, and the crisis of correction

The final thread concerns institutional responses — fact-checking, platform moderation, and their contested legitimacy. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform-funding partnerships via differentiation and transcendence strategies, while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] shows empirically that Meta's fact-checking rarely resulted in content removal, undercutting Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing and revealing fact-checkers' own preference for labeling and counter-speech over deletion. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] offers a critical discourse analysis of Meta's Newsroom communications, showing how the company frames problematic content as originating "outside" itself while eliding platform affordances like Groups. [[Renault2025-uh]] provides empirical ammunition against the "biased fact-checker" narrative, finding Republicans flagged 2.3x more than Democrats even under crowdsourced Community Notes. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written by Maldita.es fact-checkers, proposes augmenting fact-checking with narrative monitoring and argumentation-theoretic "argument-checking," using the Valencia DANA flood conspiracy as a case study — directly operationalizing the narrative-turn insights from the first section. [[Xue2025-bp]] questions fact-checking's own claimed neutrality by showing emotional language pervades fact-checking posts themselves. Finally, generative AI reshapes this whole terrain: [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows LLMs fact-check poorly without curated retrieval context (and exhibit left-leaning citation bias even when successful); [[Dierickx2026-tw]] proposes "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category needed for GenAI outputs; [[Choi2026-bz]] documents modality-congruent carryover effects in deepfake authentication difficulty; [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds visual/AI disinformation is not universally more persuasive than text, contradicting alarmist assumptions; and [[Emilio2026-ik]] synthesizes these concerns into the "Generative AI Paradox" — as synthetic content proliferates, societies may rationally discount all digital evidence, raising an "epistemic tax" that no single mitigation tool (detection, watermarking, fact-checking) can resolve alone. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] closes the loop by noting that manipulation campaigns persist precisely by appropriating new emerging events — a fitting coda to a literature increasingly organized around adaptive, narrative-level, rather than claim-level, disinformation dynamics.
