# Information Disorder & Disinformation

## Contours of a Field: From "Fake News" to Information Disorder

This body of work traces a two-decade arc in which an initially narrow, moralized vocabulary — "fake news," hoaxes, hyperpartisan sites — has given way to a far more structural, systemic understanding of how false and misleading information moves through contemporary media environments. The conceptual anchor for much of this literature remains [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]]'s hybrid media system framework, which insists that older and newer media logics coexist, adapt, and compete for power rather than one simply displacing the other. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] extends this directly into the "fake news" debate, arguing against creator-intent-centric typologies and toward a propagation-centric, cybernetics-inflected model in which truth judgments are observer-dependent and cascades are emergent, composite phenomena. This hybrid-systems lens recurs throughout the corpus — in [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]]'s treatment of disinformation as recurring cross-outlet narratives, in [[Rodarte2026-dk]]'s account of parliamentarians contesting journalistic gatekeeping during crisis, and in [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]], whose structural diagnoses of an asymmetric, radicalized right-wing media ecosystem and a broader "disinformation order" remain foundational touchstones cited across nearly every later paper in this set.

### Measuring the Problem: Scale, Prevalence, and Contested Baselines

A persistent methodological throughline concerns just how big the misinformation problem actually is. [[Allen2020-nj]] and [[Vosoughi2018-at]] stake out opposite ends of a durable debate — the former finding fake news a vanishingly small fraction of the total media diet, the latter showing false political news spreads systematically farther and faster than truth on Twitter. This tension persists into the present: [[Budak2024-ef]] argues public discourse has drifted from the evidence on prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm, while [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds exposure to untrustworthy sources on Facebook/Instagram concentrated among a small minority with no detectable attitudinal effects. Counter-currents complicate this reassurance: [[Yang2023-cg]] shows that link-based measures dramatically undercount visual misinformation, and [[Goel2025-iq]] demonstrates that domain-level blocklists miss the arguably larger phenomenon of true mainstream content repurposed to support misleading narratives. Cross-platform comparative work — [[Vincent_undated-re]], [[Mosleh2024-op]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Guess2021-ym]] — extends the US-centric Facebook/Twitter baseline established by [[Allcott2019-gn]] and [[Lazer2018-mm]] into European and multi-platform contexts, consistently finding TikTok and older, more conservative users as loci of elevated risk. Underpinning much of this empirical work is the infrastructural question of measurement itself: [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of NewsGuard and [[Scalco2026-bd]]'s operationalization of "information voids" both argue that how we instrumentalize source-credibility and demand-supply imbalances shapes — and can distort — the conclusions research draws.

### Psychology, Narrative, and the Limits of the True/False Binary

A second major vein interrogates the cognitive and narrative mechanisms of belief. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] establish the canonical psychological account: poor truth discernment stems from inattention and failures of reflective reasoning rather than motivated partisan cognition, and corrections are sticky but exploitable via alternative-explanation and prebunking strategies — a logic field-tested at scale by [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]. Yet a growing counter-literature insists that reducing disinformation to true/false judgments about discrete claims is itself a category error. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reframes disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic narrative; [[Sadler2025-vu]] draws on narratology and Ricoeur to argue content can be referentially and ethically troubling without being straightforwardly false; and [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop evidence-frame and "deep storytelling" frameworks showing that election-rumor meaning is constructed collaboratively through quote-tweet framing dynamics rather than located in isolated posts. [[Waight2025-al]]'s LLM-based narrative-similarity methodology offers a technical bridge between these qualitative insights and scalable measurement, while [[Marwick2025-vx]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]] extend the narrative-identity thesis into radicalization and conspiracy communities, showing how "redpilling" and ConspiracyTok operate as processual, identity-laden socialization rather than one-shot exposure effects — a claim resonant with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review arguing radicalization is multi-causal and irreducibly hybrid (online/offline).

### Elites, Platforms, and the Political Economy of Falsehood

A substantial cluster treats disinformation as elite-driven political strategy rather than mass cognitive failure. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] finds partisan animus, not ignorance, drives fake-news sharing; [[Tornberg2025-ir]] shows radical-right populism specifically (not populism or right-ideology alone) predicts elite misinformation-sharing; and [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorizes "strategic lying" as an evolution of political spin exemplified by Brexit and the 2019 UK election. Studies of specific national contexts — [[Rodarte2026-dk]], [[Rossini2026-jn]], [[Rossini2026-mj]], [[Ventura2025-sw]] on Brazil; [[Balluff2026-bv]] on Austria's advertising-for-coverage scandal; [[Makeev2026-ma]] on Russian dual-audience propaganda; [[Copland2025-em]] on Sky News Australia's transnational propaganda role — collectively insist that disinformation cannot be separated from institutional incentives, media capture, and the attention economy. [[Donovan2025-ws]] and [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] turn this lens on the platforms themselves, arguing that "misinformation-at-scale" is a structural feature of engagement-driven business models and that Meta's own discourse strategically externalizes blame. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] generalizes this critique into a broader warning about industry capture of the research enterprise itself, a concern sharpened by [[Pierri2026-ib]]'s account of the DSA's Article 40 as both opportunity and battleground, and by [[Bruns2019-nr]]'s earlier chronicle of the post-Cambridge-Analytica "APIcalypse." [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces how Meta's own shifting terminology for "fake news," "false news," "misinformation," and finally "borderline content" has progressively depoliticized moderation even as right-wing content continues to disproportionately dominate high-engagement borderline material — a finding echoed in [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]]'s account of fact-checkers navigating Meta's post-2025 retreat from third-party verification, and in [[Holt2026-zq]] and [[Renault2025-uh]], both of which stress-test Community Notes as its replacement.

### Coordination, Attribution, and Computational Propaganda

A technically oriented cluster addresses the infrastructure of coordinated manipulation: [[Starbird2019-qv]] and [[Keller2019-nk]] established disinformation-as-collaborative-work and principal-agent attribution respectively, foundations built upon by [[Thiele2025-ol]]'s typology of coordinated social media manipulation, [[Di_Marco2026-xu]]'s frameworks for quantifying (not merely detecting) CIB influence, [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]]'s real-time Bluesky bot detection, and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]]'s "Deceptive Information Operations" concept spanning state propaganda, gambling networks, and poorly moderated adult-content groups under a shared "Zombie Internet" logic. [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s "post-truth spaces" methodology and [[Graham2025-gp]]'s account of #IStandWithPutin both show how propaganda parasitizes rather than evades platform trust infrastructure. [[Kim2026-wg]] and [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] push from exposure to causal, real-world consequence — geo-racial voter suppression and antivaccine-tweet-attributable deaths, respectively — demonstrating the stakes of treating coordination and diffusion as more than academic abstractions.

### Generative AI and the Frontier of Verification

The most recent wave confronts generative AI as both a new vector and a potential epistemic rupture. [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s "synthetic reality" framework and [[Dierickx2026-tw]]'s "emergent facts" category both argue that GenAI content resists existing evidentiary and epistemological categories. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] and [[Choi2026-bz]] offer more tempered experimental findings — low-tech decontextualized video, not AI imagery, often proves more persuasive, and modality-congruent authentication difficulty carries over across exposures. [[Wack2026-bt]] demonstrates that deepfake impact is itself socially negotiated through surrounding audience comments, while [[DeVerna2025-dl]] and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] turn to LLMs as verification tools, finding curated retrieval dramatically outperforms raw model knowledge, and that AI search intermediaries are trusted differently than AI-authored news. Waight2026-tz closes the loop by showing how state media control can be "laundered" into ostensibly neutral LLM outputs — a direct extension of the propaganda-infrastructure concerns raised by [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Benkler2018-lw]] into the generative-AI era.

### Toward Synthesis and Self-Reflection

A final meta-layer takes stock of the field itself. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study, [[Frischlich2025-vn]]'s critique of virus/warfare/pollution analogies, [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s borrowing from climate psychology, and [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue call to de-WEIRD-ify misinformation research all converge on a shared diagnosis: the field is fragmented, overly individualistic, Western-centric, and in need of integrative, multi-level, cross-national frameworks — a diagnosis also evident in [[Humprecht2020-gd]]'s earlier comparative resilience framework and [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s finding that depolarization recommendations routinely outrun their evidentiary base. [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Tsfati2020-uo]] remain essential bridge texts connecting the propaganda and misinformation traditions, while [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s long-run global trust data and [[Nenno2025-xa]]'s cross-cultural news-values analysis insist that any account of information disorder must be historicized and de-parochialized. Together, this cluster of papers models the self-correcting, reflexive stance the broader topic increasingly demands: not merely cataloguing disinformation's forms, but interrogating the adequacy of the concepts — fake news, misinformation, coordinated inauthentic behavior, borderline content — used to apprehend it.
