# Italian Election and News Media Coverage

## The Empirical Core: Coordination, Insularity, and Partisan Amplification

The founding empirical thread of this programme begins with two studies that establish Italian elections as a laboratory for studying how partisan and populist actors exploit the architecture of social platforms. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] shows that during the 2018 campaign, news sources aligned with the Five Star Movement and the League were markedly more "insular" on Twitter, and that this insularity correlated with distinct Facebook engagement signatures — insular sources generated more shares relative to comments (amplification), while cross-partisan sources became sites of contestation. Crucially, M5S emerges as uniquely adept at mobilizing its base to amplify favorable stories and swarm unfavorable ones, a pattern the paper ties to its identity as a long-invested "cyber party." This finding about strategic, coordinated attention-shaping sets up the next major methodological leap: [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] operationalizes "coordinated link sharing behavior" as a detectable signature of manipulation across both the 2018 general election and 2019 European election, showing that coordinated networks are dramatically more likely to spread problematic domains and include known disinformation actors. This paper reframes the field's object of study from content veracity to coordinated action itself — an ecological turn that shapes everything downstream. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends this lineage into 2022, building a live, self-updating workflow that surfaces new coordinated networks (hyperpartisan M5S clusters, clickbait-driven religious pages, and a proselytizing bot operation), demonstrating that static blacklists inevitably decay and that behavioral detection must be continuous.

Together these three papers trace an arc from *diagnosing* partisan insularity, to *operationalizing* coordination as a detectable behavior, to *industrializing* detection as an ongoing monitoring infrastructure — the empirical backbone from which the MINE datasets originate.

## Media Capture, Rhetorical Weaponization, and the Limits of "Bias"

A parallel strand examines how political power co-opts news coverage through more overtly institutional channels. [[Balluff2026-bv]], though set in Austria, extends the programme's comparative ambitions by using a difference-in-differences design to show how covert government-advertising arrangements can inflate a politician's visibility and steer competitor coverage negative — a "pay positive to go negative" dynamic that complicates simple notions of partisan favorability bias. This resonates with [[Mosca2026-yh]]'s theoretical move within the Italian press specifically: reframing "fake news" accusations not as symptoms of an information disorder but as rhetorical weapons in negative campaigning, selectively amplified by outlets according to political alignment. Read together, these two papers argue that both overt content and meta-discursive framing (accusing rivals of "fake news") are strategic instruments in an actor-driven media-capture ecology, rather than incidental artifacts of algorithmic circulation — a useful counterweight to the platform-centric coordination studies above.

## Measuring Exposure and Visibility at Scale

A third cluster shifts from *who coordinates* to *what audiences actually see*, using Meta's evolving data infrastructures to interrogate exposure and curation. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s US framework to Italy, France, and Germany, finding that the proportion of untrustworthy URLs shared rose sharply in Italy's 2022 election year even as the *share of views* going to untrustworthy sources stayed comparatively stable — suggesting some dampening mechanism between sharing and exposure. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], working on US data but methodologically foundational to the whole programme, dissects that share-to-view relationship directly, showing that partisan content is systematically penalized in reach while journalistically rigorous content is rewarded, and that these effects intensify around known platform governance interventions (e.g., 2020 "break the glass" measures) — evidence that Facebook actively curates rather than passively transmits. This finding directly motivates [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], which turns back to Italy to empirically trace Meta's 2021–2025 political content reduction policy through Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach, finding a 72% collapse in visibility for re-elected MPs, a policy rollout that preceded Meta's own announced timeline by nearly a year, and a striking asymmetry whereby extremist accounts compensated through increased posting volume rather than suffering equivalent losses. Together, [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] establish that the "newsfeed" documented by [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] is not a neutral aggregation of user behavior but an actively and unevenly governed space, with consequences for who gets to be seen during election cycles.

## Method-Building: From Manual Coding to LLM Pipelines

Underlying these empirical findings is a steady infrastructural and methodological evolution visible across the corpus's earliest and most recent entries. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], predating the platform-critical turn, offers a hybrid-media-systems account of the 2013 election's TV/Twitter second-screen dynamics, using traditional content analysis to show that participation remained narrow and largely reactive to broadcast logic — a baseline against which the later Facebook-centric, computationally-driven studies can be read as a marked expansion in both scale and analytic ambition. That expansion required new tools: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] rigorously benchmarks embedding models (OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large versus the Italian-tuned UmBERTo) for clustering Italian political news across the 2018 and 2022 datasets, finding the general-purpose LLM embedding consistently superior. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] builds on this to construct and validate a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline — political classification, embedding-based clustering, and narrative labeling — while confronting the genuinely novel validation problems these tools introduce (variable narrative granularity, the inadequacy of crowdsourced coders against LLMs). These two papers essentially document the toolchain that makes the large-scale URL and post-level analyses elsewhere in this topic possible.

## Data Access as a Precondition and a Politics

Finally, three companion pieces make explicit what the empirical papers presuppose: that all of this depends on contested, unstable access to platform data. [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys how the EU's Digital Services Act has reshaped (and unevenly reopened) API-based access following the CrowdTangle-era contraction, while [[Lukito2026-nb]] offers a broader taxonomy of the "price, proficiency, or permission" barriers structuring who can do this kind of research at all, explicitly citing CrowdTangle's 2024 shutdown as a cautionary emblem. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] itself doubles as a case study in this data-access politics, using the DSA-mandated Meta Content Library to reveal transparency gaps in Meta's own policy disclosures. Read as a coda to the topic, these pieces reframe the entire longitudinal programme — from 2013's second screens through 2022's coordinated networks and content-reduction policies — as itself contingent on a shifting, often adversarial relationship between researchers and the platforms whose infrastructures make Italian election research possible.
