# Platform Data Governance & Research Access

# Platform Data Governance & Research Access

## The closing of the "Golden Age" and its aftermath

The scholarship gathered here begins from a shared diagnosis: the era of relatively open platform APIs has ended, and empirical social science built its methodological house on that now-collapsed foundation. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies this loss directly, showing a 25% annual growth in Twitter-based research through 2022 followed by stagnation and a 13% decline by 2024 as the $42,000/month Enterprise API pricing took hold. Bastos2025-ya and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodize this trajectory as movement from an open "Wild West," through a brief "Golden Age" of academic APIs, into a "walled garden" of commercial paywalls—Giglietto and Puschmann arguing this pattern (Meta's CrowdTangle closure, Reddit's pricing, TikTok's restrictions) is structural rather than idiosyncratic to any one platform. [[Freelon2024-sc]] offers the most systematic periodization, dividing data-access history into five eras and classifying current regimes into four official types plus unofficial methods, while showing that platforms under greatest scrutiny (Meta, X) impose the tightest controls. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s bibliometric review corroborates this at the level of published social science output: usage of Twitter/X and Facebook data tripled over 2010–2024 before plateauing and declining precisely as API restrictions bit, with researchers only beginning to pivot toward TikTok, Instagram, and donation-based methods. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Bruns2026-pn]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] all treat this contraction as the backdrop against which the DSA's Article 40 must be judged—not as a return to the old openness, but as an attempt to build a legally mandated substitute infrastructure.

## Article 40 DSA: promise, procedure, and friction

A cluster of papers examines the DSA's Article 40 as the central regulatory answer to platform-controlled access. [[Pierri2025-hm]], drawing on an 18-month ERC-DG CONNECT pilot, catalogs implementation bottlenecks: misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, a "circular resource problem" disadvantaging early-career researchers, and the urgent regulatory blind spot of LLMs. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] assess what the regulation concretely changes for day-to-day and election research respectively, finding new APIs and portals of highly uneven scope and eligibility. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] documents the sharpest evidence of platform resistance—response times of 2 to over 100 days, boilerplate denials, and European Commission proceedings finding TikTok and Meta in breach of Article 40(12)—while [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frames this turbulence not as regulatory failure but as proof the law is finally constraining entrenched informational asymmetries, urging a "marathon" posture of sustained implementation rather than redesign. [[Peters2026-mo]] complicates the picture by showing that "data quality," central to whether Article 40 access is actually usable, was largely absent from official EU texts and had to be inserted into the Delegated Regulation through NGO and academic advocacy against platforms (notably Meta) that strategically inverted the argument. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes for a conceptual reframing in this same regulatory moment, arguing platform collective behavior should be studied as democratic infrastructure rather than through narrow causal-effects designs. Sector-specific audits—[[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] on TikTok's Research API, [[Rieder2025-ju]] on YouTube's "forgetful by design" search endpoint, [[Tonneau2025-bv]] on cross-lingual moderator allocation disparities—show that even where DSA-mandated tools exist, they are frequently inadequate for the systemic-risk research the law envisions. [[Lukito2026-nb]] synthesizes this into a "price, proficiency, or permission" framework across 72 tools, finding consumption data starkly underrepresented relative to production data and access infrastructure still WEIRD-centric despite DSA ambitions.

## Clean rooms and the politics of controlled access

A related strand interrogates not whether access exists but what kind of access it is. [[Bruns2026-pn]] gives the most detailed account of Meta Content Library's "clean room" model, arguing that its design—mandatory monthly data deletion, restrictive Jupyter-only environments, exclusion of qualitative tools—systematically privileges quantitative, code-fluent, well-resourced Global North teams. [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "seeing like an API" framework extends this critique retrospectively to CrowdTangle, showing through interviews how its technical design and informal knowledge networks shaped what researchers could even conceive of asking. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] catalogs the broader landscape of tools emerging in this DSA-conditioned environment. Together these pieces argue that mandating access (the DSA's achievement) is necessary but not sufficient; the manner of access determines its actual research value—a claim [[Bechmann2026-dr]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] echo at a more theoretical level.

## Meta's shifting datasets: URL Shares, thresholds, and political content

A distinct empirical lineage, largely from Giglietto and collaborators, uses Meta's own released datasets to interrogate the platform's governance choices as objects of study in their own right. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] identifies the 100-public-shares threshold on the URL Shares Dataset as introducing a platform-wide artifact—likely tied to Meaningful Social Interactions ranking changes—that can overwhelm the substantive signals researchers seek, a methodological warning later heeded by [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]]'s comparative Germany/France/Italy analysis of trustworthy versus untrustworthy URL exposure. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends this into a study of the successor Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset, showing that Facebook actively dampens the reach of highly partisan content and rewards journalistic quality in ways that fluctuate sharply with known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures—direct evidence against the "neutral conduit" framing of the newsfeed also challenged empirically by [[McNally2025-dn]]'s longitudinal Granger-causality analysis of Guardian content. Most pointedly, [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses the Meta Content Library itself to show that Meta's 2021–2025 political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' reach by 72% roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout—and that this consequential policy went undisclosed in DSA transparency reporting, even as extremist accounts compensated through sharply increased posting volume. This body of work exemplifies a recurring move in the corpus: using DSA-enabled infrastructure to hold the platform accountable for governance decisions the platform itself obscures.

## CrowdTangle's shutdown and the scramble for alternatives

The CrowdTangle shutdown recurs across the collection as a hinge event. [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] treat its closure (finalized August 2024, disrupting research on the eve of the US election) as the moment that crystallized the shift from platform-philanthropy to controlled clean-room access. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Copland2025-em]] represent the kind of CrowdTangle-dependent scholarship on political emotion and transnational propaganda networks that is now foreclosed by default, underscoring what is lost rather than merely inconvenienced. [[Lukito2026-nb]] situates CrowdTangle within Meta's broader cycling through four access regimes (Social Science One, CrowdTangle, FORT, Meta Content Library), each less generous than promised. [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats project, and [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s vera.ai-funded post-truth-space mapping, exemplify researcher-built infrastructure emerging specifically to fill gaps left by platform withdrawal.

## Industry influence, epistemic dependency, and the limits of collaboration

A sharply critical strand asks what independence means when platforms control both data and, often, funding. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] document empirically that roughly half of high-profile social media research has undisclosed industry ties, concentrated in a small elite of repeatedly-engaged authors and editors, with an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" once reviewers are included—paralleling tobacco- and pharma-industry influence patterns. [[Heiss2026-qv]] draws the same analogy to argue that DSA-style regulatory reform must be paired with stronger "research on research" norms. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific critique of the Meta2020 partnership is the sharpest single case study: even the best-resourced, most consensus-driven collaboration produced narrow, low-temporal-validity estimates and inconsistent operationalizations of core concepts like "echo chamber," leading Munger to conclude that reactive academic study of platforms is structurally inadequate and that proactive disclosure mandates are the only real solution—a conclusion [[Allen2025-ot]] partially tests by contrasting Meta2020's null findings with a platform-independent LLM-based reranking experiment on X. [[Farkas2026-lr]]'s interview study with European fact-checkers shows the same dependency dynamic from the other side: fact-checkers deploy rhetorical apologia to justify their financial reliance on Meta, Alphabet, and ByteDance while asserting editorial independence, revealing an emergent institution still negotiating its own boundaries.

## Content moderation, deplatforming, and the retreat from third-party fact-checking

Several papers examine specific moderation interventions and their real-world consequences. [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-audit accounts produced network-wide "ripple effects" disproportionate to the accounts removed, flipping the authority structure of the affected community. [[Rieder2026-pp]] finds YouTube's moderation apparatus structurally outpaced by its own recommendation system in the case of Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology," while [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's authoritative-source promotion during EU elections and finds inconsistent, language-gapped application of its publisher-context labels. [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the broader institutional erosion of the "trust and safety" profession itself amid political pressure and layoffs. The endpoint of this arc is Meta's 2025 abandonment of third-party fact-checking: [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] shows empirically that Zuckerberg's censorship framing is largely unfounded (removal occurred in only ~30% of verified cases) while fact-checkers express deep skepticism about Community Notes as a replacement; [[Holt2026-zq]] finds that user "False News" reports—the mechanism now expected to substitute for professional verification—largely reflect political disagreement about polarized topics rather than detection of falsity; and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows Community Notes' bridging-based design structurally undermoderates polarizing content by construction, with direct implications for electoral integrity as the system rolls out globally.

## Algorithmic effects, ideology, and platform power in the wild

A final cluster uses independent or quasi-independent methods to probe how platform algorithms shape political life absent full cooperation. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] use field-experimental and comparative-feed designs on X to show algorithmic curation causally shifts political attitudes rightward and privileges "agitating" content and proximity to Elon Musk over ideology per se. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] demonstrates X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a highly accurate linear representation of users' political ideology, raising profiling concerns under GDPR and DSA. [[Ventura2026-yc]] shows that even blunt state-level bans (Brazil's 2024 X ban) produce durable partisan "sorting ratchets." [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] analyze how platforms discursively manage their own accountability—Meta's Newsroom externalizing blame onto "inauthentic" actors, and platforms generally deploying "changecraft" rhetoric to naturalize policy shifts. Rounding out the collection, historical and infrastructural essays ([[Helmond2026-ll]], [[Boyd2026-op]]) and adjacent empirical work on electoral advertising, media capture, and coordinated inauthentic behavior ([[Votta2025-xz]], [[Balluff2026-bv]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], [[Oprea2025-lf]], [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]]) collectively reinforce the volume's central throughline: platform governance decisions are neither neutral nor static, and researchers' capacity to detect, contest, or even describe them remains hostage to access regimes that the DSA has begun—unevenly, contentiously—to reshape.
