# Computational Social Science & Digital Methods

## From Open API to Walled Garden: The Shifting Infrastructure of Social Media Research

The most consistent thread across this collection is the disappearance of the conditions that made "Twitter studies" possible in the first place. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes the past two decades into five distinct regimes — from laissez-faire APIs through the brief "academic cooperation" moment to today's fragmented pay-to-play landscape — and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] tells a parallel story of an "open Wild West" giving way to a "walled garden" after Musk's dismantling of the Academic API. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] provides the empirical eulogy: median 25% annual growth in Twitter-based research through 2022, followed by a 13% decline in 2024 that will likely deepen. Bastos2025-ya retrospectively frames Twitter as both research object and research infrastructure, whose rebranding constitutes a genuine epistemic rupture. [[Yang2026-tq]] tracks the aggregate effect across the broader landscape: a threefold rise in social-media-based social science followed by a plateau and decline coinciding precisely with API restrictions, with 82.6% of studies still tethered to a single platform.

Two responses to this crisis dominate. The first is regulatory: [[Peters2026-mo]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] show how the EU's Digital Services Act reframes access as a right rather than platform philanthropy, though implementation has been uneven and — as [[Peters2026-mo]] documents — data *quality* is a politically contested concept that platforms strategically minimize. The second is methodological adaptation: data donations ([[Bouchaud2026-lr]], [[Fan2026-af]]), scraping (audited critically by [[Rieder2025-ju]] for YouTube and [[Ulloa2024-jm]] for news content), and cross-platform reconstructions via shared narratives rather than platform-native ties ([[Gerard2025-br]]).

## Industry Entanglement and the Politics of Evidence

A closely related literature interrogates what happens to research produced *within* the walled garden. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] draw explicit parallels to tobacco, pharma, and fossil fuel science, arguing that platform control over data, funding, and access creates structurally novel biases that ordinary safeguards (IRBs, disclosure, preregistration) cannot correct. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantifies this empirically: roughly half of high-profile social media papers have disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, with an estimated "industrial saturation" of ~80% once editors and reviewers are included. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s critique of the Meta2020 partnership sharpens this into a metascientific argument: even the best-designed platform-academic collaborations produce findings with degraded temporal and poetic validity, since platforms mutate faster than the empirical apparatus can track. Together these papers form a strong prima facie case that reactive empirical science is structurally inadequate for governing platforms.

## Post-Hoc Diagnostics: Auditing the Tools We Still Have

Several papers audit the reliability of specific research infrastructures researchers continue to depend on. [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," with videos disappearing from indexes 20–60 days post-publication regardless of continued availability. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] demonstrates that ex-situ web scraping introduces ~34% content disparity relative to in-situ browser capture, and — crucially — that these errors are systematically distributed across news categories. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits the NewsGuard trustworthiness database, showing that dichotomized (trustworthy/untrustworthy) labels can distort results dramatically even when continuous scores are stable. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] uncovers how Meta's own 100-share anonymization threshold, interacting with Feed algorithm changes, imposes a platform-wide artifact that overwhelms local signals across 46 countries. And [[McNally2025-dn]] shows Facebook's News Feed is not an opaque black box: its algorithmic interventions leave detectable, three-week-lagged traces in engagement time series. The collective message is that every seemingly neutral instrument in the computational social scientist's toolkit carries its own politics and distortions requiring auditing.

## Sampling, Representativeness, and the Iceberg Problem

A parallel line of work interrogates whether observable social media data means what researchers assume. [[Oswald2025-km]] articulates the "production-consumption gap": a small minority produces most political content, and both citizens and researchers routinely mistake this tip for the iceberg. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates conventional partisanship measures by showing that source-level (domain) scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity in partisan audiences — "curation bubbles" that emerge through networked unbundling of content. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] represents an earlier attempt to solve sampling differently, using Facebook ads as a recruitment infrastructure for hard-to-reach populations, though even there the effectiveness of interest-based targeting for reaching ideologically distinctive respondents was inconclusive.

## LLMs as Research Instruments — Promise, Peril, and Validation

Perhaps the fastest-moving debate in the collection concerns how — or whether — to fold LLMs into research pipelines. Enthusiastic demonstrations abound: [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows LLMs can scale political texts by asking and averaging; [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] uses LLM-paired comparisons for open-ended survey scaling; [[Arora2025-tx]] leverages VLLMs for multi-modal frame analysis; [[Arminio2025-tw]] shows VLLM-generated image captions dramatically improve *connotative* clustering over CNNs; [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] finds OpenAI's embeddings outperform Italian-specific BERT models for clustering; [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] documents an "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline for Italian political discourse; and [[Alizadeh2026-es]] shows frontier coding agents can reproduce 78% of published social science analyses at the paper level.

But a strong critical countercurrent runs alongside. [[Balluff2026-if]] warns of unreflective adoption: reproducibility collapses under opaque model updates, synthetic data exhibits demographic narrowing, and smaller task-specific models often match LLMs at a fraction of the cost. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse — a methodological asset and a serious privacy threat. [[Brown2025-jk]] finds LLM annotator biases are dataset-specific rather than model-specific and, crucially, that item difficulty (label entropy) dominates any demographic bias, complicating simple fairness fixes. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] also documents sycophantic drift under confirmatory prompting. Meanwhile [[Fan2025-ut]] tackles a related embedding-level pathology: pretrained sentence embeddings encode source, language, and style as observed confounders, and linear concept erasure (LEACE) can systematically remove this signal.

## Methodological Frontiers: Coordination, Temporality, and Multimodality

A distinct cluster of papers pushes the technical frontier. Detecting coordinated inauthentic behavior has become a methodological subfield in its own right: [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] both argue for temporal-multiplex frameworks over single-modality or naively flattened approaches, with [[Mannocci2025-ig]] making explicit the trade-off between integration strength and community inclusiveness. [[Minici2024-tf]] proposes a graph foundation model (IOHunter) that generalizes across campaigns. [[Pante2025-pq]] applies these more rigorous tools to reassess — and partly deflate — earlier claims of inter-state coordination. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] arrives at a striking finding along the same skeptical vein: observed coordinated accounts on X exert influence in cascades statistically indistinguishable from random placement, suggesting CIB may be less pivotal than commonly assumed.

On the temporal side, [[Fan2026-af]] argues for treating digital trace data as user-sequences rather than aggregated cross-sections, surveying six analytical approaches from sequence analysis to neural embeddings. [[Bruns2025-fz]] similarly proposes vector-embedding "practice mapping" to overcome the "hairball" problem in multimodal network visualization. [[Smith2025-kc]] uses a clever cursor-based exploit of the old Twitter API to identify a causal mechanism — the "tertius amplificans" or attention broker — behind triad transitivity. [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent ideology estimation from actors to video content via cross-subreddit sharing, and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] push framing and news-circulation analysis toward unsupervised and multi-level cartographic approaches.

## Substantive Payoffs: Where Methods Meet Consequences

These methodological threads converge on high-stakes empirical questions. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] combines Bayesian epidemic modeling with geolocated Twitter data to estimate that antivaccine tweets caused ~14,000 vaccination refusals, ~545 cases, and ~8 deaths — a lower-bound causal chain from platform speech to disease burden. [[Bailard2024-pj]] uses fine-tuned DeBERTa classification and Granger causality to link Proud Boys' online framing to offline violence with a roughly four-week cycle. [[Balluff2026-bv]] applies quasi-experimental content analysis to detect covert "journalism for sale" arrangements in Austria. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" as anomalies in demand-supply time series and links them empirically to lower-credibility content circulation. [[Nenno2025-xa]] tests whether news-value theories developed in WEIRD contexts generalize to perceived misinformation across 24 countries. And [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] shows that X's recommender system inadvertently encodes users' Left-Right ideology as a linear direction (ρ=0.887) in its embedding space, blurring the legal distinction between active and passive profiling. [[F2020-6278a4aa]] provides a useful long-arc framing for these substantive concerns, identifying networked publics, micro-targeting, and attention competition as three interlocking transformations that traditional political communication methods are ill-equipped to handle.

## Threads Running Through

Read together, these papers describe a field simultaneously more sophisticated and more constrained than it was a decade ago. The methodological toolkit has expanded enormously — LLMs, foundation models, multiplex networks, causal inference on digital traces, embedding erasure, temporal decomposition — but the empirical objects those tools address are being progressively enclosed. Three recurring imperatives emerge across the collection: (1) *auditing rather than trusting* every layer of the research pipeline, from APIs ([[Rieder2025-ju]]) to scraping ([[Ulloa2024-jm]]) to source ratings ([[Luhring2025-od]]) to platform-provided datasets ([[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]]) to LLM annotators ([[Brown2025-jk]], [[Balluff2026-if]]); (2) *institutional response* — via DSA implementation ([[Peters2026-mo]], [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]]), industry-influence norms ([[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]], [[Heiss2026-qv]]), and validation protocols ([[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]); and (3) *epistemic humility* about what visible online traces can tell us about publics ([[Oswald2025-km]], [[Green2025-ap]]) and about whether reactive empirical science can ever be fast enough for the systems it studies ([[Munger2025-cz]]).
