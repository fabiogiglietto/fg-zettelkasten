# Health Misinformation Online

## Two traditions within one topic

The seven papers filed here split into two methodological lineages that rarely cite each other but converge on the same substantive question: how does low-quality or coordinated health content actually move through media systems, and does it change what people believe or do? One lineage — [[Adam2026-tz]], [[Lyons2026-ca]], [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] — works from the audience side, using panel surveys, web tracking, and epidemic modeling to trace exposure and its downstream effects on individuals and populations. The other — [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]], [[Marino2023-9137f448]], [[Song2025-yh]], and to a lesser extent [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] — works from the supply side, mapping the coordinated actors, influence networks, and platform/aggregator structures that manufacture and circulate that content in the first place. Read together, they trace an arc from "who is exposed and with what consequence" to "who produces the exposure and how."

## From belief to behavior: measuring effects

[[Adam2026-tz]] anchors the audience-side cluster by insisting that conspiracy belief formation cannot be reduced to either media effects or predispositions alone but is a "marriage" of the two — a Zaller-style information-plus-predisposition model in which alternative media produce contagion, mainstream media produce mitigation, but populist and mistrustful audiences selectively engage with the former and avoid (or counterargue against) the latter. This paper's central methodological move — pairing panel survey data with actual tracked browsing rather than self-reported exposure — is echoed and extended by [[Lyons2026-ca]], which uses the same combination (survey plus passive web/YouTube tracking) to ask not about conspiracy content specifically but about "low-credibility health content" more broadly, in the US rather than German-speaking Europe. Where Adam et al. find that conspiracy exposure is pervasive (documents touched by ~most participants) and driven by cross-source rather than single-source selectivity, Lyons et al. find the opposite exposure profile: low-credibility health content is rare in the aggregate but sharply concentrated in a small, older, more conspiracist-minded subpopulation, and — crucially — is not funneled through social media or search but through habitual navigation between low-credibility domains themselves. Together the two studies complicate any simple contagion story: exposure pathways depend heavily on content type (conspiracy belief vs. general health misinformation) and audience segment, and platform referral is not the primary vector either paper's data would suggest.

[[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes the audience-side inquiry one step further, from belief to consequence: rather than measuring conspiracy beliefs or exposure prevalence, it asks whether antivaccine Twitter content causally reduced vaccination and thereby caused excess cases and deaths, using a novel SIRVA compartmental model fit to county-level tweet, case, and vaccination data. This is the most ambitious causal claim in the topic area — a lower-bound estimate of 14,086 vaccine refusals and 8 deaths attributable to platform speech — and it complements Adam et al.'s finding that mainstream media debunking has only a modest mitigating effect: if antivaccine content on even one platform can measurably shift population-level uptake, the stakes of the selective-exposure and selective-avoidance dynamics documented in Zurich/Germany are non-trivial at scale.

## Coordination as infrastructure

The second cluster shifts attention from what audiences absorb to how problematic content is manufactured and pushed. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] is the methodological keystone here, documenting the MINE-FACTS prototype and the CooRnet-based Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) approach as a content-agnostic way to surface problematic Italian COVID-19 content faster than manual fact-checking — tracing how a covid-skeptic network emerged from pre-existing Italian political coordination (League, Five Star) and adapted tactics (first-comment link placement, link laundering through reputable outlets, religious-group infiltration) to evade moderation. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] builds directly on this dataset, drilling into the same corpus of covid-skeptic Facebook accounts to show that an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of influencers generates disproportionate share-driven engagement, and — importantly — that legacy media and mainstream journalists remain the central amplifying nodes, recycling TV and newspaper statements from experts and public figures into conspiracy-adjacent circulation. Both papers converge on a shares-versus-comments engagement signature (share-heavy = amplification/hyperpartisan; comment-heavy = contestation) as a diagnostic tool, a finding that also surfaces, independently, in [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]]'s very different Google News/Facebook health-agenda dataset — suggesting this engagement asymmetry may be a general signature of problematic or emotionally charged health content rather than an artifact specific to Italian covid-skeptic networks.

[[Song2025-yh]] extends the CLSB methodology cross-nationally, comparing UK and US Facebook vaccine discourse and arguing — against a purely alarmist reading — that coordination itself is ideologically agnostic: pro-vaccine communities (NHS, CDC-linked) coordinate just as densely as anti-vaccine ones, differing mainly in the credibility of what they circulate and in the national-political framing of anti-vaccine claims (safety concerns in the UK, individual freedom in the US). This is a useful corrective to the Italian studies' near-exclusive focus on problematic actors: it reframes CLSB as a tactic available to both misinformation and legitimate public-health communication, with national political culture — not just network structure — determining the valence of what gets amplified.

## Gatekeepers, old and new

[[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] rounds out the picture by examining a different kind of chokepoint: not coordinated user networks but the algorithmic aggregator Google News Italia, which curates the health agenda that subsequently circulates on Facebook. Its finding that "emotional" categories (celebrity health news, healthcare malpractice) drive disproportionate engagement despite low prevalence, while technical/scientific news dominates volume but not attention, echoes the Marino and Giglietto papers' concern with what gets amplified versus what gets produced — extending the gatekeeping question beyond coordinated inauthentic actors to the ordinary, non-nefarious infrastructure of algorithmic news curation.

## Cross-cutting threads

Across both clusters, a few recurring motifs emerge. First, concentration: whether in Lyons et al.'s top-10%-of-users-account-for-77%-of-exposure finding or in Giglietto/Marino's identification of small, densely coordinated clusters driving outsized engagement, low-quality health information is not evenly distributed — it clusters among specific audiences and specific amplifying nodes rather than diffusing uniformly. Second, the persistent centrality of legitimate-seeming intermediaries — mainstream media in Adam2026, legacy journalists in Marino2023, Google News in Ducci2022 — as unwitting or semi-witting amplifiers of problematic content, complicating any narrative that locates misinformation purely in fringe or "alternative" spaces. Third, an unresolved methodological gap between the two lineages: the audience-tracking papers are agnostic about content coordination, while the network-mapping papers are largely agnostic about downstream belief or behavioral effects — [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] is the only paper attempting to bridge exposure data directly to epidemiological outcomes. A natural next step for this line of work, consistent with the broader MINE project's ambitions toward country studies beyond Western Europe and the US, would be extending either the CLSB/coordination methodology or the survey-plus-tracking design to health information ecosystems in India and Nigeria, where platform affordances, gatekeeping structures, and political framings of health discourse likely diverge substantially from the Italian, UK, US, and German-Swiss contexts examined here.
