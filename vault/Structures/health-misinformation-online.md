# Health Misinformation & Digital Gatekeeping

## From gatekeeping to ecosystems

The earliest work filed here, [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]], is a direct descendant of the Gates Foundation–funded interest in health information gatekeeping: it treats Google News Italia as a classical gatekeeper, tracing how its editorial aggregation of health topics translates into Facebook engagement. Its finding that "emotional" or "popular" categories (celebrity health news, healthcare malpractice) generate disproportionate engagement relative to their share of the news agenda — while medical-science content dominates supply but not attention — anticipates a theme that recurs across the whole topic: exposure to health content is not a simple function of what is produced or aggregated, but of a downstream demand/attention layer that behaves according to its own logic. The paper's vocabulary is still recognizably Shoemaker-and-Vos gatekeeping theory, applied to an "algorithmically mediated" environment; the rest of the collection can be read as what happens when that gatekeeping frame is generalized from a single aggregator to whole cross-platform information ecosystems, and from Italy's health agenda specifically to COVID-19 vaccine and conspiracy discourse globally.

## Measuring the pathology of supply and demand

[[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes exactly the supply/demand mismatch that [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] gestures toward, formalizing "information voids" as anomalous imbalances between demand (Wikipedia views, Google Trends) and supply (Facebook, Twitter, GDELT) around COVID-19 vaccine milestones in six European countries. Its key empirical payoff — that voids are systematically associated with a lower share of high-credibility content and a higher share of misinformation — turns what was a qualitative worry in the infodemiology literature (data voids, Golebiewski & boyd) into a measurable, mechanistic construct. [[Lyons2026-ca]] complements this from the demand side: using web- and YouTube-tracking data linked to a US survey, it shows that low-credibility health exposure is rare in the aggregate but heavily concentrated among a small, older, more conspiracist-leaning subpopulation, and — crucially — that this exposure is driven by habitual referral from other low-credibility domains rather than by search or social media referral. Read together, these two papers suggest that "gatekeeping failure" in health information is less about platforms funneling users toward bad content and more about a self-sustaining niche ecosystem that opens up during voids and is inhabited by a committed minority of consumers, a picture that sits uneasily with simpler "filter bubble" or "algorithmic amplification" narratives.

## Networked and coordinated spread

Two Italy-focused studies extend the ecosystem view to the mechanics of amplification. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] follows a coordinated network of covid-skeptic Facebook accounts and shows that an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of alternative influencers achieves outsized share-driven engagement, but that legacy journalists and right-leaning newspapers remain the central nodes actually amplifying this content — a remediation dynamic in which mainstream media outlets recycle experts' on-air statements into conspiratorial framings, blurring Chadwick's hybrid-media-system boundary between "alternative" and "legacy." [[Song2025-yh]] broadens the comparative lens to UK and US Facebook vaccine discourse, finding that coordinated link-sharing behavior (CLSB) is not inherently malicious: anti-vaccine networks in both countries circulate substantially less verifiable content than pro-vaccine networks, but the political framing differs sharply by national context (safety skepticism in the UK, individual freedom in the US), underscoring that misinformation ecosystems are shaped as much by political culture as by platform architecture. Both papers converge on the point that coordination and remediation, not raw volume, are what make problematic content disproportionately visible — a mechanism [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]]'s gatekeeping model could not capture because it assumed a single point of editorial control.

## Psychological and causal pathways from exposure to belief and behavior

A second cluster asks what exposure to this content actually does. [[Adam2026-tz]] combines panel survey and web-tracking data in Germany/Switzerland to show that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of media exposure and predispositions: populists selectively engage with conspiracy-supporting alternative media (reinforcement) and, when they do encounter mainstream debunking, sometimes have their beliefs strengthened rather than corrected (counterarguing/backfire) — a finding that complicates any simple "expose people to reliable content and misinformation will decline" policy logic, and one that resonates with [[Lyons2026-ca]]'s observation that a media-literacy intervention had no effect on downstream low-credibility exposure. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes furthest along the causal chain, building a compartmental epidemic model (SIRVA) fit to county-level US data to estimate that antivaccine tweets causally produced measurable reductions in vaccination and, ultimately, additional COVID-19 cases and deaths — moving the topic's methodological center of gravity from correlational content analysis toward mechanistic, quantifiable harm estimation, and offering the collection's most concrete answer to the "so what" question that the ecosystem and network papers largely bracket.

## The infrastructure of gatekeeping today

Finally, [[Tonneau2025-bv]] returns to the gatekeeping frame but relocates it institutionally, examining not editorial curation but the human moderation workforce platforms deploy under DSA transparency mandates. Its finding that Global South languages (Arabic, Spanish, Portuguese) receive a fraction of English's moderator allocation, and that some EU languages have no human oversight at all, reframes "digital gatekeeping" as an infrastructural and linguistic equity problem rather than a purely algorithmic or editorial one. This is a natural endpoint for the topic's arc: from Google News curating a national health agenda ([[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]]), through the ecosystem dynamics and coordinated networks that exploit gaps in that curation ([[Scalco2026-bd]], [[Lyons2026-ca]], [[Marino2023-9137f448]], [[Song2025-yh]]), through the psychological and epidemiological consequences of failed gatekeeping ([[Adam2026-tz]], [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]]), to the recognition that gatekeeping capacity itself — the human labor of moderation — is unevenly and inequitably distributed across languages and, by extension, across the very cross-national contexts (India, Nigeria, and beyond) that motivated this line of inquiry in the first place.
