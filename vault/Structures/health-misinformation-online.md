# Health Misinformation & Online Health Communication

## Cognitive foundations: why misinformation sticks and spreads

The note's theoretical anchor is [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], which lays out the cognitive architecture explaining why corrections so often fail: retractions rarely eliminate reliance on false information, can backfire under certain framings, and must compete with the fluency and narrative coherence that make misinformation "sticky" in the first place. This individual-level account of the *continued influence effect* supplies the psychological substrate for everything downstream in this topic: if debunking is this hard at the level of a single belief, then the media and platform dynamics documented in the rest of the collection become mechanisms that either compound or partially offset an already difficult cognitive problem. [[Adam2026-tz]] operationalizes exactly this tension at the societal level, tracking real browsing behavior during the COVID-19 outbreak to show a "marriage" of media exposure and political predisposition: alternative media produce *contagion* effects, mainstream media produce *mitigation* effects, but populists selectively engage with the former and avoid the latter, and when they do encounter debunking content, it can trigger counterarguing rather than correction — an ecological, field-based demonstration of the backfire dynamics Lewandowsky describes in the lab.

## Coordination and the architecture of problematic networks

A cluster of papers shifts from individual cognition to the structural question of *how* problematic health content is organized and amplified. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] documents the rise of an Italian covid-skeptic coordinated network on Facebook, showing how Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) functions as a content-agnostic signal that can surface problematic material faster than manual fact-checking, while also cataloguing the evasive tactics — link laundering, first-comment link placement, cross-posting into unrelated religious or community groups — that such networks use to survive platform enforcement. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] builds directly on this coordinated-network dataset to trace how an "Alternative Influence Network," anchored by an Intellectual Dark Web cluster, achieves outsized share-driven engagement, with legacy journalists and right-leaning newspapers remaining central nodes — evidence that remediation of mainstream expert statements, not just fringe content, is a key amplification mechanism. [[Song2025-yh]] extends the CLSB lens cross-nationally and across stance, comparing pro- and anti-vaccine coordinated communities in the UK and US; it complicates any simple "coordination = malicious" equation by showing that coordinated sharing can also scale credible public-health messaging, with national political culture shaping whether anti-vaccine narratives emphasize safety concerns (UK) or individual liberty (US). Efst6atiou2026-ij — more precisely [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] — pushes this architecture further upstream, into the circulation of actual scientific papers, finding an almost entirely contrarian coordinated network that amplifies a narrow set of credentialed anti-consensus experts, and tracing a Twitter-to-news information pathway in which superspreaders (not bots) precede and shape subsequent media coverage, with high- and low-trust outlets each gravitating toward their respective conformist or contrarian superspreader allies.

## Measuring how much is actually out there

A second strand asks a quantitative question that cuts against alarmist assumptions: how prevalent is this content, really? [[Scalco2026-bd]] develops a method for detecting "information voids" — moments where public demand for reliable content outstrips supply — showing empirically that such voids, triggered by events like vaccine authorizations or safety suspensions, coincide with measurable drops in high-credibility content and increases in misinformation share. [[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] both use large-scale behavioral tracking to show that exposure to low-credibility health content is rare in the aggregate but sharply concentrated — in Lyons's case among older adults with poorer health-discernment and more conspiracist worldviews, reached not via search or social referral but through habitual cross-domain consumption of other low-credibility sites; in Bergeron-Boutin's case concentrated among a small minority of Facebook/Instagram users more generally. Together these papers push back against "infodemic" narratives of ubiquitous exposure, relocating the problem from the population at large to a persistent, identifiable tail of heavy consumers.

## Does exposure actually change behavior?

Given this concentration, a crucial question becomes causal impact, and here the collection contains a genuine tension. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] builds a compartmental SIRVA epidemic model fit to county-level tweet, vaccination, and case data, estimating that antivaccine Twitter content causally produced roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and measurable excess cases and deaths — a mechanistic bridge from platform speech to epidemic outcomes. [[Allen2024-av]], working at Facebook-wide scale, complicates the picture further: fact-check-flagged misinformation is indeed more persuasive per view, but its reach is so limited that *unflagged*, factually accurate yet "vaccine-skeptical" content — often from mainstream outlets — is estimated to have had a 46-fold greater aggregate impact on hesitancy, because what predicts harm is not veracity but whether content implies vaccines are dangerous. Read alongside [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]]'s null attitudinal effects from reduced untrustworthy-source exposure, this strand suggests that veracity-centered interventions (labels, downranking) may be targeting the wrong slice of the content ecosystem, and that mainstream, technically-true-but-alarming coverage deserves at least as much scrutiny as overt falsehoods.

## Gatekeepers, experts, and the politicization of science

A final strand examines how legitimate scientific and journalistic institutions themselves shape health discourse, sometimes in ways that blur into the dynamics above. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] shows UK newspapers citing Great Barrington vs. John Snow Memorandum signatories along partisan lines, demonstrating that polarization in COVID-19 science coverage can emerge from the media's selective use of mainstream (not fringe) experts rather than from manufactured misinformation per se — a finding that resonates with [[Efstratiou2026-ij]]'s mainstream-vs-contrarian superspreader alignment and with [[Marino2023-9137f448]]'s remediation of credentialed voices. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] rounds out the picture by examining Google News Italia as an algorithmic gatekeeper of the health agenda, finding that emotionally charged, "popular" topics (celebrity illness, healthcare malpractice) generate disproportionate engagement relative to technical medical-scientific content — a structural incentive that, combined with the coordination and amplification mechanisms documented elsewhere in this note, helps explain why post-truth dynamics around medicine persist even without any single actor behaving maliciously.
