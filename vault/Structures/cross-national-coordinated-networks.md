# Cross-National Coordinated Networks

## Extending Coordination Detection Beyond Italy

The papers gathered here trace how the coordination-detection paradigm pioneered in Italian-language computational propaganda research has diffused into new national and platform contexts, each forcing methodological adaptation. [[Kansaon2025-id]] is emblematic: it explicitly adapts the "Rapid Spread Network" approach originally built for Twitter to the closed, encrypted architecture of Brazilian WhatsApp groups, showing that coordinated accounts can be identified even without the public API traces that anchor most coordination research. [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] pushes detection into an entirely new architectural register — the decentralized, federated Bluesky — arguing that real-time identification of coordinated bots is both feasible and urgent given the platform's growing role in viral misinformation. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] adapts network-based forwarding analysis to Telegram's affordances in the authoritarian Russian and Belarusian contexts, while [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] applies coordination-detection algorithms to multilingual Twitter activity surrounding China's 2022 COVID-19 protests, identifying over 13,000 coordinated accounts and showing that the same infrastructure can support both protest mobilization and state-aligned repression. Together these papers demonstrate that "coordination" is not a fixed signature but a platform-contingent phenomenon requiring bespoke operationalizations — message-identical time windows on WhatsApp, forwarding cascades on Telegram, hyperactive sharing thresholds on Facebook ([[Oprea2025-lf]]), or visual self-presentation features extracted via computer vision from removed troll-farm accounts (Bastos2025-ol).

## Authoritarian and Hybrid-Regime Variants

A cluster of papers maps how coordinated inauthentic behaviour takes qualitatively different forms depending on regime type. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] argues that Serbia's ruling SNS party has moved beyond the centralized "bot farm" model into a more diffuse, mimetic form of digital authoritarianism that convincingly simulates grassroots support and thereby evades standard detection — a claim that implicitly critiques the generalizability of detection methods built on more overt, centralized operations like Russia's Internet Research Agency. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] provides the organizational counterpoint, using leaked CVs to reconstruct the IRA as a labor-market institution — a five-level media-company hierarchy recruiting inexperienced graduates, most active around the 2014 Crimea annexation rather than the 2016 US election — reframing troll farms as production entities embedded in national media economies rather than as anonymous swarms. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] extend this authoritarian lens to Telegram amplification in Russia/Belarus and to dual-use protest/repression dynamics in China, while [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceptualizes Philippine influence operations not as disinformation campaigns per se but as political brokerage — intermediaries linking clients, platforms, and voters through durable political-economic infrastructures. Read together, these cases suggest a typology of coordination running from centralized bot farms (Russia) to brokered campaign firms (Philippines) to mimetic, decentralized grassroots simulation (Serbia), each demanding different detection logics.

## The Brazilian Complex: WhatsApp, Facebook, and Democratic Trust

Brazil emerges as the most densely instrumented non-Italian case in this collection, with papers spanning detection, experimental causal inference, and downstream political consequences. [[Kansaon2025-id]] documents how coordinated WhatsApp accounts disseminated misinformation and helped mobilize the January 8, 2023 attacks, while [[Ventura2025-sw]] complements this with an experimental deactivation design showing WhatsApp's causal role — distinct from feed-based platforms — in shaping exposure to misinformation and political beliefs. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] extends the audit lens to Facebook political advertising in the 2018 election, using volunteer browser-plugin crowdsourcing to surface irregular ads in a context where platform-provided transparency tools were absent. [[Marino2026-he]] then shows what happens inside a hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network once formed: rather than a stable echo chamber, affective engagement is highly event-sensitive, with a sharp de-intensification after Lula's inauguration and the coup attempt — evidence that coordinated and organic partisan communities are porous, contested spaces rather than sealed ones. Finally, [[Rossini2026-mj]] closes the causal loop by linking exposure to electoral misinformation (much of it channeled through the same digital and messaging ecosystems) to eroded trust in the 2022 election results, while showing that mainstream news use offers a partial democratic corrective even among Bolsonaro voters. Collectively this sub-cluster traces a full pipeline from coordinated network formation, through advertising and messaging exposure, to attitudinal and behavioral consequences including real-world mobilization.

## Germany and the Protest-to-Extremism Pipeline

The German material foregrounds coordination not as bot-driven manipulation but as movement-level network structure. [[Rothut2026-or]] shows how the Querdenken anti-COVID movement's Telegram infrastructure became structurally embedded within — and brokered by — a central cluster of German far-right actors, who linked ostensibly moderate protest sympathizers to international, QAnon-heavy conspiracist networks. This is less "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" in the bot-network sense than an organic but consequential coordination of attention and framing, complementing the more automation-focused detection literature. [[Schulte2026-df]] pivots to method: using the 2025 German federal election as a stress test, it argues the central obstacle to multi-platform electoral analysis (X and TikTok) is not a shortage of studies but the absence of reusable research infrastructure under post-API constraints — a methodological reflection that applies directly to the cross-national coordination-detection enterprise this topic surveys, since inconsistent platform access is precisely what impedes replicating Italian-style detection pipelines elsewhere.

## Comparative and Cross-National Framing

A final set of papers steps back to compare misinformation and coordination dynamics systematically across countries, providing the comparative scaffolding this topic calls for. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] analyzes 32 million tweets from parliamentarians in 26 countries to show that misinformation sharing is driven specifically by the interaction of populism and right-wing ideology — radical-right populism — rather than by either alone, reframing misinformation as elite party strategy rather than platform pathology. [[Iris2026-pg]] compares media visibility of radical-right actors across five European countries, finding a structural, cross-outlet overrepresentation that could itself be read as a form of "coordination" at the level of agenda-setting rather than account behavior. [[Copland2025-em]] traces how Sky News Australia operates as a node bridging Australian and US right-wing media ecosystems, exemplifying transnational network propaganda distinct from covert bot coordination. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] extend the comparative lens to Scandinavian and Swedish campaign communication, showing how platform-specific strategies (TikTok memes, Facebook reaction-driven sharing) are deployed by populist-right parties across the Nordic countries. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] provides the field-level manifesto underlying all of this: a call to de-WEIRDify misinformation research through comparative, cross-platform, interdisciplinary work — precisely the mandate this topic's own assemblage of Brazilian, German, Serbian, Chinese, Russian/Belarusian, and Philippine cases attempts to fulfill. Notably, despite the topic's ambition to extend Italian-context coordination methods to Nigeria and Latin America broadly, no paper here yet delivers a Nigerian case or a pan-Latin-American comparison beyond Brazil — a visible gap that future filing under this structure note should address as the literature matures.
