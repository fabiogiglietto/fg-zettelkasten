# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster traces a shift in how conspiracy research conceives its object: from conspiracy belief as an individual outcome of media exposure, through conspiracy narrative as a cultural and discursive form, to conspiracism as an infrastructural effect of platform grammars. Read together, the papers move from "what media do to audiences" toward "what audiences and platforms do with stories," with journalism, alternative media, and image-centric platforms each contributing distinct logics to that process.

## Media exposure and the formation of belief

The most classically "effects"-oriented work anchors the topic. [[Adam2026-tz]] combines panel survey and web-tracking data to show that COVID-19 conspiracy beliefs emerge from a "marriage" of predispositions (populism, political mistrust) and actual media diets—alternative media produce contagion, mainstream media produce mitigation, but selective engagement/avoidance and counterarguing route predispositions into belief even when overall exposure patterns look similar across the population. This fine-grained causal story is complemented by [[Rohrbach2026-rc]], which turns the lens onto journalism itself, asking how specific reporting strategies—not just alternative media—can inadvertently manufacture new conspiracy beliefs rather than merely transmitting or debunking existing ones. Zooming out from single-country, single-platform designs, [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study synthesizes expert judgment across the whole field, concluding that no single intervention point (platform, journalism, or individual) suffices and that conspiracy theories and misinformation must be treated as entangled, multi-level problems—a meta-level counterpoint that frames why the remaining papers each attack a different piece of the puzzle.

## Disinformation and conspiracy as narrative, not claim

A second cluster reconceives conspiracy content as story rather than information. [[Marwick2026-qd]] is the pivotal theoretical move here: disinformation succeeds as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic narrative, signaled by catchphrases and images rather than verifiable claims—explaining why fact-checking and platform-bound moderation struggle to contain it. This narrative logic is extended empirically by [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Prochaska2026-pr]], both working from Twitter election-rumor corpora. The former shows that the *how* of telling (style, allusion, cueing) carries as much meaning as content, with 2022 rumors relying on audiences' prior absorption of the 2020 "deep story" of fraud; the latter formalizes this into a replicable "grammar" of election rumors (Actor–Object–Action plus optional Scene and Remedy), demonstrating that apparently chaotic rumor ecosystems are built from a finite, recombinable trope inventory. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes the narrative turn toward identity: across six platforms, Great Reset discussants perform symbolic boundary work, reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor and transforming personal grievance into collective "deep story" solidarity—showing that platform affordances matter less than the relational, affective work of identity-making. Together these papers argue that conspiracy narratives are sustained by storytelling craft and social bonding, not solely by informational falsity.

## Platform logics: Telegram, Instagram, TikTok

A third strand asks how specific platform environments shape conspiratorial and extremist discourse through their particular affordances. [[Rothut2026-or]] documents how Telegram-based COVID protest channels became structurally embedded in far-right/conspiracist networks, with anti-elite framing (not explicit ideology) serving as the connective tissue—protest as a meso-level mainstreaming mechanism. On Instagram, [[Nangle2026-yo]] finds far-right actors deploying positive affect, humor, and memetic "softening" within an "affective-discursive ecology" where algorithmic proximity aids mobilization, while [[Gardam2025-er]] makes a methodological argument that image-centric platforms like Instagram demand visual-first analysis of climate-denial conspiracy discourse, since text-centric methods miss where communicative weight actually sits. TikTok receives the most sustained attention: [[Marwick2025-ov]] shows ConspiracyTok reshaping the demographic stereotype of the conspiracist (young, non-white, female creators) through a "generous epistemology" that mimics academic/journalistic legitimation while rejecting institutional authority; [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats deliberately shifting securitizing visual discourse toward TikTok memes to target younger voters, using decontextualized foreign footage; and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] complicates the picture by showing Canadian TikTok immigration discourse skewing pro-immigration, with ideologically opposed users deploying the platform's affordances in strikingly symmetrical ways. Read together, these platform studies suggest affordances are double-edged resources that can be mobilized by any ideological position, with memes and visual softening functioning as the dominant currency of normalization.

## Infrastructures of circulation and search

A more infrastructural strand treats conspiracism as an emergent property of circulation itself. [[Tuters2026-vo]] is the theoretical centerpiece: via large-scale hashtag network analysis of the Great Reset, it argues conspiratorial *alignment* precedes narrative coherence—platform grammars (repetition, modular reuse, symbolic compression) generate a "high-tech paranoid style" in which patterned visibility is read as intentional elite coordination, even when institutional language (WEF reports) rather than fringe subcultures is the source material. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] offers a conceptual complement, proposing "networked exposure" to capture how ordinary, non-conspiratorial content (news, Wikipedia) becomes weaponized once it circulates within conspiracy-aligned networks—extending exposure research beyond direct contact with flagged content. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] examines a different infrastructural choke point, the search engine, showing that Google's organic results and AI Overviews respond unevenly to conspiratorially-framed queries, debunking established theories (chemtrails) more reliably than emergent ones (15-minute cities), sometimes inadvertently legitimizing the latter by framing them as "debates." These three pieces collectively argue that the *architecture* of circulation and retrieval—not only content or belief—actively co-produces conspiratorial meaning.

## Alternative media, journalism, and discursive boundary-work

A final strand returns to the journalism/alternative-media interface that opened the topic, but through discourse rather than effects. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows how "fake news" has been emptied of descriptive content and weaponized symmetrically by left- and right-wing outlets (Occupy Democrats, Newsmax) to redraw Hallin's spheres of consensus, controversy, and deviance. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offers an earlier case of discursive dissent-work, examining how #JeNeSuisPasCharlie users articulated disagreement with #JeSuisCharlie's dominant solidarity narrative without endorsing violence—an instructive precedent for the boundary-work mechanisms later generalized in [[Grusauskaite2026-po]]. Finally, [[Ramos2026-qo]] closes the loop between discourse and intervention: reflecting on the 2024 Valencia flood "hidden bodies" conspiracy, it argues that fact-checking alone cannot neutralize narratives that evolve and regenerate, proposing "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" (targeting fallacious premises rather than individual claims) as a complement—operationalizing, in practice, the narrative-centric diagnosis that runs through [[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Tuters2026-vo]], and [[Prochaska2026-pr]].

Across all six threads, the collection converges on a shared diagnosis: conspiracy belief is not simply transmitted information to be corrected, but a narrative, affective, and infrastructural achievement—co-produced by journalism's framing choices, alternative media's rhetorical strategies, platform-specific affordances, and the circulatory architectures of networks and search—and any adequate countermeasure (per [[Mahl2026-hc]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]]) must operate at the level of narrative and infrastructure, not claim-by-claim correction.
