# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## Media Logics of Belief Formation

At the center of this cluster lies a foundational question: how does exposure to different media environments actually shape conspiracy belief, as distinct from merely reflecting pre-existing predispositions? [[Adam2026-tz]] offers the most methodologically rigorous answer, combining panel survey and web-tracking data from the early COVID-19 pandemic to demonstrate that belief formation is a "marriage" of media content and political predisposition. Its key contribution is disentangling *contagion* (alternative media exposure increasing belief) from *mitigation* (mainstream media debunking decreasing it), while also showing that populists and the politically mistrustful selectively engage with alternative media and selectively avoid mainstream debunking—and that when debunking does reach them, it can backfire into reinforced belief. This selective-exposure architecture gives empirical teeth to a claim that [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] approaches from the production side: journalism itself, through its own reporting conventions and narrative choices, can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs rather than merely transmitting or correcting existing ones. Read together, these two papers reframe conspiracy circulation as a *two-sided* media problem—not just what alternative and fringe outlets amplify, but what mainstream journalistic strategies unintentionally enable.

[[De_Leon2025-qn]] extends this logic by challenging where analysts should even look for "conspiracy content." Its concept of *networked exposure* argues that ostensibly neutral material—a Wikipedia article, a routine news story—becomes politically weaponized once it circulates within extremist or conspiracy-aligned networks. This complicates the contagion/mitigation binary from Adam2026-tz: content need not be conspiratorial in origin to function conspiratorially in circulation, meaning mainstream and alternative media are not always cleanly separable exposure categories but are entangled through redistribution.

## Infrastructures and Networked Mainstreaming

A second strand shifts from individual-level exposure to meso- and infrastructural-level dynamics of circulation. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to show how the German Querdenken movement's Telegram communication became a conduit linking otherwise moderate publics to far-right and conspiracist networks—not primarily through explicit ideological commitment, but through shared anti-elite framing that served as a discursive bridge. Its network analysis reveals that protest channels were not self-contained but structurally embedded within a broader transnational conspiracist and far-right ecosystem, with outlinking shifting steadily toward alternative and partisan media over the pandemic's course. This resonates with Adam2026-tz's finding that conspiracy-supporting content pervaded even mainstream media environments: both papers suggest porous boundaries between "conspiratorial" and "ordinary" media spaces rather than discrete silos.

[[Kasianenko2026-tn]] pushes this infrastructural lens further upstream, to the search engines that mediate access to information in the first place. Its algorithmic audit of Google Search and AI Overviews shows that query framing itself shapes what conspiratorial versus general searches surface, and that AI-generated guardrails are unevenly calibrated—robust for established theories like chemtrails, but prone to legitimizing newer narratives (e.g., 15-minute cities) by framing them as ongoing "debates." Together with Rothut2026-or, this paper demonstrates that mainstreaming is not solely a matter of social contagion between users but is actively shaped by platform and algorithmic architectures that structure discoverability and legitimacy.

## Platform Vernaculars: Image, Video, and Identity

A third cluster turns to the discursive and aesthetic strategies specific to image- and video-centric platforms. [[Gardam2025-er]] makes a methodological intervention, arguing that climate denial conspiracy discourse on Instagram cannot be adequately captured by text-first approaches, since visual elements carry primary communicative weight; it proposes a visual-first framework integrating textual and contextual cues. This methodological argument finds a natural empirical counterpart in [[Marwick2025-ov]], whose qualitative content analysis of U.S. "ConspiracyTok" reveals how TikTok's affordances—greenscreen, visual evidence collages, curated identity markers—are mobilized to construct legitimacy and intimacy with viewers. Marwick2025-ov's central claim, that ConspiracyTok is dominated by young, non-White, and/or female creators building a "generous epistemology" of populist knowledge production, complicates the classic image of the conspiracy theorist and shows how identity performance itself becomes evidentiary practice. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]], though centered on immigration rather than conspiracy per se, extends this platform-vernacular analysis by showing that TikTok's affordances (audio, community hashtags, humor) are deployed symmetrically across ideological positions, producing a shared platform grammar that can carry both inclusive and exclusionary narratives—including emergent uses of generative AI for racialized "speculative worldbuilding." Read alongside Marwick2025-ov and Gardam2025-er, this suggests a broader pattern: image- and video-centric platforms do not merely host conspiratorial content but supply the affordances and aesthetic conventions from which conspiratorial (and adjacent) narrative authority is built.

## Narrative Theory and Collective Identity

The final strand steps back to interrogate the very analytical vocabulary of "narrative" and "conspiracy community." [[Sadler2025-vu]] offers a sharp theoretical corrective, arguing that disinformation research has treated narrative as self-evident and reducible to true/false judgments. Drawing on Ricoeur and Phelan, Sadler proposes a hermeneutic realist and narrative-ethical framework, demonstrated through a case study showing that content can be referentially and ethically problematic without containing outright falsehoods—a point that reframes much of the empirical work in this collection: the conspiratorial narratives documented by Adam2026-tz, Rothut2026-or, and Marwick2025-ov often operate not through fabrication but through selective emphasis, disnarration, and monological framing dressed as multivocality.

[[Grusauskaite2026-po]] complements this theoretical move empirically, showing across six platforms that "Great Reset" discourse is sustained less by ideological content or platform affordances than by symbolic boundary work: participants reclaim the stigmatized "conspiracy theorist" label, construct a collective "we" of critical thinkers, and transform personal hardship into affective, shared narratives against a "mainstream other." This identity-centered account pushes back against techno-centric explanations (echo chambers, algorithms) favored elsewhere in the collection, arguing instead for an interactionist, meaning-making lens—one that dovetails with Marwick2025-ov's finding that conspiracy theories function as boundary objects linking otherwise disparate communities around shared distrust of elites. Finally, [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]], though more narrowly focused on the politicization of social justice language across Twitter and YouTube, gestures toward a parallel process: the same platform-and-cross-platform dynamics that mainstream conspiratorial anti-elite framing (Rothut2026-or) may also transform activist vocabulary into adversarial "culture war" framings, suggesting a shared underlying grammar of politicization across seemingly disparate discursive objects.

## Synthesis

Across these eleven papers, a layered account of conspiracy narrative formation emerges: individual belief is shaped by selective media exposure and journalistic practice (Adam2026-tz, Rohrbach2026-rc); circulation is structured by networked and algorithmic infrastructures that blur the boundary between ordinary and conspiratorial content (De_Leon2025-qn, Rothut2026-or, Kasianenko2026-tn); platform-specific affordances generate distinctive visual and vernacular strategies of legitimation (Gardam2025-er, Marwick2025-ov, Hollingshead2026-vx); and underlying all of this, contested theoretical work on narrative and identity (Sadler2025-vu, Grusauskaite2026-po, Holland_Levin2026-qx) insists that conspiracy is less a fixed content category than a relational, narrative, and identity-making practice—one whose ethical and epistemic stakes exceed simple true/false adjudication.
