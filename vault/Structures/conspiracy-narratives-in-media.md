# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## Media Ecosystems and the Pathways of Conspiracy Circulation

A first cluster of papers treats conspiracy belief as an emergent property of the broader media ecosystem rather than of any single channel. [[Adam2026-tz]] provides the most granular empirical test of this idea, combining panel survey data with web-tracking during the COVID-19 outbreak to show that conspiracy belief results from a "marriage" of media exposure and political predispositions: alternative media exposure produces contagion effects, mainstream debunking produces mitigation effects, but populists selectively engage with the former and avoid (or counterargue against) the latter. This finding that even mainstream outlets circulate substantial conspiracy-supporting content complicates any simple mainstream/alternative binary, a complication that [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] pursue structurally rather than psychologically. [[Zhu2026-tn]]'s cross-country audience-overlap networks show that "alternativeness" is not a fixed property but a relational, system-dependent one—alternative outlets are more central where professional journalism is strong and more isolated where populist actors are politically marginalized (as in Sweden)—while [[Balluff2026-ev]]'s entity-network analysis of Nord Stream 2 coverage finds that German alternative and legacy media reference largely the same actors, differing instead in how densely those actors are relationally clustered and how often alternative outlets cite other media. Together these three studies push back against a diffusionist story in which alternative media are simply a separate, contaminated pipeline; instead, mainstream and alternative media are entangled at the level of both individual exposure and network structure. [[Rothut2026-or]] extends this ecosystem view temporally, showing how the Querdenken protest movement served as a meso-level conduit—via anti-elite framing and shifting outlink patterns on Telegram—that gradually routed moderate publics toward far-right and conspiracist communities, illustrating how protest infrastructures can mainstream fringe narratives over time even without wholesale ideological conversion.

## Narrative as the Unit of Analysis

A second, more theoretical strand argues that disinformation and conspiracy studies have been too fixated on discrete true/false claims and should instead treat *narrative* as the basic unit of circulation and belief. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the sharpest version of this case, showing across three U.S. disinformation episodes that successful falsehoods travel as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic stories signaled by catchphrases and images rather than as verifiable propositions—an argument with direct methodological implications, since narratives resist domain-level or platform-bound moderation. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes this claim empirically in a U.S. survey context, finding that repeated exposure to a recurring narrative (rather than to any single claim) predicts belief, and that narrative content itself modulates persuasiveness. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the philosophical scaffolding these empirical claims implicitly need, proposing a "hermeneutic realist" narratology (via Ricoeur and Phelan) that can assess content as referentially and ethically problematic without reducing it to a false/true binary—useful precisely for cases, like Marwick et al.'s Pee Tape or Suau Martinez et al.'s recurring narratives, where individual statements are not straightforwardly falsifiable. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] narrows this narrative lens onto journalism itself, arguing that specific reporting strategies can inadvertently *form*, not just diffuse, new conspiracy beliefs—a claim that dovetails with [[Adam2026-tz]]'s finding that mainstream outlets themselves carry conspiracy-supporting content, suggesting journalistic narrative choices deserve as much scrutiny as alternative-media framing.

## Platform Vernaculars: Visual, Affective, and Memetic Construction

A third group of studies zooms into how specific platforms' affordances shape conspiratorial and adjacent narratives, with special attention to image-centric and short-video environments. [[Gardam2025-er]] makes the methodological case that text-first approaches cannot capture Instagram's visual-first climate-denial discourse, proposing a multimodal framework attentive to images as primary carriers of meaning. [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends this logic to TikTok's "ConspiracyTok," showing how young, disproportionately non-White and female creators build "populist knowledge production" through curated visual evidence, deep lore, and identity markers, generating a "generous epistemology" that can simultaneously voice marginalized standpoints and spread antisemitic or transphobic disinformation—directly complicating the demographic stereotype of the conspiracy theorist that other work in this topic (implicitly) assumes. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] extend the platform-vernacular lens to adjacent populist and immigration discourse, showing how the Sweden Democrats tailored securitizing memes specifically to TikTok's virality logics (using decontextualized foreign footage to manufacture crisis) while Canadian TikTok immigration content revealed a shared affordance "vernacular" across ideological camps, with humor and non-original audio use as the chief points of divergence. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offers an earlier, text-and-hashtag-based counterpoint from Twitter, examining how #JeNeSuisPasCharlie functioned as a discursive site of dissent against the dominant #JeSuisCharlie narrative—illustrating that even before the platform shift toward images and short video, hashtag-based counter-narration was already a key unit of conspiratorial and dissenting discourse formation.

## Identity, Boundary Work, and the Social Glue of Conspiracy Culture

Where the previous cluster emphasizes platform form, [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] insists that identity work, not affordances or ideology, is what sustains conspiracy cultures. Its cross-platform analysis of "Great Reset" discourse shows remarkably consistent symbolic boundary work—reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor, transforming personal hardship into a collective "deep story," and uniting ideologically disparate (secular-libertarian and religious-Christian) publics against a "mainstream other." This interactionist argument complements [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s finding that conspiracy theories function as boundary objects linking otherwise divergent communities, and together they suggest that affective solidarity and reclaimed stigma may travel across platforms even where surface content and demographics diverge.

## Countering Conspiracy Narratives: Interventions and Their Limits

A final set of papers turns from description to intervention, testing what can disrupt conspiratorial belief once narratives are in circulation. [[Costello2024-bg]] offers the most striking positive result: personalized, evidence-based AI dialogue durably reduces conspiracy belief, challenging motivational accounts that treat such beliefs as impervious to counterevidence. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the picture with "balanced news chatbots," finding—counterintuitively—that higher-conspiracy users trust and accept such tools *more* than low-conspiracy users, suggesting technological correction may work through different mechanisms than assumed. [[Ramos2026-qo]] argues from applied fact-checking practice (the 2024 Valencia floods) that claim-level correction is insufficient against evolving narrative structures, proposing "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" that target the fallacious premises binding hoaxes together—an approach that operationalizes the narrative-centric theorizing of [[Marwick2026-qd]] and [[Sadler2025-vu]] into newsroom practice. Finally, [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study synthesizes expert judgment across this entire terrain, concluding that no single-level fix (individual literacy, fact-checking, platform design) suffices and that coordinated, multi-stakeholder governance is required—an apt capstone for a topic whose papers collectively show that conspiracy narratives are formed, embedded, and sustained across journalism, alternative networks, and platform vernaculars simultaneously, and thus resist any correction pitched at a single layer of the media ecosystem.
