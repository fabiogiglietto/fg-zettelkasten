# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## The Media Ecology of Conspiracy: From Exposure to Narrative to Identity

This cluster of papers converges on a single provocation: conspiracy belief cannot be understood as a property of individual minds encountering discrete false claims, but must instead be traced through the media infrastructures, narrative forms, and platform grammars that generate, circulate, and stabilize conspiratorial meaning. Three broad currents run through the literature—exposure-and-effects research grounded in traditional media-effects paradigms, narrative/discursive approaches that treat conspiracy as story rather than claim, and infrastructural/platform-centered accounts that locate conspiracism in circulation itself rather than in belief or content.

### Exposure, Mainstreaming, and the Limits of the Information Paradigm

[[Adam2026-tz]] anchors the empirical end of this literature, combining panel survey and web-tracking data to show that COVID-19 conspiracy belief emerges from a "marriage" of predispositions and media diets: alternative media produce contagion effects, mainstream media produce mitigation effects, but populist and distrustful audiences selectively engage and avoid accordingly—sometimes backfiring against debunking itself. This confirms the core intuition of a predispositions-plus-information model (Zaller) while complicating any simple contagion story, since even mainstream outlets circulated substantial conspiracy-supporting content. [[Rothut2026-or]] extends this logic from individual exposure to meso-level movement dynamics, showing how the Querdenken protests on Telegram functioned as a conduit of "protest-facilitated mainstreaming," gradually embedding ostensibly moderate publics within far-right and conspiracist networks through shared anti-elite framing rather than explicit ideology. [[Nangle2026-yo]] pushes mainstreaming further into the affective register, showing how Instagram's far-right ecology relies not on hostility but on humor, positive affect, and algorithmic proximity to normalize extremist content—underscoring that platform logics and emotional tone, not just informational exposure, do the work of mainstreaming. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] adds a crucial journalism-studies inflection to this exposure literature, arguing that journalistic framing strategies themselves can inadvertently *generate* new conspiracy beliefs rather than merely transmitting or debunking pre-existing ones—reframing journalism as a site of conspiracy formation, not just circulation.

Several papers extend exposure research beyond direct contact with conspiratorial content. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] proposes "networked exposure" to capture how ostensibly neutral content—ordinary news, Wikipedia entries—becomes weaponized once circulated within conspiracist networks, a conceptual move that dovetails with [[Adam2026-tz]]'s finding that conspiracy-supporting material saturates even mainstream media streams. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] examines a different infrastructural chokepoint—search engines and their AI Overviews—showing that conspiratorial query framing yields systematically different (and sometimes self-undermining) results, with AI guardrails performing unevenly across established versus emergent theories like chemtrails and 15-minute cities. Together these studies suggest that "exposure" itself is not a fixed quantity but a function of infrastructural design, query framing, and platform architecture.

### Narrative as the Unit of Analysis

A second current rejects the true/false binary altogether, arguing that conspiracy and disinformation operate as *narratives*. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the foundational case: disinformation succeeds because it offers identity-affirming, cathartic stories with recognizable characters, catchphrases, and moral stakes that travel across platforms and genres, which is precisely why fact-checking and domain-level moderation fail to contain it. [[Sadler2025-vu]] radicalizes this narrative turn theoretically, drawing on Ricoeur and Phelan to argue that disinformation studies has used "narrative" too loosely; his hermeneutic realist framework shows how content can be referentially and ethically suspect—reducing complexity to Manichean cause, disnarrating inconvenient agency—without being straightforwardly false, a point illustrated through pro-Russian narratives about NATO. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] complements this narrative focus with an identity-centered account, showing that "Great Reset" discourse across six platforms is sustained less by shared content than by symbolic boundary work: reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label, building collective "we" identities through shared grievance narratives, and uniting ideologically disparate publics (secular-libertarian and religious) in opposition to an imagined "mainstream other." [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends this identity lens to TikTok's "ConspiracyTok," revealing a genre dominated by young, non-white, and female creators who construct legitimacy through "populist knowledge production" and a "generous epistemology"—a vernacular, affectively intimate mode of claims-making that destabilizes the stereotype of the isolated white male conspiracist even as it circulates antisemitic and transphobic tropes.

### Platform Grammar, Multimodality, and Infrastructural Circulation

A third strand insists that conspiracy cannot be studied through text alone, nor through narrative content alone—it must be traced through platform-specific visual and infrastructural logics. [[Gardam2025-er]] makes the methodological case for Instagram specifically, arguing that climate denial discourse is fundamentally multimodal and that visual-first analytic methods are needed to capture rhetorical strategies invisible to text-centric approaches. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] demonstrates a parallel dynamic on TikTok around immigration discourse, finding that users across ideological positions deploy the platform's affordances—non-original audio, community hashtags, humor—in strikingly symmetrical ways, such that the same memetic vernacular can carry both pro-immigrant solidarity and racist tropes, including emergent generative-AI "speculative worldbuilding."

[[Tuters2026-vo]] offers the most theoretically ambitious synthesis of this infrastructural current, arguing via the Great Reset case that conspiratorial *alignment* precedes narrative coherence: platform grammars (co-hashtag networks, modular slogan reuse, symbolic compression) organize suspicion at scale before any stabilized story exists, producing a "high-tech paranoid style" in which patterned visibility itself is misread as intentional elite coordination. This directly complements—while subtly reframing—[[Grusauskaite2026-po]]'s identity-based account of the same Great Reset phenomenon, suggesting that the symbolic boundary work observed at the level of user discourse is itself downstream of infrastructural circulation patterns that predate coherent belief formation.

### An Emerging Arc

Read together, these fourteen studies trace an arc from classical media-effects questions (does exposure to alternative or mainstream media change belief?) toward an infrastructural and narratological reorientation in which conspiracy is understood as an emergent property of circulation, affect, and platform grammar rather than a discrete cognitive or informational failure. The Charlie Hebdo case in [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]], with its early analysis of counter-hashtag dissent, anticipates this trajectory by showing how platforms generate discursive strategies of dissent and alignment independent of any single "true" account—an insight that the later, more theoretically elaborated papers in this collection extend into fully developed accounts of narrative, identity, and infrastructure as co-constitutive forces in conspiracy's contemporary media life.
