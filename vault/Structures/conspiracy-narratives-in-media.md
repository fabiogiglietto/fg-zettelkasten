# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## From Discrete Claims to Cultural Narratives

A recurring theoretical move across this literature is the rejection of disinformation-as-false-claim in favor of disinformation-as-narrative. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the strongest programmatic case: successful disinformation behaves like folklore, with recognizable characters, settings, and plots that travel across genres and platforms via discursive and visual "catchphrases," and that satisfy emotional and identity needs rather than epistemic ones—which is precisely why fact-checking and domain-level moderation struggle to contain it. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes this further at the level of method, arguing that disinformation scholarship has used "narrative" naively, collapsing it into true/false judgment; drawing on Ricoeur and Phelan, he proposes a hermeneutic-realist and ethical framework for evaluating stories that are referentially and morally suspect without being straightforwardly false. [[Prochaska2026-pr]] operationalizes the narrative turn empirically, showing that U.S. election rumors are generated from a recombinable "grammar" of Actor-Object-Action tropes that recur across election cycles—evidence that conspiratorial storytelling is combinatorial and structurally patterned rather than ad hoc. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] extends the same logic to survey research, finding that cumulative, cross-outlet exposure to recurring disinformation narratives (not isolated claims) predicts belief in the U.S. hybrid media ecosystem. Together these papers establish narrative—rather than the false/true binary—as the unit of analysis that should organize research on conspiratorial communication.

## Media Ecosystems, Exposure, and the Formation of Belief

A second cluster asks how exposure across mainstream and alternative media jointly produces conspiracy belief. [[Adam2026-tz]] combines panel survey and web-tracking data from the COVID-19 outbreak to show both contagion (alternative media exposure increasing belief) and mitigation (mainstream debunking decreasing it), filtered through populist and distrustful predispositions that drive selective engagement, avoidance, and even backfire effects when debunking is encountered. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] complicates the mitigation story by examining how journalistic strategies themselves—framing choices, narrative conventions—can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs rather than simply correcting them, suggesting mainstream reporting is not a neutral counterweight to alternative media. [[Zhu2026-tn]] reframes "alternative media" itself as a relational, context-dependent category: cross-national audience-overlap network analysis shows that the structural position of alternative outlets (isolated enclave vs. embedded node) depends on media-system type and the institutional access of populist parties, challenging any fixed mainstream/alternative dichotomy. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] extends this ecosystem view conceptually, arguing that existing exposure research has been too narrowly fixated on direct encounters with flagged conspiracy content, missing "networked exposure"—the way ordinary content (news articles, Wikipedia pages) is recirculated and weaponized within conspiracist networks to acquire new political meaning. Read together, these studies suggest conspiracy belief formation is not a simple contagion from "bad" sources but an emergent property of audiences' positioning within a stratified, overlapping, and sometimes rerouted media ecosystem.

## Platform Grammars: Instagram, TikTok, Telegram, and the Memeplex

A third strand shifts from media ecosystems in general to the specific affordances of individual platforms, asking how platform "grammar" shapes conspiratorial form. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues methodologically that image-centric platforms like Instagram demand a visual-first analytic approach, since climate-denial conspiracy content there is carried primarily by images rather than text. [[Marwick2025-ov]] documents how TikTok's affordances (greenscreen, documentary aesthetics, intimacy-building) enable a demographically unexpected cohort of young, non-white, and female creators to produce "populist knowledge production" under a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinformation-prone. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] likewise finds TikTok's content-centric, memetic architecture generating a different immigration discourse than prior network-centric platform studies, with users across ideological positions deploying the same affordances symmetrically—a finding that cautions against assuming platform features map neatly onto ideological outcomes. [[Rothut2026-or]] turns to Telegram, showing how the Querdenken protest movement's anti-elite framing and outlinking patterns structurally embedded ostensibly moderate publics within far-right and conspiracist networks, enacting "protest-facilitated mainstreaming." [[Tuters2026-vo]] offers the most infrastructural account, arguing via the Great Reset case that platform grammars (hashtag co-occurrence, modular slogan reuse) generate conspiratorial *alignment* before any coherent narrative stabilizes—an "infrastructural" conspiracism that precedes belief and complements the narrative-centered accounts elsewhere in this collection. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] closes this cluster by auditing search engines and AI Overviews, showing that query framing itself—conspiratorial vs. general—elicits systematically different, sometimes conspiracy-legitimizing results, making search infrastructure itself a site of platform-specific conspiratorial logic.

## Narrative Identity and Boundary Work

A related but distinct thread emphasizes identity and symbolic boundary-drawing as the social glue of conspiracy culture, independent of platform or content specifics. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows, across six platforms, that Great Reset communities cohere less through shared ideology than through relational identity work: reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor, constructing a "mainstream other," and transforming personal hardship into affective collective narrative—remarkably consistent regardless of platform affordances. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offers an earlier, adjacent case of discursive dissent-construction, examining how #JeNeSuisPasCharlie users articulated nuanced opposition to a dominant solidarity narrative after the Charlie Hebdo shooting, illustrating how counter-hashtags can function as sites of identity-based boundary work within a crisis news cycle.

## Countering Conspiracy: Debunking, Chatbots, and Search Infrastructures

A final set of papers turns from diagnosis to intervention, several converging on the insight that claim-level correction is insufficient against narrative-level persistence. [[Ramos2026-qo]], drawing on the Valencia flood "hidden bodies" case, proposes supplementing fact-checking with narrative monitoring and argumentation-theoretic "argument-checking" that targets the fallacious reasoning patterns sustaining an evolving narrative rather than its individual claims—directly operationalizing the narrative-over-claim thesis advanced by [[Marwick2026-qd]] and [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]]. [[Dubey2026-bl]] tests a technological intervention, the "balanced news chatbot," and finds counterintuitively that high-conspiracy-belief users trust and accept such tools more than low-belief users, complicating assumptions that conspiracy believers are uniformly resistant to counter-attitudinal information. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]], already noted above, doubles as an intervention study insofar as it evaluates how well AI-mediated search guardrails actually debunk conspiratorial queries, finding uneven success that favors established over emergent conspiracy theories. Taken together with the narrative-first reconceptualizations that open this note, these studies suggest that effective countermeasures—whether editorial, conversational, or algorithmic—must engage conspiracy theories as circulating, identity-laden stories embedded in specific media and platform infrastructures, not as isolated errors awaiting correction.
