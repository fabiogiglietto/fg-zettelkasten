# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## From Media Exposure to Belief: Effects-Based Accounts

A first cluster of papers treats conspiracy belief as an outcome of measurable media exposure, integrating classic media-effects logic with attention to today's fragmented media environment. [[Adam2026-tz]] anchors this approach empirically, combining panel surveys with web-tracking data during the COVID-19 outbreak to show that conspiracy belief results from a "marriage" of predispositions (populism, political mistrust) and actual exposure: alternative media produce contagion effects while mainstream debunking produces mitigation, but only for those not already primed to counterargue. Crucially, the study finds that even quality mainstream outlets circulated substantial conspiracy-supporting content, complicating any clean alternative-versus-mainstream dichotomy. [[Rothut2026-or]] extends this logic to the meso level, showing how the Querdenken protest movement on Telegram functioned as a mainstreaming conduit—not merely reflecting pre-existing conspiratorial publics but actively routing moderate sympathizers toward far-right and international conspiracist networks through shared anti-elite framing and shifting outlink ecologies. Together these two papers suggest that "exposure" is not a passive variable but an evolving structural condition shaped by movements, platforms, and selective engagement.

[[De_Leon2025-qn]] pushes this exposure paradigm further by arguing that existing research has focused too narrowly on direct encounters with conspiracy or hyperpartisan content, proposing "networked exposure" to capture how ostensibly neutral material (news stories, Wikipedia entries) becomes politically weaponized simply by virtue of its circulation within conspiracy-aligned networks. This reframing dovetails with [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]], which finds that cumulative, repeated exposure to disinformation narratives—rather than encounters with single false claims—predicts belief, and that narrative content itself (not just frequency) drives persuasiveness. Read together, this sub-cluster marks a shift from asking "was someone exposed to a false claim?" toward "was someone embedded in a network or narrative ecology that repeatedly recontextualizes information as conspiratorial?"

## The Narrative Turn: Conspiracy as Story, Not Claim

A second, closely related cluster argues that disinformation and conspiracy scholarship must move beyond fact/falsity binaries toward narrative as the proper unit of analysis. [[Marwick2026-qd]] is foundational here, proposing that disinformation succeeds as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic narrative—complete with characters, catchphrases, and moral arcs—rather than as discrete false facts, a claim illustrated through cases (Parkland "crisis actor," the "Pee Tape") that traveled across satire, comedy, and mainstream aggregation alike. [[Sadler2025-vu]] radicalizes this narrative turn theoretically, arguing that the field's use of "narrative" has been analytically naïve; drawing on Ricoeur and Phelan, he proposes a hermeneutic-realist and narrative-ethical framework capable of identifying content that is referentially and ethically problematic without being straightforwardly false—demonstrated via a case of pro-Russian narrative framing of NATO enlargement. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes a version of this narrative-centered view empirically in the U.S. hybrid media system, while [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] locates part of the causal story within journalism itself, suggesting that reporting strategies can inadvertently *generate* new conspiracy beliefs rather than merely transmit pre-existing ones—an argument that complicates the mitigation effect found in [[Adam2026-tz]] by implicating journalistic form, not just content valence, in belief formation.

## Platform Vernaculars: Visual, Affective, and Identity-Based Construction

A third arc turns to how conspiratorial narrative construction is shaped by the specific affordances of image- and identity-centric platforms. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues methodologically that climate-denial conspiracy discourse on Instagram is irreducibly multimodal, requiring a visual-first analytic approach that text-centric disinformation research cannot supply—an implicit rebuke to the claim-checking paradigms critiqued by [[Sadler2025-vu]]. [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends the platform-vernacular argument to TikTok, showing that "ConspiracyTok" is populated disproportionately by young, non-white, and/or female creators who construct legitimacy through a "generous epistemology" and "populist knowledge production" that mimics journalistic and academic evidentiary forms while rejecting institutional authority—complicating stereotypes of the conspiracy theorist and revealing how identity markers themselves become rhetorical evidence.

[[Grusauskaite2026-po]] generalizes this identity-centered insight across six platforms in its study of "Great Reset" discourse, arguing that conspiracy cultures are sustained less by platform affordances or ideological content than by symbolic boundary work: reclaiming the stigmatized "conspiracy theorist" label, constructing a collective "critical thinker" identity, and transforming personal hardship into affective solidarity against an imagined "mainstream other." This finding—that discursive strategies were remarkably consistent across platforms—stands in productive tension with the visual-first, platform-specific claims of [[Gardam2025-er]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]], suggesting a layered picture: surface-level modalities (image, hashtag, short video) vary by platform, but the deeper identity and narrative logics recur across them. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s earlier case study of #JeNeSuisPasCharlie, though focused on dissenting hashtag discourse rather than conspiracy per se, anticipates this boundary-work dynamic, showing how counter-publics articulate dissent and distinct collective positioning against a dominant narrative using platform-specific discursive strategies (hashtags as condensed narrative markers)—a precursor to the "catchphrase" mechanism later theorized by [[Marwick2026-qd]].

## Infrastructures of Discovery and Circulation

A fourth thread examines the infrastructural layer through which conspiratorial content is surfaced and legitimized. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] audits Google Search and its AI Overviews, finding that conspiratorially framed queries return systematically different—and sometimes conspiracy-legitimizing—results compared to general queries, with AI guardrails performing unevenly across established versus emergent theories. This work extends the exposure-based concerns of [[Adam2026-tz]] and [[De_Leon2025-qn]] into the algorithmic search layer, showing that even ostensibly neutral infrastructures (search engines, AI summaries) can inadvertently frame conspiracy claims as legitimate "debates."

## Countering Conspiracy Narratives: Correction, Dialogue, and Argument

The final arc turns from explanation to intervention, testing whether and how conspiracy belief can be reduced. [[Costello2024-bg]] offers the most optimistic empirical result: personalized, evidence-tailored dialogues with an LLM durably reduced conspiracy belief across topics and durations, challenging motivational accounts (echoed in [[Grusauskaite2026-po]]'s identity-based framing) that treat conspiracy belief as impervious to counterevidence. [[Ramos2026-qo]], drawing on the 2024 Valencia flood disinformation case, complicates the picture from the practitioner side, arguing that claim-level fact-checking—even effective dialogic correction—is insufficient against the narrative-level persistence and mutation of conspiracy stories; the paper proposes supplementing fact-checking with narrative monitoring and argumentation-theoretic "argument-checking" that targets fallacious premises rather than discrete falsehoods. Read against [[Sadler2025-vu]]'s call for narrative- and ethics-sensitive analysis, and against [[Marwick2026-qd]]'s insistence that narratives are cross-platform and multi-genre, [[Ramos2026-qo]] shows what a narrative-first countermeasure looks like in applied fact-checking practice—closing the loop between this topic's descriptive and interventionist strands.

## Synthesis

Across these fourteen papers, a shared trajectory emerges: from models that treat conspiracy belief as an effect of discrete exposure to false claims, toward frameworks that treat conspiracy narratives as cross-platform cultural objects—affectively resonant, identity-affirming, and structurally embedded in movements, platforms, and search infrastructures alike. The visual/platform-specific studies ([[Gardam2025-er]], [[Marwick2025-ov]]) and the boundary-work study ([[Grusauskaite2026-po]]) suggest that surface form varies by medium even as deeper narrative and identity logics persist across them, while the intervention studies ([[Costello2024-bg]], [[Ramos2026-qo]]) show the practical stakes of choosing between claim-level and narrative-level correction strategies. The throughline connecting journalism ([[Rohrbach2026-rc]], [[Adam2026-tz]]), alternative and social media ([[Rothut2026-or]], [[De_Leon2025-qn]]), and algorithmic infrastructures ([[Kasianenko2026-tn]]) is that conspiracy narratives are not contained by any single medium or genre—they are made, remade, and legitimated through circulation itself.
