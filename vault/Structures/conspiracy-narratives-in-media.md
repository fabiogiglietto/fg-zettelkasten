# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## Media Ecosystems and the Flow of Conspiracy Content

The structure note gathers papers that approach conspiracy narratives not as isolated beliefs but as artifacts of media circulation, asking how different communicative environments—legacy journalism, alternative media, protest infrastructures, and platform-specific visual grammars—condition the formation, spread, and endurance of conspiratorial meaning. A foundational empirical anchor is [[Adam2026-tz]], which combines panel survey and web-tracking data to show that conspiracy belief emerges from a "marriage" of predispositions and media exposure: alternative media produce contagion effects while mainstream media produce mitigation, though populists selectively engage and avoid accordingly, and even quality outlets inadvertently circulate conspiracy-supporting content. This holistic, audience-side model of exposure is complemented from the supply side by [[Balluff2026-ev]], which complicates the alternative/legacy binary at the level of content: German alternative and legacy outlets reporting on Nord Stream 2 reference largely overlapping named entities, differing instead in relational structure (alternative media form tighter, more self-referential entity networks) and in their heavier practice of mentioning other media—suggesting that alternative media's distinctiveness lies in discursive organization rather than topical divergence. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] pushes this journalism-centered inquiry further upstream, examining how mainstream reporting strategies themselves can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs, reframing journalism as a site of formation rather than merely transmission or correction.

## Protest, Networked Exposure, and the Mainstreaming of Fringe Content

A second cluster addresses how ideas migrate from marginal to mainstream circulation via social infrastructures. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to describe how the Querdenken movement's Telegram communication, dominated by anti-elitism and conspiracy framing, became structurally entangled with far-right and international conspiracist networks—demonstrating that shared anti-establishment affect, rather than explicit ideology, bridges moderate publics and extreme milieus. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] offers a complementary conceptual move, arguing that exposure research has been too narrowly fixated on direct encounters with misinformation or conspiracy content, neglecting "networked exposure"—the way ordinary, credible content (news stories, Wikipedia entries) is repurposed and weaponized once embedded in conspiracy-aligned networks. Together these papers suggest that the infrastructural routing of attention, not just the content of claims, is a key mechanism of conspiratorial reach, a theme also central to [[Tuters2026-vo]], discussed below.

## Disinformation and Conspiracy as Narrative and Identity

A substantial strand reframes conspiracy/disinformation as narrative and identity-work rather than propositional error. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the foundational theoretical move, conceptualizing disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic cultural narrative—complete with characters, catchphrases, and moral stakes—that evades correction precisely because fact-checking targets claims rather than stories. This narrative lens is extended empirically by [[Starbird2025-jj]], whose evidence-frame framework shows that election-integrity rumors on Twitter arise less from false facts than from politically charged framing applied to accurate evidence, with quote-tweet structures revealing a "call and response" dynamic between elite evidence-sharers and audience frame-supplying. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shifts the emphasis to collective identity construction, showing across six platforms how "Great Reset" discussants perform symbolic boundary work—reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor and transforming personal hardship into shared "deep stories" against a "mainstream other," uniting ideologically disparate groups through affective solidarity rather than shared content. [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends this identity-centered approach to TikTok, documenting how young, disproportionately non-White and female ConspiracyTok creators perform a "generous epistemology" and "populist knowledge production" that mimics journalistic/academic evidentiary forms while rejecting institutional authority—simultaneously enabling marginalized standpoint expression and disinformation circulation. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]], on dissenting #JeNeSuisPasCharlie discourse, offers an earlier, more modest case of how counter-narratives articulate dissent through hashtag-based discursive strategies, anticipating these later identity-and-narrative-centered analyses.

## Platform Grammars, Infrastructures, and the "High-Tech Paranoid Style"

[[Tuters2026-vo]] provides the most ambitious infrastructural account, arguing that conspiratorial alignment around the Great Reset precedes narrative coherence: platform grammars generate symbolic proximity and modular reuse (the "Memeplex") before any shared story stabilizes, extending Hofstadter's paranoid style into a "high-tech" variant that misreads patterned visibility as elite coordination. This argument productively tensions with the identity- and narrative-centered papers above: where Marwick and Grusauskaite foreground meaning-making and affect, Tuters insists that infrastructural repetition can produce alignment even prior to coherent belief—a claim that complements rather than displaces narrative accounts. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends the infrastructural lens to search engines, auditing how Google's organic results and AI Overviews respond unevenly to conspiratorially-framed versus general queries, finding that newer conspiracies (15-minute cities) receive weaker algorithmic guardrails than established ones (chemtrails)—underscoring how algorithmic and AI-mediated infrastructures, not just social platforms, now shape conspiratorial information-seeking.

## Visual and Image-Centric Platforms

The final arc turns to image-driven media logics. [[Yang2023-cg]] establishes at scale that visual misinformation on Facebook is pervasive and partisan-asymmetric, arguing that text/link-centric misinformation research has systematically underestimated the problem by ignoring images. [[Gardam2025-er]] develops a visual-first methodology for Instagram climate-denial discourse, arguing that image-centric platforms demand analytic frameworks that treat visuals as primary carriers of conspiratorial meaning rather than textual supplements. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]]—on TikTok immigration discourse—extends this attention to platform affordances, showing that users across ideological positions deploy TikTok's memetic, interactive features symmetrically, producing a "double-edged" dynamic where the same visual-platform grammar enables both inclusive and xenophobic (including generative-AI-assisted) narratives. Read together with [[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Tuters2026-vo]], these studies suggest that image- and video-centric platforms do not merely host conspiracy content but actively reshape its construction through visual evidence, aesthetic intimacy, and algorithmically legible modular forms—closing the arc from journalism's textual framing strategies to the infrastructural and multimodal logics of contemporary platforms.
