# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## The Narrative Turn: From False Facts to Cultural Stories

A central fault line running through this literature is the move away from treating conspiracy theories as discrete false claims to be corrected, and toward treating them as *narratives*—stories that circulate, mutate, and bind communities together regardless of their truth-value. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes this case most forcefully, arguing that disinformation succeeds because it has characters, settings, and plots that satisfy desire and produce catharsis, and that domain-level fact-checking is structurally incapable of countering content that travels as "shorthand" catchphrases across memes, satire, and late-night comedy. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes the point further theoretically, arguing that disinformation scholarship has used "narrative" naively, and proposing a hermeneutic-realist framework (via Ricoeur and Phelan) in which stories can be referentially and ethically suspect without containing a single falsifiable claim. This reframing finds empirical traction in the CSCW tradition represented by [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]], who show that U.S. election-fraud rumors are less about fabricated facts than about the interaction of (often accurate) evidence with partisan frames, and that audiences are recruited into a persisting "deep story" (Hochschild) that lets influencers present bare artifacts and rely on followers to supply the fraud interpretation. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] extends this narrative-and-identity logic to the "Great Reset," showing across six platforms that conspiracy culture is sustained less by ideological content than by symbolic boundary work—reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label, building a collective "we" against the "sheeple," and converting personal hardship into shared deep stories. Together these papers stage a shift in the field's object of study: from informational accuracy to narrative form, affect, and identity.

## Media Ecosystems: Contagion, Mitigation, and the Alternative/Mainstream Boundary

A second cluster asks how conspiracy content actually moves across the media system, and complicates any simple alternative-media-versus-mainstream-media binary. [[Adam2026-tz]] offers the most direct test, combining panel survey and web-tracking data to show both a contagion effect (alternative media exposure increasing belief) and a mitigation effect (mainstream debunking decreasing it), while demonstrating that even quality outlets circulated substantial conspiracy-supporting content and that populist predispositions drive selective engagement and backfire effects. [[Rothut2026-or]] examines the Telegram ecology of the German Querdenken protests, showing how anti-elite framing—more than explicit ideology—functioned as the connective tissue linking moderate protest sympathizers to far-right and transnational conspiracist networks, a case of "protest-facilitated mainstreaming." The structural position of alternative media itself is interrogated by [[Zhu2026-tn]], whose cross-country audience-overlap networks show that "alternativeness" is relational and politically contingent: overlap with mainstream audiences rises where populist parties gain institutional access, while isolated alternative enclaves emerge where professional journalism remains strong but radical actors are marginalized. [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates assumptions of divergence further, finding that German alternative and legacy outlets reference largely the same named entities on Nord Stream 2, differing not in *who* they cover but in *how* densely those actors are relationally bound together and how often alternative outlets cite other media. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] offers a conceptual corrective to this whole line of work, proposing "networked exposure" to capture how ostensibly neutral content (ordinary news, Wikipedia) becomes politically weaponized simply by virtue of its circulation within conspiracist networks—a reminder that the content/context distinction, not just the alternative/mainstream distinction, needs rethinking.

## Platform Logics and the Visual Turn

A third strand shifts attention to how specific platform affordances—especially image- and video-centric ones—shape conspiratorial discourse in ways text-based models miss. [[Gardam2025-er]] makes the methodological case explicit, arguing that Instagram climate-denial discourse is irreducibly multimodal and proposing a visual-first analytic framework. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]] both document how affect and aesthetics—humor, memetic softening, "conspiritual" styling, curated intimacy—do the work of normalization on Instagram and TikTok respectively; Marwick's ConspiracyTok study is notable for showing that conspiracy content is increasingly produced by young, non-white, and female creators who repurpose academic and journalistic genre conventions into a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinforming. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] extends the platform-affordance lens to TikTok immigration discourse in Canada, finding that ideologically opposed users deploy the same memetic vernacular (humor, community hashtags, non-original audio) and that economic rather than identity-based framing dominates—suggesting platform grammar can be ideologically agnostic even as content diverges. [[Tuters2026-vo]] offers the most ambitious synthesis of the infrastructural view, arguing via the "Great Reset" case that conspiratorial alignment is produced by platform grammars—repeated adjacency, symbolic compression, modular reuse—*before* any coherent narrative stabilizes, and coining "the high-tech paranoid style" to describe how patterned visibility itself gets read as evidence of elite coordination. Read alongside Marwick's insistence on cross-platform narrative circulation, Tuters's paper suggests two complementary but distinct mechanisms—narrative resonance versus infrastructural pattern—that may operate simultaneously.

## Journalism, Search, and Governance as Sites of (Mis)Mediation

A final set of papers turns to institutional actors—journalists, search engines, platforms—as agents that shape conspiracy uptake, sometimes unwittingly. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] argues that journalistic strategies can contribute to the *formation*, not just the diffusion, of conspiracy beliefs, a claim that resonates with Adam et al.'s finding that mainstream debunking can backfire among distrustful audiences, and that stands in productive tension with [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s earlier case of counter-hashtag dissent (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie) as a space where mainstream solidarity narratives were openly contested from within social media rather than alternative outlets. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends the institutional-infrastructure question to search engines, showing that Google's organic results and AI Overviews respond unevenly to conspiratorially-framed queries—reliably debunking established theories like chemtrails but sometimes legitimizing newer ones (15-minute cities) by framing them as "debates" or "concerns." [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] rounds out this governance strand by auditing platform policy documents themselves, finding that climate misinformation is rarely subject to binding rules and that platforms perform "governance-washing"—cultivating a narrative of benevolent neutrality while offloading responsibility onto creators, users, and external experts. Taken together with Zhu and Balluff's structural findings, this cluster suggests that the “gatekeeping” institutions nominally positioned against conspiracy narratives—journalism, search infrastructure, platform policy—are themselves active, often ambivalent, co-producers of the conditions under which conspiratorial narratives take hold.

## The Arc of Inquiry

Across these nineteen papers, the topic traces a movement from asking *whether* media cause conspiracy belief (Adam, Rothut) to asking *what kind of object* conspiracy content actually is—narrative, affect, identity performance, or infrastructural pattern (Marwick, Sadler, Grusauskaite, Tuters)—and finally to asking how the institutions meant to mediate or moderate this content (journalism, search, platforms) are themselves implicated in its production and circulation (Rohrbach, Kasianenko, Kuznetsova). The throughline is a shared skepticism toward single-cause, single-platform, or single-post explanations: belief and narrative emerge from the interaction of predispositions, platform grammars, journalistic routines, and governance choices distributed across an increasingly fragmented, image-centric, and cross-platform media environment.
