# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

### From information exposure to belief formation

A first cluster of papers treats conspiracy belief as an outcome of media exposure interacting with predispositions. [[Adam2026-tz]] offers the most direct test of this "marriage" model, combining panel survey and web-tracking data to show that alternative media exposure produces contagion effects while mainstream media exposure produces mitigation—except among populists and the politically mistrustful, for whom selective avoidance and counterarguing can turn even debunking coverage into reinforcement. This finding complicates a tidy media-effects story and dovetails with [[Rohrbach2026-rc]], which turns the causal arrow around to ask how journalistic strategies themselves—framing choices, narrative conventions—can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs rather than merely transmitting pre-existing ones. Together these two papers suggest that "mainstream" and "alternative" media are not cleanly opposed poles but co-constitutive parts of a single informational ecosystem, a point sharpened empirically by [[Balluff2026-ev]], whose named-entity network analysis of Nord Stream 2 coverage finds substantial overlap in the actors referenced by alternative and legacy German outlets. The real divergence, Balluff shows, lies in relational structure (tighter, more self-referential clustering) and in alternative media's heavier practice of mentioning other media—evidence that alternative outlets position themselves reactively and parasitically vis-à-vis the mainstream rather than constructing an entirely separate reality.

### Infrastructures of circulation: platforms, protest, and search

A second thread examines the infrastructural and algorithmic conditions that route audiences toward conspiratorial content. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to describe how the Querdenken movement's Telegram communication became a conduit linking moderate anti-lockdown sympathizers to far-right and QAnon-adjacent networks—not primarily through shared ideology but through anti-elite framing and structural embeddedness in a broader conspiracist network. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] generalizes this insight conceptually, proposing "networked exposure" to capture how even ordinary, non-conspiratorial content (news articles, Wikipedia pages) becomes politically weaponized once it circulates within extremist-aligned networks—shifting the analytic focus from the content itself to its circulatory context. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends this infrastructural lens to search engines, auditing how Google's organic results and AI Overviews respond differently to conspiratorially- versus neutrally-framed queries; it finds that AI-mediated guardrails are inconsistent, especially for emergent theories like "15-minute cities," sometimes legitimizing conspiratorial framings by presenting them as reasonable "debates." Read together, these three papers reposition conspiracy circulation as an emergent property of platform architecture, protest dynamics, and algorithmic curation rather than simply of individual belief or deliberate falsehood.

### Narrative, not information: reframing the object of study

A third and increasingly central thread argues that the field's default vocabulary—true/false, information/misinformation—misdescribes what conspiracy content actually is and does. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the case most forcefully, reconceiving disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative* built from recognizable characters, settings, and catchphrases, and argues that fact-checking and domain-level detection fail precisely because they target claims rather than stories. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes this further theoretically, critiquing the field's "naïve" and telegraphic use of "narrative" and proposing a hermeneutic-realist, narratological framework (drawing on Ricoeur and Phelan) that can assess content as referentially and ethically problematic even when no discrete claim is straightforwardly false—demonstrated through a case of NATO-blame narratives about the Ukraine invasion. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] operationalizes a related but sociological version of this shift: analyzing "Great Reset" discourse across six platforms, it shows that conspiracy culture is sustained less by shared ideological content than by symbolic boundary work—reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label, building a collective "we" of critical thinkers against "sheeple," and transforming personal grievance into affective solidarity. This interactionist account explicitly displaces platform-affordance and echo-chamber explanations in favor of identity and meaning-making, forming a conceptual bridge between the narrative theorists and the platform-ethnographic studies discussed next.

### Genre and identity on image- and video-centric platforms

A fourth cluster investigates how these narrative and identity dynamics are inflected by the specific affordances of visual and short-video platforms. [[Marwick2025-ov]] documents "ConspiracyTok," showing that young, non-white, and female creators construct "populist knowledge production" through visual evidence, deep lore, and intimacy-building aesthetics, generating a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously a vehicle for marginalized standpoint knowledge and for disinformation. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] extends the platform-affordance lens to a different topic (immigration) and finds, counterintuitively, that pro-immigration content dominates and that ideologically opposed users deploy TikTok's affordances symmetrically, complicating assumptions drawn from Twitter/X-centric hostility research; it also flags generative AI as an emerging tool for racist "speculative worldbuilding." [[Cabbuag2024-me]] rounds out the TikTok cluster by examining Filipino "dogshow" culture, showing how legal-but-harmful humor is normalized among Gen Z influencers—illustrating that platform vernaculars of incivility and conspiratorial framing share underlying affordance-driven logics even outside explicitly conspiratorial content. On the more explicitly image-first end, [[Gardam2025-er]] argues methodologically that Instagram climate-denial discourse cannot be understood through text-centric coding and proposes a visual-first framework for multimodal conspiracy analysis, while [[Arminio2025-tw]] supplies a computational complement, showing that vision-language models can recover the *connotative* (rather than merely denotative) meaning of large Instagram image corpora—directly enabling the kind of multimodal, meaning-sensitive analysis Gardam calls for.

### Solidarity, dissent, and expert diagnosis

Two outlying but framing-relevant papers bookend the topic. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] examines #JeNeSuisPasCharlie as a case of counter-narrative and discursive dissent within a moment of imposed solidarity, an early precedent for the boundary-work and counter-public dynamics later theorized more systematically in [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]]. Finally, [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study offers a meta-level synthesis, gathering 47 experts' assessments of where conspiracy and misinformation research is headed; its call for integrative, multi-level interventions—prioritizing platform governance and journalism-ecosystem reform over individual-level literacy fixes—effectively names the very fragmentation this topic's papers are working to overcome, from media-effects modeling through platform ethnography to narrative theory.
