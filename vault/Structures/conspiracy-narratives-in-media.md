# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## Media Logics and the Dissemination Problem

The cluster opens with a basic empirical puzzle: through which media pathways do conspiracy beliefs actually form and spread? [[Adam2026-tz]] offers the most direct test, combining panel survey data with web tracking to show that conspiracy belief is not a simple function of exposure but a "marriage" of media content and preexisting predispositions—alternative media exposure produces contagion, mainstream debunking produces mitigation, but populist and distrustful audiences selectively engage with the former and counterargue against the latter, sometimes backfiring. This holistic, audience-side model sets up a central tension running through the rest of the papers: is conspiracy formation best explained by what audiences are exposed to, or by what media institutions and infrastructures do in producing and circulating conspiratorial content in the first place? [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] pushes this upstream, arguing that journalism itself—not just alternative or fringe outlets—can inadvertently generate new conspiracy beliefs through its own framing and narrative choices, complicating any clean mainstream/alternative dichotomy. [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates it further empirically: comparing German alternative and legacy coverage of Nord Stream 2, it finds the two media types reference largely the same entities, diverging not in *what* they cover but in *how* they relationally organize actors (more cohesive, self-referential networks in alternative media) and in their heavier practice of mentioning other media outlets—suggesting alternative media's distinctiveness lies in structural and reflexive positioning rather than exclusive gatekeeping of fringe facts.

## From Protest and Platforms to Networked Exposure

A second strand examines how conspiratorial content moves through social and political infrastructures rather than editorial decisions. [[Rothut2026-or]] traces this through the German Querdenken movement, introducing "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to show how anti-elite framing—more than explicit ideology—let grassroots pandemic protest channels on Telegram become structurally embedded in far-right and conspiracist networks over time, with outlinking drifting steadily toward alternative and partisan media ecosystems. This resonates with [[De_Leon2025-qn]]'s conceptual argument that exposure research has been too narrowly fixated on direct encounters with misinformation or conspiracy content, when much of the real political work happens through "networked exposure"—ordinary content (news stories, Wikipedia pages) repurposed and weaponized once it circulates inside ideologically charged networks. Both papers suggest that conspiracy circulation is less about discrete pieces of false content than about the relational infrastructures—protest movements, hyperlink networks, Telegram communities—that reroute attention and confer meaning on otherwise unremarkable material. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends this infrastructural lens to search engines, showing that Google's organic results and AI Overviews respond unevenly to conspiratorially-framed queries: established theories like chemtrails trigger consistent debunking guardrails, while emergent ones like "15-minute cities" are sometimes legitimized as mere "debates," and commercial sources disproportionately shape what users encounter—demonstrating that platform infrastructure, not just editorial or audience behavior, actively co-produces the conditions for conspiratorial belief.

## Platform Grammars, Modularity, and the Image-Centric Turn

[[Tuters2026-vo]] crystallizes this infrastructural turn into a theoretical framework, arguing via the Great Reset case that conspiratorial alignment is produced by platform grammars—repeated adjacency, symbolic compression, modular reuse—*before* any coherent narrative stabilizes, extending Hofstadter's paranoid style into a "high-tech" variant where patterned visibility itself is read as evidence of elite coordination. This directly complements [[Grusauskaite2026-po]], which studies the same Great Reset phenomenon from the inside, showing via multimodal analysis of six platforms that what sustains the conspiracy community is not shared ideological content or platform affordances per se but symbolic boundary work: reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label, building collective identity via affective "deep stories," and uniting ideologically disparate groups (secular-libertarian and religious-Christian) against a common "mainstream other." Together these two papers mark a pivot from information/narrative-centric accounts toward infrastructural and identity-based explanations of conspiracism—though Tuters explicitly frames this as complementary to, not a replacement for, belief-centered accounts.

The image-centric and platform-specific papers extend this question of *how* medium shapes conspiratorial discourse. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues methodologically that climate denial conspiracy content on Instagram is irreducibly multimodal, demanding a visual-first analytic approach that text-centric discourse methods cannot supply. [[Marwick2025-ov]] examines TikTok's "ConspiracyTok" and finds that the platform's affordances for intimacy and identity performance enable a demographically unexpected cohort—young, often non-White, often female creators—to construct "populist knowledge production" through a "generous epistemology" that mimics journalistic/academic evidentiary forms while rejecting institutional authority, serving simultaneously as standpoint epistemology and disinformation vector. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]], examining Canadian immigration discourse on TikTok, similarly shows platform affordances (audio, duets, community hashtags) are used nearly symmetrically across ideological positions, suggesting a shared platform vernacular that can carry both inclusive and xenophobic narratives—paralleling Tuters' and Grusauskaite's point that the same infrastructural/discursive forms can be mobilized toward divergent political ends.

## Narrative Theory and the Limits of True/False

Finally, [[Sadler2025-vu]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] push back toward the level of narrative and discourse construction itself. Sadler critiques disinformation studies' naïve use of "narrative," proposing a hermeneutic-realist and narrative-ethics framework (via Ricoeur and Phelan) to show how content can be referentially and ethically problematic—using sideshadowing, disnarration, and false multivocality—without being straightforwardly false, thereby destabilizing the true/false binary that much of the dissemination-focused literature (e.g., [[Adam2026-tz]], [[Kasianenko2026-tn]]) implicitly relies on. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]], examining the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag, offers an earlier, more modest case of how dissenting discursive strategies form around a dominant narrative event on social media, anticipating the boundary-work and counter-public dynamics later elaborated by [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] and [[Rothut2026-or]].

## Synthesis

Read together, these thirteen papers trace an arc from audience-reception models of exposure and belief ([[Adam2026-tz]], [[Rohrbach2026-rc]]), through network and infrastructural accounts of how conspiratorial content circulates across protest movements, search engines, and alternative media ecosystems ([[Rothut2026-or]], [[De_Leon2025-qn]], [[Balluff2026-ev]], [[Kasianenko2026-tn]]), toward platform-specific and multimodal analyses of conspiratorial discourse on image- and video-centric platforms ([[Gardam2025-er]], [[Marwick2025-ov]], [[Hollingshead2026-vx]]), culminating in theoretical reframings that treat conspiracism as an infrastructural/identity phenomenon rather than a matter of discrete false beliefs ([[Tuters2026-vo]], [[Grusauskaite2026-po]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]). The collective trajectory suggests a field moving away from a simple contagion/debunking model of media effects and toward accounts in which conspiracy narratives are co-produced by platform grammars, journalistic practice, and communal identity work across increasingly image- and network-centric media environments.
