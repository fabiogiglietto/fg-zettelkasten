# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## The Arc: From Belief to Infrastructure to Narrative

This cluster of papers traces a disciplinary pivot in how conspiracy is studied: away from treating conspiracy theories as discrete false beliefs transmitted through identifiable media channels, and toward understanding them as emergent effects of narrative form, affective circulation, and platform infrastructure. Reading the set sequentially reveals three overlapping registers of explanation—media-effects/exposure models, narrative-and-discourse models, and infrastructural/platform-grammar models—that together constitute the field's current state of argument rather than a settled consensus.

## Exposure, Predisposition, and the Limits of the Effects Model

The most traditional register is represented by [[Adam2026-tz]], which combines panel survey and web-tracking data to show that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of media exposure and political predisposition: alternative media produce contagion effects, mainstream media produce mitigation effects, but populist and distrustful audiences selectively engage and counterargue their way around debunking. This individual-level, dosage-and-selectivity framework is echoed institutionally in [[Rothut2026-or]], which scales the analysis up to movement dynamics, showing how the Querdenken protests functioned as a meso-level conduit linking ordinary anti-elite sentiment to entrenched far-right and conspiracist Telegram networks—mainstreaming not through individual radicalization but through "content" and "connective" mechanisms that render fringe material repeatable and accessible. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] narrows the same causal question to journalism itself, arguing that newsroom framing strategies can inadvertently *generate* new conspiracy beliefs rather than merely transmit pre-existing ones—a claim that complicates [[Adam2026-tz]]'s assumption that mainstream media exposure straightforwardly mitigates. [[Balluff2026-ev]] further destabilizes the alternative/mainstream binary underlying all three: analyzing named-entity networks in German coverage of Nord Stream 2, it finds substantial referential overlap between alternative and legacy outlets, with divergence occurring not in *which* actors are covered but in *how* they are relationally structured and how heavily alternative media lean on citing other media. Together these four papers suggest that the "media effects" paradigm remains analytically productive but requires increasingly fine-grained distinctions—between exposure and selectivity, between movement and journalism, between content and network structure—to avoid flattening conspiracy circulation into a simple contagion metaphor. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] pushes this exposure research further still, proposing "networked exposure" as a corrective: audiences encounter not just misinformation directly but ordinary content (news stories, Wikipedia entries) that becomes politically "weaponized" through its circulation within conspiracy-aligned networks, collapsing the distinction between conspiratorial and mainstream content at the point of reception.

## Narrative as the Unit of Analysis

A second cluster insists that "narrative" itself is the proper analytic object, and that disinformation studies has used the term too loosely. [[Sadler2025-vu]] makes this case most explicitly, arguing for a hermeneutic-realist narratology (via Ricoeur and Phelan) that can assess ethical and referential problems in storytelling without collapsing into true/false binaries—demonstrated through a case of NATO-blame narratives about the Ukraine invasion that are rarely "false" yet ethically monological. [[Marwick2026-qd]] arrives at a structurally similar conclusion from a different direction: disinformation succeeds as cross-platform, identity-affirming, *cathartic* narrative, recognizable through catchphrases and visual shorthand (the "pee tape," the "crisis actor") rather than through verifiable claims, which explains why fact-checking and platform-bound moderation fail to contain it. This narrative-centrism is extended into platform-specific ethnography by [[Marwick2025-ov]], whose study of "ConspiracyTok" shows young, often non-White or female creators building "populist knowledge production" through documentary aesthetics, deep lore, and personal testimony—a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering for marginalized standpoints and a vector for disinformation and bigotry, directly complicating the stereotype of the lone white male conspiracist implicit in earlier exposure models. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes the narrative turn toward identity and sociology: examining "Great Reset" discourse across six platforms, it argues that conspiracy culture is sustained less by shared ideological content than by symbolic boundary work—reclaiming the stigmatized "conspiracy theorist" label, converting personal hardship into collective "deep stories" (echoing Marwick's use of Hochschild), and uniting ideologically disparate groups around a shared "mainstream other." Read together, [[Sadler2025-vu]], [[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Marwick2025-ov]], and [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] constitute a coherent counter-paradigm to the exposure-effects literature: conspiracy is not primarily a matter of encountering wrong information but of narrative form, identity affirmation, and affective solidarity.

## Image, Affect, and the Problem of Multimodality

A related but distinct thread addresses what text-centric narrative and exposure models miss on image-first platforms. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues methodologically that climate-denial discourse on Instagram cannot be understood without a visual-first analytic framework, since images—not accompanying text—carry primary communicative weight. [[Nangle2026-yo]] extends this multimodal attention to far-right content specifically, showing how Instagram Reels deploy positive affect and humor as "softening" strategies that normalize extremism through an "affective-discursive ecology" in which platform algorithmic logics, ideology, and emotion are mutually constitutive. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] offers a comparative counterpoint on TikTok immigration discourse in Canada, finding—contrary to assumptions drawn from X/Twitter research—that pro-immigration content substantially outweighs hostile framing, and that affordances like non-original audio and community hashtags are deployed in strikingly symmetrical ways across ideological camps, suggesting platform vernacular itself, not just ideology, structures discourse. [[Topinka2026-hb]] radicalizes the affect-over-belief argument furthest, theorizing AI-generated "slopaganda" as content that works not by deceiving viewers but by affectively "attuning" to feed environments—obviously fake yet emotionally resonant, intensifying existing ideological formations (reactionary, racialized, misogynist) rather than persuading anyone of a coherent claim. Across this subgroup, affect and visual/platform form displace propositional belief as the operative mechanism of conspiratorial and extremist circulation.

## Infrastructure and the "High-Tech Paranoid Style"

The most structurally ambitious papers reframe conspiracy as an infrastructural rather than narrative or psychological phenomenon. [[Tuters2026-vo]] is the keystone text here: tracing the "Great Reset" slogan from a WEF foresight article into a portable antagonistic meme across platforms, it argues that platform grammars produce conspiratorial *alignment*—symbolic proximity and modular reuse—before any coherent narrative stabilizes, and that patterned visibility itself is misread as evidence of intentional elite coordination (the "high-tech paranoid style"). This directly complements, rather than displaces, the narrative-centered papers: where [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]] examine the identity-work and storytelling that *follows* alignment, [[Tuters2026-vo]] examines the infrastructural conditions that produce alignment prior to story. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends the infrastructural lens to search engines, auditing how Google's organic results and AI Overviews respond differently to conspiratorially-framed versus general queries, finding uneven guardrails that can inadvertently legitimize newer theories (like 15-minute cities) by framing them as "debates." [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]], on counter-hashtag dissent after Charlie Hebdo, stands as an earlier, more modest instance of this same infrastructural insight: how a platform's hashtag affordance itself becomes the site where dissenting narrative strategies are articulated and organized.

## Synthesis

Taken as a whole, the hub traces a field moving from asking "what media exposure causes conspiracy belief" ([[Adam2026-tz]], [[Rothut2026-or]], [[Rohrbach2026-rc]], [[Balluff2026-ev]], [[De_Leon2025-qn]]) toward asking "what narrative and affective forms make conspiracy content *travel* and *cohere identity*" ([[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], [[Marwick2025-ov]], [[Grusauskaite2026-po]]), toward asking "what platform and infrastructural grammars produce conspiratorial alignment prior to narrative or belief at all" ([[Tuters2026-vo]], [[Kasianenko2026-tn]], [[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Gardam2025-er]], [[Topinka2026-hb]], [[Hollingshead2026-vx]]). No single paper claims these registers are mutually exclusive; rather, the strongest pieces (especially [[Tuters2026-vo]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]]) explicitly position themselves as complementing belief- and narrative-centered accounts rather than refuting them. The open question left running across the set is methodological as much as theoretical: whether image-centric, affect-driven, infrastructurally-produced conspiracism can still be meaningfully captured by narrative and discourse analysis, or whether—as [[Topinka2026-hb]] and [[Tuters2026-vo]] suggest—coherence and story are themselves becoming optional to conspiratorial circulation.
