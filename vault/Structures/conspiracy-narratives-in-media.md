# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## Media Logics and the Formation of Conspiracy Belief

The cluster's foundational question is not whether media matter to conspiracy belief but *how*—through which mechanisms, at which stage of belief formation, and across which media forms. [[Adam2026-tz]] offers the most systematic causal account, combining panel survey and web-tracking data to show that conspiracy belief emerges from a "marriage" of predispositions and information exposure: alternative media produce contagion effects, mainstream media produce mitigation effects, but populist and distrustful audiences selectively engage with the former and avoid (or counterargue against) the latter. This finding that even mainstream outlets circulate substantial conspiracy-supporting content anticipates [[Rohrbach2026-rc]]'s claim that journalism is not merely a diffusion channel but an active site of conspiracy *formation*—specific reporting strategies can inadvertently seed conspiratorial readings rather than simply transmitting or debunking them already-formed. Together these two papers reframe the media-conspiracy relationship as constitutive rather than merely transmissive, a thread that runs through the entire topic.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the mitigation story from a design perspective: testing "balanced" chatbots that mix mainstream and alternative perspectives, it finds that people with stronger conspiracy beliefs actually trust and prefer such tools more than low-conspiracy users—an unexpected result that unsettles assumptions (shared with [[Adam2026-tz]]'s counterarguing pathway) that conspiracy-minded audiences are reflexively hostile to balanced or corrective content. Read together, these studies suggest the mainstream/alternative binary is analytically real but behaviorally messier than intuitive models of echo chambers predict.

## Platform Infrastructures and the Alternative Media Ecosystem

A second set of papers shifts from individual-level belief formation to the structural question of how alternative media ecosystems are organized and how they relate to legacy journalism. [[Balluff2026-ev]]'s entity-network analysis of Nord Stream 2 coverage is a useful corrective here: alternative and legacy outlets reference largely the *same* actors, differing not in whom they cover but in how they relationally structure those actors (tighter, more self-referential networks) and in their heavier practice of citing other media—suggesting alternative media are parasitic on, rather than simply divergent from, the mainstream. [[Rothut2026-or]] extends this structural lens to protest movements, showing via Telegram network analysis how the Querdenken movement's anti-elite framing became a connective tissue linking ostensibly moderate protest sympathizers to organized far-right and conspiracist milieus—not through ideological conversion but through shared discursive framing and platform-routed attention. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] generalizes this insight conceptually, proposing "networked exposure" to capture how even ordinary, non-conspiratorial content (a news article, a Wikipedia page) becomes weaponized once it circulates within conspiracy-aligned networks—shifting the analytic focus from the content itself to its network context of circulation, a move that resonates with both [[Rothut2026-or]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]].

## Narrative, Identity, and the Cultural Logic of Conspiracy

A third strand rejects information-centric and even network-centric framings altogether in favor of narrative and identity as the primary units of analysis. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the strongest theoretical claim: disinformation should be understood as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *stories*—with characters, catchphrases, and moral arcs—rather than discrete false claims, which explains why fact-checking and platform-bound moderation so often fail. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes this narrative turn further by supplying it philosophical rigor, arguing that disinformation scholarship's loose, common-sense use of "narrative" needs replacement by a hermeneutic-realist and narrative-ethical framework capable of showing how content can be referentially and ethically suspect without being straightforwardly false—a direct complement to [[Marwick2026-qd]]'s cultural-narrative approach and an implicit challenge to the true/false binary underlying [[Adam2026-tz]] and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]].

[[Grusauskaite2026-po]] operationalizes this identity-centered view empirically, showing across six platforms that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere less through shared ideological content than through symbolic boundary work: reclaiming the stigmatized "conspiracy theorist" label, converting personal hardship into affective "deep stories," and constructing a "mainstream other." This finding—that discursive strategies are remarkably consistent *across* platforms—sits in productive tension with the topic's platform-specific studies, suggesting identity work may be more portable than platform affordances themselves. [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends the identity lens to TikTok's "ConspiracyTok," showing how young, non-white, and female creators build epistemic authority through "populist knowledge production" and a "generous epistemology" that blends standpoint-epistemological critique with disinformation, complicating the stereotype of the conspiracy theorist inherited from earlier (implicitly white, male) paradigms.

## Platform-Specific Affordances: Image, Sound, and Video

The remaining papers examine how particular platform affordances shape conspiratorial and adjacent political narratives at the level of form rather than content. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues methodologically that Instagram's image-centrism demands a "visual-first" analytic approach, since text-centric methods miss how climate-denial narratives are carried primarily by visual rhetoric. [[Bosch2024-hj]] makes an analogous case for TikTok and sound, showing that audio functions as an under-examined, platform-specific propaganda technique in war-related disinformation about Ukraine. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]], while focused on immigration rather than conspiracy per se, extends this affordance-centered logic by showing that TikTok's memetic, algorithmically curated architecture produces a "shared vernacular" used symmetrically by pro- and anti-immigration creators alike—reinforcing the broader point, visible also in [[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Grusauskaite2026-po]], that platform form can be ideologically ambivalent, enabling both inclusion and xenophobia, both solidarity and disinformation, depending on who wields it.

Taken as a whole, the thirteen papers trace an arc from causal, individual-level models of media exposure ([[Adam2026-tz]], [[Rohrbach2026-rc]], [[Dubey2026-bl]]) through structural accounts of alternative media networks and mainstreaming ([[Balluff2026-ev]], [[Rothut2026-or]], [[De_Leon2025-qn]]) to cultural and narrative reconceptualizations of what conspiracy content even *is* ([[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], [[Grusauskaite2026-po]], [[Marwick2025-ov]]), before finally attending to the sensory and formal specificity of the platforms—image, sound, video—that carry these narratives ([[Gardam2025-er]], [[Bosch2024-hj]], [[Hollingshead2026-vx]]). The through-line is a shared dissatisfaction with information-centric, true/false models of conspiracy and disinformation, and a convergent turn toward narrative, identity, and platform form as the more adequate units of analysis.
