# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## The Media Ecology of Conspiracy: Formation, Circulation, Form

Taken together, these seventeen papers trace a shift in how conspiracy scholarship conceives its object: from conspiracy theories as *beliefs transmitted* through media, to conspiracy as something *produced* by the specific grammars, affects, and infrastructures of the platforms and institutions through which it travels. The collection moves across three interlocking registers—journalistic/mainstream media as (inadvertent) co-producers of conspiracy, alternative and fringe media ecosystems as circulatory infrastructures, and image-and-video-centric platforms (Instagram, TikTok) as sites where conspiratorial affect and identity are performed multimodally.

### Mainstream and Alternative Media as Co-Producers, Not Just Vectors

A foundational strand complicates the simple opposition between "truthful mainstream" and "deceptive fringe" media. [[Adam2026-tz]] demonstrates empirically that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of predisposition and exposure: alternative media produce contagion effects, but mainstream media—despite debunking intent—circulated substantial conspiracy-supporting content themselves, and for populist audiences such debunking could even backfire via counterarguing. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] pushes this further into journalism studies proper, arguing that specific journalistic strategies can inadvertently *form* new conspiracy beliefs rather than merely report on or correct existing ones—reframing journalism as an active participant in conspiracy genesis rather than a neutral corrective. This destabilization of the mainstream/alternative boundary is reinforced structurally by [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]]: Zhu's cross-country audience-overlap networks show that "alternativeness" is relational and politically contingent (higher overlap where populist parties gain institutional access; isolated enclaves only under specific conditions like Sweden's), while Balluff's entity-network analysis of Nord Stream 2 coverage finds that German alternative and legacy media reference largely the *same* actors, differing not in content but in relational structure (tighter, more self-referential networks) and in heavier other-media citation. Together these four papers argue against a simple contagion model: the alternative/mainstream distinction is porous, context-dependent, and itself a discursive resource rather than a fixed media-type boundary.

### Platform Infrastructures and the Logic of Circulation

A second cluster treats conspiracy not as a narrative content to be analyzed but as an effect of infrastructural circulation. [[Tuters2026-vo]] offers the most theoretically ambitious statement here: the "Great Reset" shows that conspiratorial *alignment* precedes narrative coherence—platform grammars (hashtag co-occurrence, modular slogan reuse, symbolic compression) generate a "Memeplex" of patterned adjacency that is later read, paranoid-style, as evidence of intentional elite coordination. This infrastructural argument is echoed by [[De_Leon2025-qn]], which proposes "networked exposure" to capture how even ordinary, non-conspiratorial content (news stories, Wikipedia entries) becomes weaponized once embedded in conspiracy-aligned networks—shifting the analytic unit from discrete pieces of misinformation to circulatory position. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends this infrastructural lens to search engines and AI Overviews, showing that conspiratorially-framed queries summon materially different results than neutral ones, and that AI guardrails are unevenly calibrated, sometimes inadvertently legitimizing newer theories (15-minute cities) by framing them as "debates." [[Topinka2026-hb]] pushes the infrastructural argument into generative AI itself, theorizing "slopaganda" as content whose power lies not in deception but in affective attunement—AI models as "ideological attunement machines" that intensify ambient moods rather than persuade via coherent claims. Across this cluster, the common move is to relocate conspiracy's causal engine from belief and content to the technical/relational substrate—search algorithms, hashtag networks, model alignment—that makes certain associations thinkable before any story is told.

### Protest, Mainstreaming, and the Far Right

[[Rothut2026-or]] bridges the media-ecology and platform-infrastructure strands by examining how the Querdenken protest movement functioned as a "meso-level" conduit mainstreaming far-right and conspiracist content on Telegram, via both content mechanisms (anti-elite, conspiratorial framing) and connective mechanisms (outlinking toward alternative/partisan ecosystems, network embedding with far-right brokers). This dovetails with [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]], which turn to Instagram and TikTok/cross-platform campaigning respectively to show how far-right and populist actors deploy affect, humor, and memetic form—rather than overt hostility—to normalize extremist content and construct visual threat ("wall-building" securitization memes using decontextualized foreign footage). Read alongside [[Hollingshead2026-vx]], which finds TikTok immigration discourse in Canada skewing pro-immigration but structured by a shared "platform vernacular" (humor, memetic remix) used symmetrically across ideological positions, this group demonstrates that the same affordances enabling benign community-building also furnish the connective tissue for mainstreaming conspiratorial and exclusionary narratives—platform grammar is politically ambivalent, not inherently radicalizing or innocuous.

### Image-Centric and Narrative Construction on Social Platforms

A third strand addresses the specifically multimodal and narrative dimensions of conspiracy construction. [[Gardam2025-er]] makes a methodological intervention, arguing that Instagram climate-denial discourse is irreducibly visual and demands a visual-first analytic framework rather than text-centric coding—an argument with implications for the whole topic cluster, since several other papers (Nangle, Bouchafra, Marwick) likewise foreground multimodality as constitutive rather than decorative. [[Marwick2025-ov]] examines ConspiracyTok's demographic and rhetorical departure from the stereotypical white-male conspiracist, showing how young, non-white, and female creators build "populist knowledge production" and a "generous epistemology" through curated visual evidence, personal intimacy, and deep lore—simultaneously enabling marginalized standpoint critique and disinformation. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] shifts to Twitter/X and election rumors, introducing "deep storytelling" to show that *how* a story is told (style, allusion, cueing)—not just its propositional content—sustains collective sensemaking across incidents, with 2022 audiences interpreting ambiguous events through a pre-established 2020 "deep story" of fraud. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] complements this by analyzing "Great Reset" discussions across six platforms as sites of symbolic boundary work: users reclaim the stigmatized "conspiracy theorist" label, build collective identity through shared "deep stories" of hardship, and unite ideologically disparate (secular-libertarian and religious) publics against an imagined "mainstream other"—arguing that identity and affective solidarity, not platform affordances or content per se, sustain conspiracy culture. Finally, [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]], the historical outlier in the set, shows an early instance of counter-hashtag dissent (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie) complicating a dominant solidarity narrative—anticipating the later papers' concern with how platforms host dissenting, boundary-drawing discourse against a hegemonic frame.

### Synthesis

Across this body of work, three convergent lessons emerge. First, the mainstream/alternative media binary is analytically insufficient: conspiracy content permeates "quality" journalism and shares referential ground with legacy outlets, while its political salience is produced relationally and contextually ([[Adam2026-tz]], [[Rohrbach2026-rc]], [[Zhu2026-tn]], [[Balluff2026-ev]]). Second, conspiratorial meaning increasingly precedes and exceeds narrative coherence—it is an effect of infrastructural circulation, algorithmic adjacency, and affective attunement as much as of propositional belief ([[Tuters2026-vo]], [[De_Leon2025-qn]], [[Kasianenko2026-tn]], [[Topinka2026-hb]]). Third, on image- and video-centric platforms, conspiracy and extremist normalization are achieved multimodally and relationally—through humor, aesthetics, identity performance, and collective storytelling—rather than through argumentative persuasion alone ([[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Bouchafra2026-ts]], [[Gardam2025-er]], [[Marwick2025-ov]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]], [[Grusauskaite2026-po]], [[Hollingshead2026-vx]]). The protest-mainstreaming work ([[Rothut2026-or]]) sits at the hinge of all three registers, showing how real-world mobilization, platform infrastructure, and discursive normalization jointly produce the conditions under which conspiratorial worldviews move from margin to mainstream.
