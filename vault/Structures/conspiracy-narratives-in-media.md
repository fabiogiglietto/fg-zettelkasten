# Conspiracy Narratives Across Media Contexts

## Media Ecologies of Conspiracy: Formation, Circulation, Identity

### The problem of causal attribution across media types

A first cluster of work asks a deceptively simple question: which media *cause* conspiracy belief, and how? [[Adam2026-tz]] answers with unusual empirical precision, combining panel survey data with behavioral web tracking to show that both alternative media (contagion) and mainstream media (mitigation) shape belief, but that predispositions—populism, political mistrust—determine *which* effect dominates through selective exposure, avoidance, and backfire. Crucially, the study complicates the alternative-vs-mainstream binary that structures much of this literature: even quality outlets circulated substantial conspiracy-supporting content, meaning the media environment itself is saturated regardless of source legitimacy. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] pushes this logic further upstream, asking not how journalism helps *diffuse* existing conspiracy beliefs but how journalistic strategies—framing choices, narrative conventions—can inadvertently *generate* new conspiratorial thinking in the first place. Together these two papers reframe journalism not as a neutral corrective to misinformation but as a co-constitutive actor in conspiracy formation, a claim that sits in productive tension with classic hierarchical models pitting "good" mainstream media against "bad" alternative media.

### Platform infrastructures and the mainstreaming of fringe content

A second thread examines how platform-level infrastructures—not just message content—channel audiences toward conspiratorial milieus. [[Rothut2026-or]] offers the most structurally granular account: using Telegram data from the Querdenken movement, it shows protest movements can act as "meso-level" conduits, with anti-elite framing (present in 80% of posts) serving as a low-cost bridge connecting moderate sympathizers to far-right and international conspiracist networks, even when explicit ideology remains marginal. This resonates with [[De_Leon2025-qn]]'s conceptual intervention on "networked exposure," which argues that people encounter conspiracist meaning not only through direct exposure to fringe content but through the recirculation of ostensibly ordinary material (news, Wikipedia) once it is repurposed within ideologically charged networks. Both papers insist that conspiracy circulation cannot be understood at the level of individual posts or claims; it requires tracing network position, outlink structure, and infrastructural embedding. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends this infrastructural lens to search engines, showing that Google's organic results and AI Overviews respond unevenly to conspiratorially-framed queries—effectively debunking established theories like chemtrails while sometimes legitimizing newer ones (15-minute cities) by framing them as reasonable "debates." This suggests search infrastructure, like protest networks, can function as an inadvertent on-ramp to conspiracist framings even while nominally moderating them.

### Narrative and affect as the unit of analysis

A third, more theoretically ambitious cluster argues that the proper unit of analysis is not the claim but the *narrative*. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the foundational move here, reconceptualizing disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic story-form—with characters, plots, and catchphrases—that spreads because it satisfies desire and moral alignment, not because it persuades on evidentiary grounds. This directly explains why fact-checking fails, a concern taken up practically by [[Ramos2026-qo]], whose account of the Valencia DANA floods conspiracy shows disinformation narratives evolving and absorbing new claims even after individual facts are debunked, motivating a shift toward "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" that targets underlying premises and fallacies rather than isolated falsehoods. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] deepens the narrative-as-identity argument sociologically: across six platforms, "Great Reset" discourse coheres not through shared ideology or platform affordance but through symbolic boundary work—reclaiming the stigmatized label "conspiracy theorist," transmuting personal hardship into collective "deep stories" (echoing Hochschild, also invoked in [[Marwick2026-qd]]), and constructing a "mainstream other." This cross-platform consistency is a striking empirical rebuke to techno-determinist explanations (echo chambers, algorithmic radicalization), aligning this cluster's central claim: conspiracy narratives are relatively platform-agnostic *cultural forms* that migrate across media environments largely intact.

### Platform-specific vernaculars: TikTok and Instagram

A final cluster asks the inverse question—how do specific platform affordances inflect conspiratorial narrative construction? [[Marwick2025-ov]] shows ConspiracyTok creators—disproportionately young, non-White, and/or female—building "populist knowledge production" through visible identity markers, curated visual evidence, and a "generous epistemology" that welcomes divergent truths, complicating the stereotype of the conspiracy theorist and revealing how marginalized standpoint epistemology and disinformation can be discursively intertwined. [[Bosch2024-hj]] isolates a narrower affordance—sound—as an underexamined vector of TikTok-specific computational propaganda around the Ukraine invasion, while [[Hollingshead2026-vx]], though centered on immigration rather than conspiracy per se, offers a useful comparative data point: it finds TikTok's affordances used symmetrically across ideological positions, suggesting a shared platform vernacular rather than affordance-driven polarization—a finding that qualifies claims elsewhere in this topic about platform features straightforwardly amplifying extremity. [[Gardam2025-er]] makes a methodological argument with theoretical stakes: because platforms like Instagram are image-centric, text-first discourse analysis methods systematically miss how conspiracy narratives are constructed visually, necessitating a visual-first approach—a call answered empirically by [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s attention to curated visual evidence and implicitly by [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s attention to sonic modality. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] serves as an earlier, adjacent case—dissenting hashtag discourse after Charlie Hebdo—illustrating that discursive counter-narrative strategies on social media predate and structurally resemble later conspiracist boundary-work, even outside explicitly conspiratorial content.

### Synthesis

Read together, these thirteen papers trace an arc from causal, media-effects questions (which channels transmit belief, and to whom) toward increasingly cultural and infrastructural accounts (what narrative and platform logics make conspiracy content *cohere*, *travel*, and *feel true*). The throughline is a shared skepticism toward single-cause explanations—whether "bad platforms," "gullible audiences," or "false claims"—in favor of models that integrate predispositions, network structure, narrative form, and platform vernacular as jointly constitutive of conspiracy belief across the contemporary media environment.
