# LLM-Assisted Computational Content Analysis

## The methodological problem: can LLMs stand in for human coders?

The starting point for this literature is a deceptively simple question: when a large language model classifies, scales, or clusters text, does it produce something communication researchers can trust as measurement? [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the emerging landscape of LLM-based annotation and synthesis and frames the core tension that runs through nearly every paper here — the promise of scale versus the need for validated methodology. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] answer this empirically for a concrete case: an LLMs-in-the-loop pipeline for classifying and clustering Italian political news on Facebook. Giglietto shows that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large produces more semantically coherent clusters than the Italian-specific UmBERTo model, extending prior English-language findings about embedding quality to a non-English setting; Marino then reflects on what it actually takes to validate such a pipeline once fine-tuned classification, embedding-based clustering, and LLM-generated cluster labels are chained together, arguing that each stage needs its own tailored validation protocol and that crowdsourced raters are no longer adequate benchmarks once LLMs routinely outperform low-skilled human coders. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] pushes this insight further, reframing the LLM not as a classifier to be validated against ground truth but as an "analytical collaborator" whose disagreements with a deductive codebook (Semetko and Valkenburg's frames, applied to Chilean news) expose ambiguities and historically contingent theoretical assumptions that the codebook itself needs to confront — a genuinely different validation logic, oriented toward theory-building rather than accuracy scores.

Several papers push validation into more adversarial or fine-grained terrain. [[Brown2025-jk]] tests whether LLM annotations of contentious material (toxicity, offensiveness, politeness) systematically diverge from particular demographic groups, finding that item difficulty — human annotator disagreement itself — swamps demographic bias as a predictor of LLM-human agreement, a finding that reframes "bias" debates around fairness in subjective annotation. [[Paci2025-ag]] stress-tests LLMs on a much harder inferential task: recovering implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political speech, where even the best model falls well short of expert human interpretation, showing that classification competence does not straightforwardly extend to pragmatic reasoning. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] similarly finds that LLMs are unreliable political fact-checkers on their own — even with reasoning and web search — but that curated retrieval-augmented context closes much of the gap, a finding that echoes Gomez-Zara's and Marino's emphasis on human-curated scaffolding as the difference between failure and workable performance. Together these papers converge on a shared lesson: LLMs are not oracle annotators; they are components whose reliability is task-, domain-, and context-dependent, and must be established anew for each application rather than assumed from general capability.

## Embeddings, clustering, and the search for latent structure

A second cluster of papers treats embeddings, rather than prompted classification, as the analytical workhorse, extending validation concerns to unsupervised and semi-supervised discovery of topics, frames, and narratives. [[Fan2025-ut]] identifies a fundamental confound in embedding-based similarity measures — that source, language, and style leak into vector space and distort clustering and retrieval — and shows that a simple linear erasure technique (LEACE) can dramatically improve cross-lingual and cross-source clustering without harming out-of-domain performance, a low-cost fix directly relevant to any AoIR-style cross-country pipeline pooling multilingual corpora. [[Waight2025-al]] extends embedding-plus-LLM pipelines to a more ambitious estimand: "narrative similarity," the extent to which texts make the same claims about the same events, which the authors show is conflated by cruder methods like exact text reuse or topic modeling, with real substantive consequences for tracking how Russian disinformation about Ukrainian "biolabs" diffused into mainstream and low-quality U.S. outlets. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes a complementary "practice mapping" approach that uses vector embeddings of network actions, rather than text, to overcome the visualization limits of conventional multimodal network analysis — extending the embedding paradigm from content to behavior. [[Sarmiento2025-as]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] both push unsupervised and multi-modal framing analysis beyond predefined categories, the former combining machine learning and network analysis to surface emergent frames in polarized discourse, the latter arguing that text-only framing analysis systematically misses visual and cross-modal editorial choices in news coverage of contested topics like gun violence. [[Gardam2025-er]] makes the multimodal case most forcefully for a genuinely image-first platform, arguing that climate-denial discourse on Instagram cannot be understood through text-centric methods at all. Read together, this cluster documents a methodological arc from text-only, single-modality, single-language pipelines toward architectures that explicitly model confounds, multiple modalities, and narrative-level rather than merely topical structure.

## From measurement to substantive inference

A third set of papers puts these validated pipelines to work answering communication-research questions, and in doing so tests their limits under real-world conditions. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to classify collective-action frames across half a million Proud Boys Telegram messages, linking online framing dynamics to offline violence via Granger causality — an example of supervised classification enabling longitudinal causal inference at a scale no human coding team could achieve. [[Larsson2026-ro]] demonstrates a lighter-weight, zero-shot GPT-4 sentiment classification (validated against human coders) applied to a decade of non-English Facebook posts, showing that LLM-assisted content analysis can support longitudinal, non-English political communication research that was previously prohibitively costly. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes Greimas' actantial model via an open-weight LLM to measure "narrative polarisation" on YouTube, finding — counter to echo-chamber expectations — that user comments actually converge on shared surface narratives even as deeper narrative motifs still encode partisan difference, a subtle finding only detectable because the LLM pipeline could recover role-structure rather than mere sentiment or topic. [[Nenno2025-xa]] scales computational news-values detection across twenty-four countries to test the WEIRD-bias critique directly, finding mostly small but non-trivial cross-cultural differences and outlier clustering around the U.S. and Brazil — a genuinely cross-country application in the AoIR collaborative spirit. [[Lee2026-je]] and [[Waight2026-ts]] both use LLM inference to reveal what is otherwise hidden in text: the former shows LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical discourse via subtle cultural lexical cues, raising privacy concerns; the latter shows that state media control is embedded in training corpora and shapes model outputs across 37 countries, meaning the very tool being validated for content analysis is itself a carrier of the political biases researchers are trying to study — a reflexive turn that complicates the entire enterprise.

## Qualitative synthesis and human-in-the-loop design

Two papers extend LLM-assisted analysis into qualitative and interpretive territory rather than purely quantitative classification. [[Ober2026-vd]] integrates topic modeling, LLM-based cluster labeling, and human codebook refinement to analyze teacher focus-group transcripts, explicitly defending topic modeling's mathematical transparency as a check against LLM opacity while treating the LLM as a labeling aid rather than an interpreter. This dovetails with [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]]'s "analytical collaborator" framing above, and together they stake out a position — human-in-the-loop, interpretive authority retained — that is echoed methodologically across much of this topic area, even where the underlying tasks (interview coding vs. news framing) differ substantially.

## Infrastructure, data access, and the collaborative pipeline problem

A distinct but closely linked strand addresses not model validity but the data infrastructure on which any LLM-assisted pipeline depends — directly relevant to cross-country AoIR-style collaborations. [[Yang2026-tq]] documents, via its own human-in-the-loop LLM annotation pipeline, that empirical social media research plateaued and declined after 2022 as platform APIs closed, and that research usage remains skewed toward Twitter/X and Facebook relative to actual platform popularity. [[Rieder2025-ju]] audits the YouTube Data API's search endpoint and finds it "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay and inconsistency that undermine replicability and DSA-mandated systemic-risk research. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this critique to the post-API "clean room" regime (the Meta Content Library), arguing that these environments, while addressing legitimate privacy concerns, structurally privilege well-resourced, code-fluent, quantitative Global North teams and foreclose qualitative and cross-platform analysis — a direct threat to the kind of collaborative, mixed-methods, multi-country pipelines this topic area otherwise champions. These three papers collectively argue that no amount of methodological sophistication in LLM annotation compensates for degraded or inequitably distributed access to the underlying data.

## Reflexivity, epistemics, and the limits of automation

A final group of papers steps back to interrogate the epistemic and ethical stakes of this entire research program. [[Balluff2026-if]] offers the most direct critique, arguing that LLM adoption in political communication research has outpaced reflection on reproducibility, environmental cost, corporate dependency, and bias, and calling for a trade-off mindset favoring smaller, open, task-appropriate models — a useful counterweight to the encoder-embedding and prompting pipelines documented elsewhere in this collection. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] theorizes why traditional fact-checking epistemologies (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) fail for generative AI outputs, proposing "emergent facts" as a new category — a conceptual companion to DeVerna's empirical finding that LLMs need curated evidence to fact-check reliably at all. At the frontier, [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] extend the validation question to AI's role in the research process itself: the former benchmarks AI coding agents' ability to reproduce published social science findings (finding high accuracy but also documenting sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting), while the latter reports on Google's agentic Paper Assistant Tool for scientific peer review. Both signal that the methodological questions raised throughout this topic — reliability, validation protocol design, human oversight, susceptibility to bias and confirmation — are migrating from content analysis proper into the infrastructure of scholarly verification itself, suggesting this is not a closed methodological debate but one whose scope is actively expanding.
