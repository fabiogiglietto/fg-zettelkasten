# Computational Methods & LLMs for Content Analysis

## Validating the LLM-in-the-loop: from convenience to accountability

The dominant meta-narrative across this corpus is a shift from naïve enthusiasm about LLMs as content-analysis tools toward increasingly rigorous validation protocols. [[Balluff2026-if]] frames the terms of the debate: LLM adoption in political communication has outpaced reflection on reproducibility, environmental cost, corporate dependency, and bias, and the authors call for a "least-resource-necessary" ethic rather than reflexive scaling-up. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] answers this call empirically, documenting a three-stage LLMs-in-the-loop pipeline (classification, embedding, cluster labeling) for Italian political Facebook data and showing that each stage needs its own tailored validation, because expert human judgment—not crowd workers—is required once model outputs approach or exceed crowd-worker quality. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] pushes reproducibility itself into the LLM era, benchmarking coding agents (Claude Code, Codex) on their ability to rerun published social-science analyses; near-ceiling accuracy is qualified by evidence of sycophantic "specification search" once agents are shown the paper's reported results, a caution that resonates with [[Brown2025-jk]]'s finding that LLM-human disagreement in subjective annotation is driven far more by item difficulty (entropy) than by demographic bias—undercutting a common fairness critique while reinforcing the need for task-level, not model-level, audits. [[Tan2024-vl]] supplies the taxonomy underlying all of this work, organizing the field around generation, assessment, and downstream use of LLM-generated labels. [[Matias2025-px]] extends the validation conversation beyond technical benchmarking, arguing that participatory, lived-experience input can materially strengthen the *science* (not just the legitimacy) of AI evaluation at each stage from equipoise to interpretation—an argument that dovetails with [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]'s parallel point that survey-sample "quality" is multidimensional and cannot be reduced to a single validity metric.

## Choosing representations: embeddings, clustering, and their failure modes

A cluster of methodological papers interrogates the representational substrate beneath text-analysis pipelines. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] jointly establish that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large outperforms the Italian-specific UmBERTo for clustering political news, extending English-language findings to a non-English setting and demonstrating that clustering-algorithm choice (K-means vs. HDBSCAN) matters as much as embedding choice. [[Fan2025-ut]] generalizes the underlying problem: pretrained embeddings encode observed confounders (source, language) that bias similarity estimates, and linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove this contamination cheaply without degrading out-of-distribution performance—a technique directly relevant to any multi-source or multilingual corpus assembled for the clustering work above. [[Arminio2025-tw]] takes the representational question into the visual domain, showing that vision-language models generate connotative textual descriptions of images that, once embedded, produce more socially meaningful (if less denotatively precise) clusters than CNN pipelines—reframing "semantic clustering" as inherently a question of *which* meaning (denotative vs. connotative) a method captures. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] extends multimodal validation to person-level recognition tasks, finding GPT-4o dramatically outperforms specialized computer-vision models for face verification and person counting in campaign imagery, while also surfacing a possible gender bias in the older specialized models it replaces.

## Positioning and scaling political text and actors

A distinct line of work asks whether LLMs can recover latent political quantities—ideology, alignment, narrative position—without full supervised training. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes "ask-and-average" prompting to place texts on policy dimensions, validating strong correlations with established scaling benchmarks across languages. [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent ideology estimation from actors to content itself, inferring YouTube video ideology from Reddit sharing patterns via correspondence analysis. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer *hidden* political alignment even from nonpolitical text, exploiting culturally politicized lexical cues and outperforming supervised baselines—raising privacy concerns that echo through to [[Bouchaud2026-lr]], which finds that X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear left–right direction in its user embeddings, independent of demographics, blurring the active/passive profiling distinction under data-protection law. Together these papers suggest ideological signal is not merely present but pervasive and recoverable across nearly any behavioral or textual trace, a theme [[Waight2026-ts]] radicalizes further by showing that state media control itself leaks into LLM training data and shapes model outputs cross-linguistically—embedding effects converge with representational bias to political power in geopolitically consequential ways.

## Classifying political and coordinated content at scale

The largest practical use case in this literature is supervised or LLM-assisted classification of political and extremist content. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to classify collective-action frames in Proud Boys Telegram messages, linking online framing dynamics to offline violence via Granger causality—an early exemplar of coupling a validated classifier to a substantive causal claim. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Marino2026-he]] both deploy GPT-4o as a hybrid classifier (with human validation) for longitudinal sentiment/affect analysis of partisan Facebook communication, in Norway and Brazil respectively, and both findings unsettle static assumptions: Larsson shows negative sentiment's engagement advantage is stable but leader-driven, while Marino shows that hyperpartisan "echo chambers" are in fact temporally volatile, reshaped by discrete political shocks rather than behaving as fixed communities. [[Meher2025-qb]] demonstrates that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) makes conflict-event classification (à la GTD) accessible on consumer hardware, with especially large gains for rare event categories—directly answering the resource-constraint concerns raised in [[Balluff2026-if]]. At the network/coordination level, [[Minici2024-tf]] proposes a graph-foundation-model detector (IOHunter) generalizing across information operations, [[Orlando2025-ul]] models how coordination *emerges* endogenously among LLM agents simulating influence campaigns without explicit instruction, Bastos2025-ol extends detection to visual self-presentation of troll accounts, and [[Pante2025-pq]] and [[Kim2026-br]] both offer corrective moves: Pante re-audits claimed inter-state coordination with stronger controls, while Kim's explainable hierarchical classifier reveals that Korean troll operations rely on morally condemning rhetoric rather than direct foreign-state praise—each paper pushing the field toward more skeptical, better-controlled detection claims.

## Fact-checking, misinformation, and the epistemics of AI content

A parallel thread interrogates LLMs' reliability for veracity judgments and the conceptual status of AI-generated claims. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows that even reasoning- and search-augmented LLMs perform poorly at political fact-checking absent curated retrieval, and that search-enabled models show a left-leaning citation skew—an empirical counterpoint to [[Waight2026-ts]]'s finding of pro-regime skew in different linguistic contexts, jointly suggesting that ideological "bias" in LLM outputs is multidimensional and context-dependent rather than unidirectional. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes a related but distinct problem—information *voids*, not falsehoods—showing empirically that these voids correlate with reduced credible content and increased misinformation prevalence. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] theorizes the deeper epistemological problem underlying both: generative AI outputs are "emergent facts" that existing evidentiary, constructivist, and institutional fact categories cannot adequately evaluate, proposing new indicators (accuracy, verifiability, contextual relevance, consistency). [[Triedman2025-uy]] provides a concrete case study of these stakes, finding Grokipedia to be a heavily derivative but more source-unreliable AI rewrite of Wikipedia, disproportionately so on controversial and political topics.

## Narrative and framing beyond keyword coding

Several papers push text-as-data methods past frame/topic classification toward structural narrative analysis. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes Greimas' actantial model via an LLM to measure "narrative polarisation" in Israeli-Palestinian YouTube discourse, finding that comments paradoxically converge on surface narrative even as deeper motifs remain partisan—suggesting narrative is partially orthogonal to audience ideology. [[Waight2025-al]] formalizes "narrative similarity" as its own estimand, distinct from lexical, topical, or semantic similarity, and shows via a claim-distillation LLM pipeline that low-quality U.S. outlets share narratives with Russian state media at rates invisible to exact-text-reuse methods. [[Sadler2025-vu]] offers the theoretical counterweight, arguing that disinformation studies has used "narrative" too loosely and proposing a hermeneutic-realist, ethically inflected alternative to the true/false binary—directly relevant to how [[Elfes2026-jb]] and [[Waight2025-al]] should interpret their own narrative measures. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] both push framing analysis past predefined categories, into multimodal and fully unsupervised territory respectively, while [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] reframes the LLM's role explicitly as an "analytical collaborator" that surfaces ambiguities in deductive codebooks (e.g., Semetko and Valkenburg's frames) rather than a classifier to be benchmarked—closing the loop back to the validation debates opened by [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] and [[Balluff2026-if]]. [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Hartmann2025-px]] round out this cluster: the former as a concrete hybrid topic-modeling-plus-LLM workflow for qualitative interview coding, the latter as a systematic review showing that echo-chamber research's persistent disagreements stem largely from inconsistent operationalization—an argument that could be read as a diagnosis of the very methodological pluralism visible across this whole topic area.

## Persuasion and conversational AI as both tool and hazard

A tightly linked experimental literature asks what makes conversational AI persuasive and what that implies for democratic communication. [[Costello2024-bg]] establishes the foundational result—personalized, evidence-dense AI dialogue durably reduces conspiracy belief—which [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematizes across 19 models and hundreds of issues, isolating information density as the central mechanism and revealing a persuasion–accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, showing AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the human-throughput constraint experiment confirming information density (not rapport) as the mechanism. [[Lin2025-xp]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] extend these findings to real elections and contested policy domains respectively, both finding persuasion is strongest among skeptics/out-partisans and largely fact-driven rather than manipulative, while noting a partisan asymmetry in claim accuracy. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] supplies an important boundary condition: perceived political bias in the model attenuates its persuasive power by roughly a quarter, implicating source credibility as an additional lever alongside those catalogued by Hackenburg. [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Dubey2026-bl]] explore adjacent applications—conversational inoculation against misinformation and "balanced" chatbots for conspiracy-prone users—both finding that interactive, adaptive dialogue outperforms static content, though Dubey's counterintuitive result (higher-conspiracy users trust the chatbot more) complicates simple models of motivated resistance.

## Reception, disclosure, and the politics of AI-mediated communication

A final cluster examines how audiences and institutions respond to AI's growing presence in political and journalistic communication. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] documents that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs in news is real but far from dominant, varying by outlet more than region. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] both study AI disclosure effects in journalism, arriving at complementary findings: undisclosed AI-written text is perceived as equal in quality to human writing, but disclosure produces short-term curiosity rather than durable trust, and task-specific AI disclosures (especially for fact-checking) consistently penalize trustworthiness. [[Wedel2026-ix]] reframes the underlying audience concept itself, proposing "informativeness" as a platform-agnostic replacement for institutional notions of news among young adults. [[Manovich2026-ih]] steps back to theorize generative AI as a creative medium with built-in "media cognition," useful context for understanding why AI text and image outputs exhibit the aesthetic and stylistic conservatism documented empirically elsewhere in this topic (e.g., Triedman's Grokipedia analysis, Nguyen's metaphor study). At the level of infrastructure and governance, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] surveys how LLM-enabled "digital public square" technologies (bridging systems, collective dialogue tools, AI-assisted moderation) could reshape online deliberation, while [[Jayaram2026-wd]] extends automation to the scientific process itself via an agentic peer-review tool—both papers gesturing toward futures in which the pipelines validated elsewhere in this topic become embedded governance infrastructure rather than research instruments. Grounding these speculative and receptive strands are two papers on tangible downstream stakes of computational content analysis: [[Kim2026-wg]] uses direct ad-exposure tracking to show geo-racially targeted digital voter suppression measurably depressed 2016 turnout, and [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] couples a compartmental epidemic model with geolocated antivaccine tweet data to estimate a causal, quantifiable death toll from online misinformation—demonstrating, alongside [[Allen2025-ot]]'s discussion of platform-independent experimental designs, that the methodological investments surveyed throughout this topic are ultimately in service of establishing causal, not merely descriptive, claims about how computationally mediated political content shapes real-world outcomes.
