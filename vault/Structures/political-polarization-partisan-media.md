# Political Polarization & Partisan Media Ecosystems

# Political Polarization & Partisan Media Ecosystems

## The problem of definition: what are we even measuring?

Before synthesizing empirical claims, several papers in this collection interrogate the conceptual foundations of polarization research itself. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] finds that depolarization recommendations in the literature are systematically disconnected from the evidence scholars themselves produce, resting instead on implicit deliberative-democratic ideals. [[Hartmann2025-px]] extends this diagnosis to echo chamber research specifically, arguing that contradictory findings largely reflect heterogeneous operationalizations rather than divergent realities. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates this empirically: applying specification curve analysis to 504 measurement combinations of media slant and polarization, they show that methodological choices dramatically inflate or deflate effect sizes (though not their direction), with audience-based measures systematically overstating relationships relative to content-based ones. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] adds a geographic critique, noting the field's disciplinary fragmentation and overwhelming concentration on US/Western cases — a gap this very topic cluster partially addresses through its Italian and Brazilian material. Together these pieces suggest a field maturing into self-scrutiny, aware that "polarization" is not one phenomenon but a family of loosely related measurement constructs.

## Rethinking mechanisms: narrative, identity, and affect over information

A second cluster pushes back against information-centric models of polarization and misinformation, arguing that identity and affect do the real work. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reconceptualizes disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative* rather than discrete false claims, explaining why fact-checking and platform-bound moderation fail to contain it. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes a cognate idea computationally, introducing "narrative polarisation" — divergence in how partisan groups cast actors into roles (via Greimas' actantial model) — and finding, counterintuitively, that YouTube comments *reduce* surface narrative divergence relative to videos even as deeper narrative motifs preserve partisan difference. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] similarly argues that conspiracy cultures (the "Great Reset") cohere through symbolic boundary work and reclaimed stigma ("we, the conspiracy theorists") rather than platform affordances or ideological content per se, uniting otherwise disparate secular-libertarian and religious-Christian publics. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a parallel identity-driven bias — the "hostile misinformation effect," a misinformation-domain analogue of the hostile media effect — whereby partisans believe their in-group is disproportionately targeted by falsehood, moderated by identity strength and right-wing orientation. [[Schulz2026-ts]] complicates the picture further by showing that much apparent online polarization is itself an artifact of self-selection into posting by ideological extremes, meaning visible discourse overstates underlying opinion division. Read together, this cluster argues that polarization lives less in propositional disagreement than in the affective and narrative construction of identity and grievance.

## Elite strategy and epistemic authority

Several papers examine how political elites strategically exploit fragmented media environments to build or contest authority. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians pursuing distinct "epistemic authority" strategies during the Manaus COVID crisis — objectivity-mimicking derivative reporting, authority-claiming from Bolsonaro allies, and locally-brokered proximity narratives — illustrating how politicians increasingly compete with journalists as narrators. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" traces how the Critical Race Theory issue let ideologically extreme Republicans forge ties to hyper-partisan digital surrogates, converting media attention into intra-party factional leverage. [[Bennett2025-xs]] scales this argument up theoretically, arguing that "digital surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Stop the Steal, Proud Boys) have become a missing institutional link between disinformation and democratic backsliding — bridging technocentric and institutionalist accounts and drawing explicit parallels to European illiberal movements. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] both examine "fake news" itself as a rhetorical weapon: the former shows US alternative outlets (Newsmax, Occupy Democrats) using the term to redraw Hallin's spheres of legitimacy, the latter proposes a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model for Italian newspapers, treating fake-news accusations as negative-campaigning tools rather than epistemic claims. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] situates all of this within a broader claim that platforms bear direct responsibility for global democratic backsliding.

## Platform architecture, algorithms, and infrastructural effects

A substantial empirical thread examines how platform design and governance choices causally shape partisan visibility. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment is pivotal here: switching X's algorithmic feed on shifts users' attitudes rightward via a persistence mechanism (algorithm-induced following of conservative accounts that survives deactivation), reconciling earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies. [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents a related asymmetry following Brazil's X ban: conservative users disproportionately circumvented it while liberal users went silent, producing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet." [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] scrutinize X's crowdsourced Community Notes system, finding Republican posts flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats' (undermining bias-based justifications for abandoning fact-checkers) while simultaneously showing the bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing content across countries. [[Iris2026-pg]] extends the visibility question cross-nationally, finding the Radical Right systematically overrepresented in European election coverage regardless of outlet orientation. [[Copland2025-em]] traces how Sky News Australia became a transnational right-wing propaganda node via differentiated native-Facebook versus YouTube strategies, and [[Balluff2026-bv]]'s Austrian "Inseratenaffäre" study finds government-advertising capture increasing a chancellor's visibility (though not favorability) in a client outlet — media capture operating through a "pay positive to go negative" mechanism even in an established democracy. [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates alternative-media distinctiveness assumptions by showing German alternative and legacy outlets reference largely overlapping named entities, differing mainly in relational network structure and media self-reference.

## Echo chambers, curation, and the question of cross-cutting exposure

The classic echo-chamber debate is substantially revised by newer work. [[Bakshy2015-rn]], the foundational Facebook study, found individual choice curtails cross-cutting exposure more than algorithms do — a finding [[Green2025-ap]] complicates by showing that domain-level partisanship measures obscure vast story-level heterogeneity, meaning "moderate" sources often mask deeply divergent partisan audiences per story ("curation bubbles"). [[Dehghan2026-sy]] offers a structurally different picture on Reddit, finding minimal cross-ideological overlap even between discursively similar subreddits and arguing polarization is "sedimented" as a precondition of platform architecture, moderation, and discourse jointly — not resolvable through more deliberative contact alone, a conclusion in tension with [[Esau2025-tf]]'s normative proposal of deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening as an antidote. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] extend the audience-overlap approach cross-nationally, finding alternative-mainstream media overlap shaped by media-system type and populist parties' institutional access rather than by any fixed property of "alternativeness." [[Mosleh2024-op]] reminds us that most of this evidence derives from Twitter/Facebook alone, and that engagement patterns diverge substantially across the seven-platform landscape.

## Emotion, engagement, and affective community dynamics

Several papers directly probe the mechanics of affective polarization within partisan communities. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] both examine Facebook's emotional reaction affordances, finding anger reliably drives sharing and disproportionately benefits populist actors across Scandinavian and broader contexts. [[Marino2026-he]], filed centrally within the Brazilian strand alongside [[Rodarte2026-dk]] and [[Ventura2026-yc]], offers a crucial longitudinal corrective: rather than stable echo chambers, a three-year study of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook networks reveals highly volatile affective engagement tied to political events (Lula's inauguration, the January 2023 coup attempt), with declining emotional intensity and rising internal debate — suggesting hyperpartisan communities are dynamically contested rather than sealed. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]'s earlier Italian election study anticipates this nuance, showing that populist (M5S) supporters combine insular sharing with active contestation of adversarial content rather than pure self-segregation, while [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s Charlie Hebdo case and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s hybrid TV-Twitter study of the 2013 Italian election ground these dynamics in earlier moments of the country's polarized-pluralist media system.

## Method and measurement innovations

Finally, a methodological throughline runs across the cluster, offering new instruments for future polarization research: [[Eady2025-vm]]'s URL-based joint ideology scaling of media, politicians, and publics; [[Le-Mens2025-qz]]'s LLM-based "ask and average" method for positioning political texts; [[Lee2026-je]]'s demonstration that LLMs infer political alignment even from nonpolitical discourse (raising the same micro-targeting risks flagged by [[Bennett2025-xs]] and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]); and exploratory computational pipelines for framing and discourse analysis in [[Arora2025-tx]], [[Sarmiento2025-as]], and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]], the latter explicitly critiquing prior work's oversimplified, single-country, outlet-level treatment of partisan news circulation. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]]'s study of UK scientist-citation patterns and [[Lieu2025-nl]]'s experimental test of climate misinformation content types both show how polarization dynamics extend into science communication, with [[Karlsson2026-hd]] demonstrating that even *narratives about* election interference — independent of interference's actual efficacy — can asymmetrically deepen affective polarization. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] closes the loop by tracing how activist language itself (from #StayWoke to "culture wars") becomes politicized across platforms, exemplifying the narrative-capture dynamics this whole literature increasingly foregrounds over narrowly informational accounts of polarization.
