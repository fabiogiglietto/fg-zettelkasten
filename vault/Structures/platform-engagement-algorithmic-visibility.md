# Platform Engagement & Algorithmic Visibility

Looking at these 40 papers, I'll build a structure note that traces the actual argumentative arc: from measurement disputes, through mechanism, to normative stakes.

## Platform Engagement & Algorithmic Visibility

## The measurement problem: is the algorithm doing anything at all?

The foundational tension running through this collection is methodological before it is political: how do we even detect whether curation systems shape what people see, distinct from what people *choose*? [[Bakshy2015-rn]] set the terms early, showing that Facebook's ranking algorithm modestly dampens cross-cutting exposure but that individual clicking behavior dampens it more — a finding that has haunted every subsequent study as a null-effect baseline to explain away or overturn. [[Brown2026-br]] revives this design logic for YouTube, formally distinguishing echo chambers, rabbit holes, and radicalization pathways, and finds — again — that apparent ideological sorting is mostly demand-side, while the algorithm's real, robust effect is a content-adjacency "rabbit hole" plus a platform-wide moderate-conservative tilt applied to everyone. [[McNally2025-dn]] pushes back on the "black box" framing itself, showing that Facebook's News Feed changes leave detectable, lagged fingerprints in engagement data — an argument that curation is not unknowable, merely under-audited. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] complicates the picture further: exposure to untrustworthy sources on Facebook/Instagram is low on average, concentrated in a minority, and experimentally inert on attitudes — reinforcing the null-effects camp. Against this, [[Gauthier2026-iq]] delivers the sharpest causal counter-evidence: switching X users onto the algorithmic feed shifts political attitudes rightward, but switching it off does *not* reverse the effect, because the algorithm operates by reshaping *who users follow*, not just what they momentarily see — a persistence mechanism that reconciles Meta's earlier null findings with X's apparent effects. [[Brady2026-ln]] extends this reconciliation project experimentally on Bluesky, reframing the debate away from direct attitude change toward algorithm-mediated distortion of perceived social norms, and demonstrates that alternative ranking designs (a "diversified extremity" feed) can reduce toxic amplification without attitudinal engineering.

## Mechanisms of differential visibility: partisanship, quality, and platform governance as active variables

A second cluster asks not "does the algorithm matter" but "along which dimensions does it discriminate visibility." [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] is the anchor here: across 130,000 viral URLs on Facebook, partisan-audience intensity dampens the shares-to-views relationship while journalistic quality amplifies it, and — crucially — both effects fluctuate sharply around known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures, showing Facebook as an active, event-responsive curator rather than a stable pipe. This complements [[Bouchaud2026-lr]]'s discovery that X's recommender embeds a near-perfect linear encoding of Left-Right ideology, entirely inadvertently, with concrete consequences for "Who to Follow" suggestions and a proposed method for stripping it out. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] similarly interrogates a specific algorithmic regime change (post-Musk X) and shows that apparent right-leaning advantage dissolves once you control for agitation and Musk-adjacency — a finding that reframes "political bias" as "engagement-and-proximity-to-owner bias." [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] narrow in on the reaction-based layer of engagement specifically: Love suppresses sharing while Angry promotes it, and populist actors are disproportionately good at eliciting anger — a pattern [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] operationalizes at the level of whole partisan media ecosystems in Italy, showing that insular, populist-aligned sources get shared more per comment while cross-cutting sources get contested more. [[Marino2026-he]] extends this into longitudinal terrain, showing that even a hyperpartisan Bolsonaro-aligned Facebook network is not a stable echo chamber but an event-sensitive affective system whose emotional intensity and boundary permeability shift dramatically around political shocks. Rounding out this cluster, [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] complicate any single-platform story: untrustworthy-URL prevalence rises in European election years without proportional rises in views, and engagement with partisan/low-quality news diverges meaningfully across seven platforms, undermining Twitter/Facebook-centric generalization.

## Content quality, curation, and the story-versus-source problem

A related methodological correction concerns unit of analysis. [[Green2025-ap]] argues that domain-level partisanship scores systematically mischaracterize story-level audiences — moderate-looking outlets conceal wildly heterogeneous partisan uptake story by story — a point [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] generalizes into a call for multi-level "political cartography" of news circulation. [[Eady2025-vm]] contributes the measurement infrastructure this agenda needs: a shared URL-sharing scale that jointly locates media, politicians, and ordinary users, revealing that political extremity (not just party) predicts sharing volume and polarization, and that reduced electoral competition breeds more extreme sharing. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] applies a cognate gatekeeping lens to health news, finding that Google News Italia's aggregation choices and Facebook engagement diverge by topic rather than source — engagement is driven by "emotional" versus "technical" framing more than outlet identity, echoing the Reactions literature's emphasis on affect over source credibility.

## Institutional and citizen interaction with platformed campaigns

A more applied strand examines how citizens and institutions actually interact with political communication once it is platform-mediated. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] and [[Knupfer2025-vt]] bookend a decade of hybrid-media-system research: the former shows Italian second-screen Twitter practices around talk shows were narrow, TV-driven, and dominated by opinion rather than agenda-setting; the latter shows how, a decade on, digitally networked party factions actively cultivate hyper-partisan media ties to manufacture visibility and radicalize discourse from within. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] turn to paid and covert institutional campaigns specifically: ad delivery algorithms shape reach and cost in ways opaque to campaigners, while 2016-era voter suppression advertising was geo-racially targeted and produced measurable turnout declines concentrated exactly where targeting was heaviest — a reminder that "engagement" audits must sometimes measure *suppression* rather than amplification. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] supplies connective tissue for this whole strand, arguing that big data has blurred organizational boundaries, enabled microtargeting, and rewarded strategic amplification in ways that outstrip traditional exposure-measurement methods — a diagnosis this entire topic can be read as answering, paper by paper.

## Beyond Facebook/X: cross-platform and adjacent phenomena

Several papers push the topic's boundaries productively. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] offers the master framework for why platform comparison is hard: TikTok's "clustered publics" are structurally unlike Facebook's "networked publics," a shift that reframes visibility itself as interest-cluster assignment rather than follower-graph traversal — a claim empirically substantiated in [[Hollingshead2026-vx]]'s finding that TikTok immigration discourse skews pro-immigration and that affordance use is ideologically symmetric, unlike prior X-centric findings. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] examine how algorithmic proximity and recommendation-outpacing-moderation sustain far-right and deplatformed content (Tate) through affective softening and ambient circulation rather than overt endorsement. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] deliberately counters techno-centric explanations, arguing conspiracy communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity as much as through algorithmic sorting — a useful corrective to over-attributing engagement patterns to platform architecture alone. [[Dehghan2026-sy]], [[Kristensen2025-ni]], and [[Wang2026-ub]] extend the inquiry to Reddit, alternative platforms, and Mastodon migration respectively, each showing that fragmentation, polarization, and platform loyalty are entangled outcomes of design, moderation, and user agency rather than clean algorithmic effects. [[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] bring visibility auditing into the moderation-and-search domain (YouTube election search, Google AI Overviews), showing inconsistent authority-signaling and uneven guardrails against conspiratorial framing. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] identifies source-driven toxicity dynamics around influencer misinformation, and [[Smith2025-kc]] proposes a causal mechanism ("attention brokerage") for how amplification structurally reshapes network ties — a foundational finding for why visibility events (a retweet, a Reel, a share) propagate into durable structure. [[Tang2026-gu]] and [[Much2026-gu]] extend the engagement-visibility nexus into generative-AI content engineering and gendered podcast media spaces respectively, while [[Lukito2026-il]] shows that even within the "alt-tech" ecosystem (Truth Social vs. Twitter), right-leaning outlets practice selective, platform-specific curation — underscoring that cross-platformization is itself a visibility strategy. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] closes the loop by cataloguing how users' own algorithm literacy — itself unevenly distributed and inconsistently theorized — mediates all of the above.
