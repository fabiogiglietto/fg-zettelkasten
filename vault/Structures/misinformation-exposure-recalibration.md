# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

A defining move across this cluster of papers is the substitution of platform-scale measurement for anecdote and assumption. [[Budak2024-ef]] serves as something of a manifesto for the whole topic, arguing directly that public discourse about online misinformation has drifted from the evidence: average exposure is lower than claimed, algorithms are not the primary driver of that exposure, and causal links to broad societal harm are weaker than commonly asserted. The papers filed here largely operationalize and stress-test that thesis across platforms, countries, and content types.

### Concentration, not ubiquity

The single most recurring empirical pattern is that exposure to problematic content is heavily concentrated in a small subset of users rather than diffusely distributed. [[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion [[noauthor_undated-bm]] show that 49 deceptive networks around the 2020 US election reached tens of millions, but that three networks drove ~80% of that reach, that 1% of users accounted for the majority of exposures, and that network content made up only 0.3% of the political content an average exposed user saw. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds the same shape for untrustworthy-source content on Facebook/Instagram in 2020: low average exposure, concentrated among a minority, and no detectable attitudinal effects when exposure was experimentally reduced. [[Lyons2026-ca]] extends this logic to health misinformation, showing that exposure is rare overall but that the top 10% of users account for 77% of it—concentrated specifically among older adults with poorer discernment and more conspiracist worldviews, and driven by habitual cross-domain browsing rather than search or social referral. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] corroborates the age-skew and concentration pattern in a European context (Germany, France, Italy), showing that while the *share* of untrustworthy URLs shared has risen, the share of *views* has stayed comparatively stable, and that older cohorts dominate both exposure and sharing while under-34s are marginal. Together these papers suggest a durable empirical regularity: headline "prevalence" statistics obscure a highly skewed underlying distribution that maps population risk very differently than aggregate averages imply.

### Causal effects are smaller, rarer, or absent

A second thread interrogates whether documented exposure translates into measurable harm. [[Allcott2025-jb]] finds that experimentally removing political ads from Facebook/Instagram feeds for six weeks before the 2020 election had no detectable effect on knowledge, polarization, or perceptions. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] and the reanalysis in [[Appel2026-qr]]/[[noauthor_undated-bm]] similarly show that naive correlations between exposure and outcomes like truth discernment or belief in election illegitimacy evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled for, and that surviving estimates are fragile to plausible unobserved confounding. [[Ventura2025-sw]] pushes the causal-design approach into WhatsApp, a messaging context outside the usual feed-based frame, using a deactivation experiment to assess effects on knowledge and misinformation exposure in Brazil. Where causal effects *are* identified, they tend to be modest in absolute terms even when statistically robust: [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]]'s epidemic model attributes antivaccine tweets to roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and 8 additional deaths nationally—a real but epidemiologically small effect relative to the ~27 million Americans who grew hesitant during the period. [[Brown2026-br]]'s algorithmic audit of YouTube similarly finds no evidence for radicalization pathways and only mild, largely user-driven echo-chamber effects, despite popular narratives to the contrary—echoing the earlier, foundational finding in [[Bakshy2015-rn]] that individual click choices, not the News Feed algorithm, are the dominant force suppressing cross-cutting exposure on Facebook.

### Perceived versus actual misinformation, and what "everyday" misinformation looks like

Several papers complicate the very object of study by showing that what people *label* as misinformation diverges from what is actually false, and that "everyday" misinformation is far more mundane than the alarmist imagination of coordinated disinformation campaigns. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds that Australians flag content across business, celebrity, and crime topics—not just politics or health—that it is overwhelmingly text-based (news headlines), attributed mainly to mainstream and alternative outlets rather than fringe sources, and that independent verification found only 9% of flagged items were objectively false. [[Holt2026-zq]] extends this insight to Facebook's user-reporting system, showing that "False News" reports overwhelmingly target polarizing political content that contextualizes controversial events rather than outright falsehoods—user flagging functions as a statement of objection or disagreement more than a signal of factual inaccuracy, a finding with direct implications now that Meta has shifted toward Community Notes and user reports. [[Goel2025-iq]] complements this by showing that source-level classification schemes miss a large phenomenon in the opposite direction: factually true mainstream articles are disproportionately co-shared alongside fake news and contain misleading narrative structures at higher rates, with roughly twice the reach of fake news itself—meaning domain-blocklist approaches undercount misinformation's true footprint even as flagging systems overcount it. [[Nenno2025-xa]] shows that news-value differences between flagged and unflagged content are statistically real but small in magnitude, and that findings from WEIRD contexts (the US, Brazil as outliers) do not generalize cleanly to the 24-country sample studied. [[Renault2025-uh]] adds a further recalibration to partisan-asymmetry debates: even under a crowdsourced, cross-partisan-bridging system (Community Notes), Republican posts are flagged as misleading 2.3 times more often than Democratic posts, undercutting claims that fact-checker bias explains observed partisan asymmetries.

### Measurement infrastructure and its distortions

A methodological throughline concerns how the tools researchers use to measure misinformation can themselves manufacture the phenomena being studied. [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of NewsGuard shows that continuous trustworthiness scores are fairly stable since 2022, but that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs are highly sensitive to a handful of major sources crossing threshold—meaning published misinformation-prevalence estimates can shift dramatically based on database snapshot and coding choices alone, not real-world change. [[Green2025-ap]] makes an analogous point about domain-level partisanship scores: individual stories from "moderate" outlets can reach starkly divergent partisan audiences, so source-level measures mistake heterogeneity for moderation and understate the true partisan sorting of information. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies generalizes this diagnosis across the whole subfield, arguing that disagreement about whether echo chambers exist stems substantially from inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than from contradictory underlying reality. [[Mosleh2024-op]] adds that most of this measurement infrastructure has been built on Twitter/X and Facebook alone, and that engagement patterns with partisan and low-quality news diverge meaningfully once seven platforms are compared—a caution against generalizing single-platform findings. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s audit of Grokipedia exemplifies how a new information source can be interrogated with the same empirical rigor, revealing derivativeness from Wikipedia alongside a measurable tilt toward unreliable and blacklisted citations on controversial topics.

### Perception, narrative, and the social life of misinformation claims

A final cluster turns from exposure metrics to how misinformation is *perceived, discussed, and made meaningful*—showing that societal harm narratives are partly about discourse and identity rather than direct exposure effects. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] documents a "hostile misinformation effect": citizens across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland systematically believe their own political side is targeted by misinformation more than their opponents', a bias amplified by political interest and partisanship rather than by actual differential targeting. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows that media narratives *about* election interference can generate real polarization effects—but only asymmetrically, when audiences perceive their opponents as beneficiaries, not through simple aggregate alarm. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Wack2026-bt]] both argue that misinformation's meaning and impact are collectively negotiated rather than fixed at the point of a single post or fabricated artifact: election-fraud "deep stories" are sustained through evolving, elite-audience co-construction on Twitter, while in Kenya, comment-section skepticism or credulity substantially moderates whether a political deepfake damages a candidate's support. [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies a related psychological mechanism—modality-congruent carryover effects—by which difficulty authenticating one fake item shapes vulnerability to subsequent same-format fakes, adding nuance without validating maximalist deepfake-panic claims. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] directly tests and complicates the "seeing is believing" alarm around AI-generated visual disinformation, finding that video and image disinformation only outperform text when authentic footage plausibly exists (as with the Ukraine war), and that fact-checks reduce credibility about equally across modalities—suggesting generative-AI panic may be overstated relative to older, low-tech decontextualization tactics. [[Rossini2026-jn]] shows that belief in electoral misinformation predicts rising political intolerance in Brazil, illustrating that even where direct effects on knowledge are hard to find, misinformation belief can still track and amplify pre-existing political cleavages.

### Structural and systemic recalibrations

Two papers push the recalibration up to the level of platform structure and collective epistemics. [[Oswald2025-km]] frames the "production-consumption gap"—a small minority producing most political content while most users lurk—as a structural distortion that misleads both citizens and researchers who treat visible discourse as representative public opinion, a claim empirically reinforced by [[Schulz2026-ts]], whose decade of linked behavioral data, experiments, and 40-country survey evidence shows that self-selection into posting by ideologically extreme users manufactures "false polarization" that overstates real-world opinion divergence. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] examines diffusion trees from roughly a billion Facebook reshares to show that virality itself is concentrated and structurally shaped, reinforcing the broader concentration theme from a network-diffusion angle. [[Vincent_undated-re]] offers a counterpoint from continuous platform monitoring in Europe, showing that on TikTok specifically, mis/disinformation prevalence rose between measurement waves and low-credibility accounts retain an engagement advantage—a reminder that recalibration toward "limited harms" is not uniform across platforms and can coexist with genuine, worsening problem areas on specific services.

### Framing the field's next moves

Two conceptual papers close the loop by asking how the field should think about its own excesses and blind spots. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that dominant analogies—infodemic, information warfare, information pollution—each illuminate only part of a genuinely multilevel, complex adaptive system, and that single-analogy thinking produces interventions (like blunt media-literacy campaigns) that can backfire by increasing generalized distrust. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] extends this systems-level self-critique by importing lessons from climate psychology, noting that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world behavioral outcomes and urging the field toward contextual generalizability, integrated individual/system analysis, and organized resilience against the same vested-interest pressures that have long shaped climate denial research. Read together, these two pieces suggest that recalibrating the misinformation-harms narrative is not simply a matter of showing effects are smaller than claimed, but of rebuilding the field's conceptual and methodological infrastructure so that its claims—alarmist or reassuring—are anchored in exposure data, causal design, and cross-context generalizability rather than intuition or platform-specific anecdote.
