# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## The founding empirical correction: exposure is rare and radically concentrated

The backbone of this literature is a sequence of large-scale, individual-level studies that replaced speculation about "fake news" with direct measurement — and found the problem to be far smaller, and far more concentrated, than alarmist commentary assumed. [[Allcott2017-yz]] set the template: the average American adult saw and remembered only about one fake news story before the 2016 election, an order of magnitude too little to plausibly swing the result. [[Grinberg2019-ua]] and [[Guess2019-ym]] sharpened this into a distributional claim—fake news exposure and sharing on Twitter and Facebook were dominated by a tiny fraction of "supersharers" and older, highly conservative users, while most people's feeds remained overwhelmingly mainstream. [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Guess2021-ym]], and [[Allen2020-nj]] generalized the pattern across platforms and, crucially, across the *entire media ecosystem*: once television and passive consumption are counted, online fake news shrinks to roughly a tenth of a percent of the daily media diet, and even dedicated misinformation consumers are a "deep but narrow" minority. [[Allcott2019-gn]] showed this was not static—Facebook engagement with fake news fell sharply after 2016 even as Twitter's held steady, suggesting platform-specific interventions mattered. [[Eady2023-xg]] extended the concentration finding to the canonical worst case, Russian IRA influence operations, showing 1% of users absorbed 70% of exposures, dwarfed by domestic news exposure, with no detectable attitudinal or electoral effect. [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] bring this logic fully into the Meta-collaboration era: deceptive networks and untrustworthy sources reached millions in aggregate but were concentrated in a handful of actors and a thin slice of heavy users, with associations to downstream beliefs vanishing once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Lyons2026-ca]] pushes the concentration finding into a new domain—health misinformation—showing that low-credibility health content, too, is rare on average but disproportionately consumed by older adults already primed by conspiracist worldviews, arriving via cross-site referral rather than search or social media.

## From exposure to effects: does what little misinformation exists even matter?

A parallel cluster of large field experiments asks the harder causal question—even granting some exposure, does it change minds? The answer, repeatedly, is no. Meta's 2020 US Election Studies produced a remarkable run of precisely estimated nulls: removing political ads ([[Allcott2025-jb]]), stripping reshares from feeds ([[Guess2023-ai]]), replacing algorithmic ranking with reverse chronology ([[Guess2023-ur]]), and cutting exposure to like-minded sources by a third ([[Nyhan2023-gb]]) all substantially altered what users saw and did on-platform, yet moved almost none of the preregistered attitudinal or behavioral outcomes. [[Allen2024-av]] delivers perhaps the most striking reframe in the set: fact-checked vaccine misinformation was more persuasive per view, but reached so few people that factually accurate yet "vaccine-skeptical" mainstream content had 46 times the aggregate behavioral impact—relocating the public-health threat away from the canonical "misinformation" category entirely. [[Ventura2025-sw]] extends causal testing to a previously neglected venue, WhatsApp in Brazil, showing messaging platforms deserve the same experimental scrutiny as public feeds. Even ostensibly contrary findings reinforce the general caution against strong causal narratives: [[Bail2018-fk]] shows that *simply exposing* partisans to the opposing side can backfire into more extreme attitudes, complicating easy "just show more diverse content" fixes, while [[Brown2026-br]] formally decomposes YouTube "radicalization" claims and finds no evidence of an algorithmic radicalization pathway—only a mild, user-driven echo effect and a strong but ideologically neutral "rabbit hole" of topical stickiness. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] shift attention from direct persuasion to perception: *talk* about interference and misinformation can itself polarize people's sense of division, independent of whether any underlying campaign worked, and partisans systematically believe misinformation disproportionately targets their own side (a "hostile misinformation effect") — suggesting some documented "harms" are self-reinforcing meta-narratives rather than effects of the content itself.

## Echo chambers, filter bubbles, and the limits of algorithmic blame

A closely related strand interrogates the diagnosis that algorithms or platform architecture are the primary drivers of polarized exposure. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Flaxman2016-lm]] remain foundational: individual choice, not algorithmic ranking, is the dominant suppressor of cross-cutting content, and most news consumption is simply direct visits to mainstream outlets—leaving comparatively little room for either feared "filter bubbles" or hoped-for diversity gains. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] complicates this reassurance by showing that *at Facebook-wide scale*, URL-level segregation is far higher than web-browsing studies suggested, and asymmetrically concentrated in a homogeneously conservative corner of the ecosystem where most fact-checked misinformation also lives—indicating that aggregation level and granularity shape whether one sees comfort or alarm in the same platform. [[Hartmann2025-px]] steps back from any single finding to show, via systematic review, that the field's persistent disagreement over whether echo chambers even exist is substantially an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than a settled empirical fact. [[Green2025-ap]] offers a structural reconciliation: domain-level partisanship measures mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, so apparent "bubbles" partly reflect networked curation and identity performance rather than simple informational isolation. [[Schulz2026-ts]] and [[Oswald2025-km]] push the recalibration further upstream, arguing that visible online discourse is not representative public opinion at all—a small, more extreme, more vocal minority self-selects into posting, producing "false polarization" that misleads both ordinary citizens and researchers who treat social media traces as public-opinion proxies.

## Measurement artifacts: how data pipelines manufacture the appearance of a crisis

A distinct, methodologically reflexive cluster shows that some "misinformation prevalence" findings are themselves downstream of data engineering choices rather than social reality. [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] dissect the Social Science One/Meta URL Shares Dataset and show that its 100-public-shares censoring threshold—not differential-privacy noise—inflates apparent fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold relative to a representative panel, and generates suspiciously uniform cross-country "trends" that likely reflect global feed-ranking changes rather than local events. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs an analogous audit of NewsGuard, the source-trustworthiness database underlying much of this literature, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs (rather than continuous scores) can swing results dramatically based on a handful of borderline domains crossing threshold. [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Nenno2025-xa]] warn against generalizing from single-platform (usually Twitter/Facebook, usually US/WEIRD) studies: engagement patterns diverge sharply across seven platforms, and the news-value signatures of "misinformation" vary non-trivially between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries. Even where source lists are trusted, [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[Yang2023-cg]] argue the field's instruments miss large classes of the phenomenon entirely: true mainstream articles repurposed into misleading narratives reach roughly twice the audience of flagged fake news, and image-based misinformation—ignored by link-based classifiers—may constitute 20%+ of political image posts, a volume link-based audits structurally cannot see. [[Renault2025-uh]] uses X's crowdsourced Community Notes as a bias-resistant instrument and still finds a robust partisan asymmetry (Republicans flagged 2.3x more than Democrats), suggesting some asymmetries are not artifacts of fact-checker measurement after all—underscoring that "measurement reform" cuts in more than one direction.

## Beyond "fake news": misinformation as mundane, textual, and everyday

A complementary set of papers argues the entire conceptual frame of "fake news as exotic, fabricated content from fringe sites" misdescribes how ordinary people actually encounter falsehood. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds Australians' everyday misinformation concerns center on mainstream and alternative news outlets' clickbait and sensationalism, spanning business and celebrity topics far more than "hot button" politics, and expressed mostly through text, not video or AI content. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] shows that mainstream media, not fringe sites, are likely the dominant *disseminators* of fake news to the broader public, paradoxically amplifying stories even while debunking them. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] relocates the psychological mechanism away from partisan motivated reasoning toward inattention and a lack of careful reasoning—explaining the well-documented gap between what people believe and what they share. [[Budak2024-ef]] synthesizes much of this cluster explicitly, naming three public misperceptions (about prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm) that diverge from the evidence reviewed throughout this note. [[Hegarty2026-qr]] historicizes the broader alarmist mode itself, arguing that "internet decline narratives" are a situated cultural genre—performing blame-attribution and nostalgia work—that recurs across media history and should be studied rather than simply endorsed, a fitting meta-commentary on this entire topic.

## Where concern remains warranted, and how the field should recalibrate going forward

The set does not uniformly minimize harm. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] remains the canonical finding that falsehood, especially political falsehood, diffuses farther and faster than truth, driven by human novelty-seeking rather than bots—a genuine asymmetry that coexists with the concentration findings above. [[Keller2019-nk]]'s ground-truthed study of South Korean astroturfing shows coordination leaves detectable traces but that even a professionalized, state-resourced campaign had limited measurable discourse impact, reinforcing the broader "harder to move opinion than assumed" throughline while validating concern about coordination itself. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] documents a genuine, half-century decline in trust in news—real, but gradual, pre-dating the internet, and non-universal (rising in unfree states)—cautioning against attributing a long structural trend to recent platform dynamics. On interventions, [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] and [[Voelkel2026-lc]] show modest cause for optimism and modest cause for restraint simultaneously: scaled prebunking can shift narrow, well-defined skills (spotting fearmongering) durably, while even the most-cited climate-messaging strategies move attitudes only a few percentage points and fail to budge costly behavior like donations. [[Hameleers2026-mc]], [[Choi2026-bz]], and [[Wack2026-bt]] extend the recalibration to generative AI: visual and deepfake disinformation is not uniformly more potent than text, its credibility is socially negotiated through audience commentary rather than fixed at production, and the panic over AI specifically may be overstated relative to low-tech decontextualization. [[Much2026-gu]] and [[Spampatti2026-kx]] look outward—toward gendered media ecosystems and climate psychology, respectively—arguing that misinformation research should broaden its outcome measures, contexts, and disciplinary borrowing rather than continue over-indexing on US partisan politics and narrow cognitive proxies. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] crystallizes the field's self-critique: no single analogy (virus, warfare, pollution) captures misinformation's genuinely multilevel complexity, and portfolio-style, complexity-aware policy — not single silver-bullet interventions — is the appropriate response. Set against this consensus, [[Vincent_undated-re]]'s ongoing platform audits (finding rising TikTok mis/disinformation prevalence and persistent low-credibility engagement advantages) serve as a reminder that recalibration is not synonymous with reassurance: the empirical picture is one of concentration and limited average effect, not of a solved problem.
