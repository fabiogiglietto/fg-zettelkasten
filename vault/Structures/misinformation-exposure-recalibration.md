# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

A cluster of large-scale, trace-data studies converges on a single empirical pattern: exposure to clearly "fake" or untrustworthy content online is small in the aggregate, heavily concentrated among a narrow slice of users, and weakly connected to measurable downstream harm. [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], and [[Guess2019-ym]] established this template for the 2016 US election—average exposure to fake news was on the order of one remembered article, sharing was dominated by a tiny fraction of (mostly older, conservative) "supersharers," and mainstream sources still accounted for the bulk of political news diets. [[Guess2020-rr]] extended this to website-level browsing data, showing untrustworthy sites made up roughly 6% of the average news diet while being "deep but narrow" in their concentration among the most conservative decile of users. [[Allen2020-nj]] generalized the finding further by zooming out to the whole media ecosystem, showing fake news is a vanishingly small share (~0.15%) of total daily media consumption once television is accounted for—implicitly indicting a research literature disproportionately fixated on online fakery. [[Eady2023-xg]] applies the same logic to foreign influence operations, finding Russian IRA content on Twitter was dwarfed by domestic political content, concentrated among strongly Republican users least in need of persuasion, and unrelated to attitude or vote change. Together these papers reframe "misinformation exposure" as a tail phenomenon rather than a mass one.

### Null effects from experimental and platform-scale interventions

A second wave of work moves from descriptive concentration to causal testing, largely confirming that manipulating exposure does not reliably move attitudes. The Meta-academic collaboration studies—[[Guess2023-ai]], [[Guess2023-ur]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]], and [[Allcott2025-jb]]—used population-scale randomized field experiments around the 2020 US election to strip out reshares, revert to chronological feeds, reduce like-minded exposure, and remove political ads, respectively. In each case the on-platform content environment changed substantially, yet polarization, knowledge, and most attitudinal outcomes remained essentially unmoved. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] extends this to untrustworthy-source exposure specifically, again finding concentration without attitudinal effect. [[Allen2024-av]] complicates this slightly: it finds that flagged misinformation genuinely can move vaccination intentions per-exposure, but its low reach means its *aggregate* societal impact is dwarfed by unflagged, factually accurate but misleading mainstream content—a crucial nuance that shifts the locus of concern from false content to misleading-but-true content circulated by credible outlets. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] and [[Pennycook2021-jq]] supply psychological grounding for these patterns, showing fake-news sharing follows ordinary partisan and inattention-driven logics rather than being a distinct pathology, and that truth discernment is undercut more by lax reasoning than by motivated partisanship.

### Reframing what counts as "misinformation" and where it lives

Several papers argue the dominant operationalization of misinformation—domain blocklists, fact-checked falsehoods, fringe sources—misses where everyday harm and perceived untrustworthiness actually concentrate. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds Australians flag mainstream and alternative news outlets, not fringe sources, as primary propagators of perceived misinformation, and that the content is overwhelmingly textual, topically broad (business, celebrity, crime), and tied to clickbait rather than exotic falsity. [[Goel2025-iq]] shows factually accurate mainstream articles are systematically repurposed to support misleading narratives, arguing domain-level classification obscures a larger, narrative-level phenomenon. [[So2026-up]] extends the reframing to the AI era, arguing "slop" is not a class of false objects but a condition in which evaluative signals themselves have broken down—explicitly distinguishing this from misinformation's traditional truth/falsity axis. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s audit of Grokipedia operationalizes a related concern empirically, finding AI-generated encyclopedic content skews toward unreliable sourcing particularly on controversial topics, while [[Waight2026-ts]] documents a more structural, geopolitical channel by which state media control seeps into LLM outputs—both suggesting new infrastructures for degrading information quality that fall outside classic "fake news" framing.

### Algorithms, echo chambers, and polarization: more modest culprits than assumed

A parallel strand interrogates the mechanisms presumed to drive misinformation's societal harms—algorithmic curation and echo chambers—and finds them weaker than folk theories suggest. [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube, finding only mild, user-driven echo chambers and no radicalization pathway, though a platform-wide conservative tilt remains. [[Nyhan2023-gb]] and [[Guess2023-ur]] similarly show like-minded exposure and algorithmic ranking are less causally potent for polarization than assumed. [[Schulz2026-ts]] and [[Oswald2025-km]] push the critique upstream, arguing that apparent polarization and distorted public opinion are substantially artifacts of self-selection into posting and of a production-consumption gap in which a vocal minority dominates visible discourse—misleading both ordinary citizens and researchers who treat visible content as representative. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] adds a further twist: even discourse *about* election interference, independent of the interference's actual effects, has only limited and asymmetric polarizing consequences, mainly activating partisans already primed to see their opponents benefiting.

### Measurement artifacts and the data infrastructure problem

A methodologically reflexive set of papers warns that many alarming prevalence estimates are themselves artifacts of how platform data is collected and censored. [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] dissect the Social Science One/Meta URL Shares Dataset, showing its 100-public-share threshold inflates fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold relative to representative panels and introduces spurious cross-national synchrony driven by feed-ranking changes rather than local events. [[Guess2021-ym]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] use this same dataset family more cautiously to show low-credibility content is a modest, age- and ideology-skewed share of exposure in the US and Europe respectively, while [[Allcott2019-gn]] tracks a genuine post-2016 decline in Facebook (though not Twitter) misinformation diffusion. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Rogers2026-zz]] bring this up to the platform-governance present, showing how shifting definitional categories ("fake news" to "borderline content"), metrics (engagement vs. reach), and data-access regimes shape what can even be measured—with [[Rogers2026-zz]] arguing Meta's narrowing definitions have served to depoliticize moderation even as right-wing sources remain disproportionately represented in what residual engagement exists. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] provides a long-run corrective of its own: trust in news has been declining since the 1980s, predating the internet and digital platforms entirely, undercutting narratives that pin trust collapse solely on social media and misinformation.

### Synthesis

Read together, these papers do not deny that misinformation exists or that some actors cause real harm—[[Allen2024-av]] and [[Goel2025-iq]] show real, if differently located, damage—but they consistently push back against three alarmist defaults: that exposure is pervasive, that algorithms are the primary engine of harm, and that fake, fringe content is the main vector of epistemic damage. [[Budak2024-ef]] serves as the explicit thesis statement for the whole cluster, naming these three misperceptions directly. The accumulated evidence instead relocates concern toward concentration among vulnerable subgroups (older adults, in [[Lyons2026-ca]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Guess2021-ym]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]]), toward the repurposing of true content and collapsing evaluative signals ([[Goel2025-iq]], [[So2026-up]], [[Waight2026-ts]]), toward self-selection effects in visible discourse ([[Schulz2026-ts]], [[Oswald2025-km]]), and toward the measurement infrastructures that have shaped the field's own alarmist priors ([[Allen2021-ai]], [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]]). Classic cognitive-science accounts like [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] and policy calls like [[Lazer2018-mm]] remain useful background on why corrections fail and why rigorous measurement matters, but the newer literature largely answers their call for evidence by showing the problem is narrower, more concentrated, and more structurally embedded in everyday and mainstream information flows than the "infodemic" framing assumed.
