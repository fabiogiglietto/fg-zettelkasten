# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## The empirical corrective to alarmism

[[Budak2024-ef]] functions as the programmatic statement for this cluster: public and scholarly discourse about misinformation has drifted from the evidence base, particularly in three respects — how much problematic content people actually see, how much of that exposure is algorithmically driven versus chosen, and how directly any of it connects to society-level harms. The rest of the papers filed here can be read as empirical tests of exactly these three claims, each supplying large-scale or naturalistic data that the alarmist narrative typically lacks.

## Exposure is real but radically concentrated

A recurring, almost mechanical finding across studies with platform-scale access is that misinformation exposure follows a power law: most users see almost none of it, while a small minority absorbs the overwhelming majority. [[noauthor_undated-bm]] shows this most starkly for Meta's own "deceptive online networks" data around the 2020 election — 49 CIB and financially-motivated operations reached tens of millions of users, but three networks drove roughly 70–80% of that reach, and 1% of Instagram viewers accounted for 96% of network content views. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds the identical structural signature for untrustworthy-source content on Facebook and Instagram: low average exposure, heavily concentrated in a minority, with no attitudinal effects when exposure is experimentally reduced. [[Lyons2026-ca]] extends this logic beyond political misinformation into health information, finding that low-credibility health content exposure is rare in the aggregate (top 10% of users account for 77% of it) but systematically skewed toward older adults, and — notably — is driven by referral chains among low-credibility sites themselves rather than by search or social media, undercutting a platform-algorithm-centric explanation. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] documents the same concentration dynamic from the diffusion side: most reshared Facebook posts reach small audiences, with virality and misinformation's disproportionate share of it concentrated in a narrow set of viral cascades and actors. [[Oswald2025-km]] generalizes this into a structural claim about social media itself — a small, highly active minority produces the visible political content that both citizens and researchers mistake for public opinion, meaning the concentration problem documented for misinformation specifically is really an instance of a broader production–consumption asymmetry. [[Schulz2026-ts]] gives this asymmetry a psychological mechanism: ideologically extreme users self-select into posting, so visible discourse looks more polarized than the underlying population, a "false polarization" reading that dovetails with the concentration-of-exposure findings — what looks like a pervasive phenomenon is substantially an artifact of who chooses to speak and who chooses to click.

## From exposure to effects: a persistent null

A second thread interrogates whether documented exposure — even where concentrated — translates into measurable harm. [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s randomized removal of political ads from Facebook and Instagram feeds for six weeks before the 2020 election produced no detectable shifts in knowledge, polarization, or perceptions. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] reaches the same conclusion via experimental reduction of untrustworthy-source exposure. [[noauthor_undated-bm]] shows that bivariate associations between exposure to deceptive networks and outcomes like truth discernment or partisan news consumption evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are adjusted for, and are fragile to plausible unobserved confounding — a methodological lesson as much as an empirical one. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s megastudy on climate messaging complements this pattern from the persuasion side: even the most-cited, well-resourced messaging strategies move attitudes only a few percentage points and fail entirely to shift costly behavior (donations), reinforcing that small, detectable attitudinal effects do not straightforwardly cascade into behavioral or societal consequences. Together these studies suggest the causal arrow from "exposure" to "harm" assumed in public discourse is far weaker and more conditional than commonly asserted.

## What counts as misinformation is narrower — and broader — than assumed

A third strand challenges the definitional and measurement infrastructure of the field itself, arguing that source-level and topic-narrow conceptions of misinformation both overstate exotic threats and miss mundane ones. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds that everyday "misinformation," as perceived by ordinary users, is dominated by clickbait headlines from mainstream and alternative news outlets — not fringe or AI-generated content — and spans banal domains like business and economics rather than only politics and health, with independent verification showing many flagged items are not objectively false at all. [[Goel2025-iq]] arrives at a structurally similar conclusion from the opposite direction: domain-blocklist approaches miss the larger phenomenon of factually accurate mainstream articles being repurposed to support misleading narratives, with such co-shared mainstream content reaching roughly twice the audience of "fake news" proper. [[Green2025-ap]] generalizes the critique of source-level measurement to partisan curation more broadly, showing that domain-level audience scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity — especially for "moderate" outlets — so that headline claims about how homogeneous or extreme audiences are can be artifacts of the wrong unit of analysis. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies makes the meta-level version of this point explicit: much of the field's disagreement about whether echo chambers exist stems from incompatible operationalizations rather than incompatible realities. [[Bakshy2015-rn]], the foundational study in this vein, established early that individual click choices — not the News Feed algorithm — do most of the work suppressing cross-cutting exposure on Facebook, a finding that anticipates and structurally supports [[Budak2024-ef]]'s later claim that algorithms are not the primary driver commonly assumed.

## Extending the recalibration to new terrains

Two papers push this recalibrating lens into adjacent, less-studied domains where "harm" narratives are emerging but ungrounded in comparable evidence. [[Triedman2025-uy]] undertakes the first large-scale comparison of Grokipedia against Wikipedia, showing it is heavily derivative yet disproportionately cites low-quality and blacklisted sources on controversial topics — offering an early empirical baseline for a new AI-mediated knowledge platform before alarmist or dismissive narratives harden around it. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] shifts attention from exposure to infrastructure, documenting via DSA transparency data that human moderation capacity is starkly skewed against Global South languages and that millions of EU users post in languages with no moderation coverage at all — a reminder that "misinformation harm" is unevenly distributed not because exposure itself is exotic, but because the infrastructure meant to address it is itself concentrated and unequal, mirroring the exposure-concentration finding from a governance angle rather than a consumption one.

Read together, this set of papers converges on a consistent recalibration: exposure to misinformation, broadly defined, is real but concentrated in small, identifiable populations; effects of that exposure on attitudes and behavior are frequently null or fragile once naturalistic and experimental designs replace correlational proxies; and the categories researchers use to measure "misinformation" — domain lists, topic silos, algorithmic blame — often obscure more mundane, more diffuse, and differently distributed phenomena than the public narrative assumes.
