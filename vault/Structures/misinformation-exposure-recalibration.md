# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

# Recalibrating Misinformation Exposure and Harms Narratives

## The empirical corrective

A core thread running through this collection is a direct challenge to alarmist narratives about misinformation's ubiquity and potency, grounded not in theory but in painstaking measurement. [[Budak2024-ef]] serves as something of a manifesto for this cluster, explicitly naming three misperceptions—about prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm—that have come unmoored from the evidence base. The empirical backbone for this claim comes from a lineage of studies using passively collected behavioral data rather than self-report or curated samples: [[Allen2020-nj]] shows that "fake news" occupies roughly 0.15% of Americans' daily media diet once television is properly weighted in; [[Guess2020-rr]] finds untrustworthy websites made up only ~6% of the average 2016 news diet; [[Grinberg2019-ua]] demonstrates that even on Twitter, mainstream media dominated political news exposure across the ideological spectrum; and [[Allcott2017-yz]] calculates that the average voter saw and remembered only about one fake news article during the entire 2016 campaign. [[Guess2019-ym]]'s title—"Less than you think"—could stand as an epigraph for the whole cluster.

## Concentration, not contagion

Where exposure does occur, a striking regularity emerges: it is not evenly distributed but concentrated among a small, identifiable minority. [[Grinberg2019-ua]] finds that 1% of users accounted for 80% of fake news exposures on Twitter; [[Eady2023-xg]] finds a similar pattern for Russian IRA content, with 1% of users absorbing 70% of exposures—mostly strongly partisan Republicans already disinclined toward persuasion. [[Guess2021-ym]] and [[Lyons2026-ca]] extend this logic to Facebook sharing and health misinformation respectively, the latter showing the top 10% of users account for 77% of low-credibility health exposure, concentrated among older, more conspiracist-minded consumers. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] replicates the pattern at platform scale for 2020 Facebook/Instagram data. Age emerges repeatedly as the master variable: [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Allcott2019-gn]]/[[Guess2021-ym]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] (Europe), and [[Lyons2026-ca]] all converge on older users as disproportionately implicated, independent of ideology. [[Oswald2025-km]] offers a structural explanation for why visible discourse so badly misrepresents this reality: a small, unrepresentative set of highly active producers generates the content that both citizens and researchers mistake for public opinion—a point sharpened by [[Schulz2026-ts]], which shows self-selection into posting by ideological extremity manufactures "false polarization" independent of actual attitude distributions.

## Null and modest effects where alarm predicts large ones

A parallel body of large-scale field experiments repeatedly fails to find the attitudinal effects that prevailing narratives would predict. Meta's 2020 election research collaboration produced a string of precisely estimated nulls: [[Guess2023-ai]] (removing reshares), [[Guess2023-ur]] (chronological vs. algorithmic feeds), [[Nyhan2023-gb]] (reducing like-minded exposure), and [[Allcott2025-jb]] (removing political ads) all altered users' content diets substantially while leaving polarization, knowledge, and most attitudes essentially unchanged. [[Allen2024-av]] applies a similar exposure-times-persuasion logic to vaccine hesitancy and finds that flagged misinformation, despite being more persuasive per view, had negligible aggregate impact next to unflagged but vaccine-skeptical mainstream content—reframing the public health threat as broader and less "exotic" than the misinformation frame implies. [[Flaxman2016-lm]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]] similarly complicate the echo-chamber story by showing direct browsing, not social media, dominates consumption, and that polarization is topic- and time-contingent rather than a fixed structural feature. [[Brown2026-br]] extends this disaggregation to YouTube, finding rabbit holes but not algorithmic radicalization pathways. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review explains why the field disagrees so persistently: divergent conceptualizations and operationalizations, not divergent realities, drive much of the contradictory evidence on echo chambers.

## Methodological undertow: what the data can and cannot show

Several papers interrogate the infrastructure underlying these prevalence claims, cautioning that measurement artifacts can manufacture both false reassurance and false alarm. [[Allen2021-ai]] shows that Facebook's own privacy-driven censoring thresholds inflate apparent fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold relative to an uncensored benchmark, a crucial caveat for any study built on the URL Shares Dataset (including [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Guess2021-ym]]). [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a comparable audit of NewsGuard, the source-rating system underlying much of this literature, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications can be destabilized by single sources crossing a threshold, while continuous scores are comparatively stable—a direct methodological lesson for interpreting country comparisons. [[Yang2023-cg]] complicates the reassuring consensus from a different angle, arguing that link-based measures used throughout this literature systematically miss image-based misinformation, which by their count is more prevalent (~20%) and more partisan-skewed than text-based estimates suggest—a reminder that "limited exposure" conclusions are partly an artifact of what is counted. [[Goel2025-iq]] makes a structurally similar point: source-level blocklists miss the larger phenomenon of true content from mainstream outlets being repurposed into misleading narratives, meaning domain-based prevalence estimates may understate certain harms even as they overstate others. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Rogers2026-zz]] extend this epistemic concern to the shrinking, strategically managed data-access regimes (CrowdTangle, Social Science One, Meta Content Library) that determine what independent researchers can even observe—Rogers tracing how Meta's own redefinition of "fake news" into "borderline content" has narrowed the empirical object of study over time.

## Complicating the "limited harm" consensus

Not every paper in this set simply reinforces reassurance; several push back against overcorrection or identify harms that survive the recalibration. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] use causal modeling to argue that even modest-seeming exposure effects can translate into non-trivial real-world consequences (vaccine refusals, suppressed turnout) when aggregated across large populations and concentrated on susceptible subgroups—an important counterpoint to purely descriptive concentration findings. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] shows that even minimal elite-cue exposure can erode confidence in specific institutions (election integrity) even where broader democratic support is untouched, suggesting that "no detectable attitudinal effect" studies may be looking at the wrong dependent variables. [[Bail2018-fk]] offers the sharpest complication: an intervention widely assumed to help (cross-partisan exposure) actually backfires, increasing polarization among Republicans—a caution against assuming all effects, once found, are subject to naive remedy. [[Marwick2025-vx]] similarly argues that even where single-exposure "redpill" moments are rare, disinformation is causally central to far-right socialization once reframed as a gradual, cumulative process rather than a discrete persuasion event—effects obscured by designs looking for immediate, large-magnitude shifts. Together these papers caution that "limited effects" findings are partly a function of where and how researchers look, not proof that harm is absent.

## Reframing what misinformation even is

A final cluster questions the definitional and topical boundaries of "misinformation" itself, arguing that the phenomenon is both more mundane and more context-dependent than alarmist framings assume. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds that everyday misinformation is dominated by mainstream news sensationalism and clickbait across prosaic topics like business and economics, not fringe or political "hot-button" content—directly undercutting the dominant topical focus of the field. [[Nenno2025-xa]] shows news-value patterns distinguishing misinformation from ordinary content are statistically real but small, and vary meaningfully between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, warning against universalizing Western-derived theories. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that reigning metaphors (infodemic, information warfare, information pollution) each capture only part of a genuinely multilevel, complex system, and that single-analogy thinking has distorted both research and policy. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a subtler distortion: partisans systematically believe their own side is more targeted by misinformation than it actually is, a "hostile misinformation effect" that may itself be contributing to exaggerated public perceptions of the problem's scale. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Osmundsen2021-et]] revisit the psychological mechanisms underneath sharing and belief, with Pennycook relocating the central failure in inattention rather than motivated partisan reasoning, and Osmundsen finding partisan animus rather than ignorance as the driver—together suggesting that even where misinformation effects are real, their cognitive etiology is less exotic, and more continuous with ordinary political psychology, than crisis narratives imply. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], [[Tsfati2020-uo]], [[Vosoughi2018-at]], [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]], and [[Lazer2018-mm]] provide the earlier canonical evidence base (continued-influence effects, mainstream media's paradoxical amplifying role, differential diffusion of falsehood, echo-chamber percolation dynamics, and the founding "science of fake news" agenda) against which this entire recalibration project is implicitly arguing—establishing the alarmed baseline that papers like [[Budak2024-ef]], [[Guess2020-rr]], and [[Allen2020-nj]] exist to correct, while newer entries such as [[Triedman2025-uy]] (on Grokipedia's sourcing patterns), [[Wack2026-bt]] (on socially negotiated deepfake impact in Kenya), [[Hameleers2026-mc]] (on contingent visual-disinformation effects), [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and [[Pante2025-pq]] (on overstated coordinated-behavior influence), and [[Renault2025-uh]] (on persistent partisan asymmetry even under crowdsourced moderation) show the recalibration project extending into AI-generated content, deepfakes, and platform governance debates as the empirical frontier continues to move.
