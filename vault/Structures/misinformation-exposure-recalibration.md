# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

The papers gathered here converge on a single provocation: much of what the public, journalists, and even researchers believe about the scale and consequence of online misinformation does not survive contact with large-scale, naturalistic data. [[Budak2024-ef]] states this thesis most directly, arguing that public discourse has drifted from the empirical record on three fronts — how much problematic content people actually see, how much algorithms drive that exposure, and how strongly social media causes broader societal harm. The rest of the corpus can be read as evidence for, elaboration of, or productive friction with that claim.

### Concentration among the few, not exposure of the many

A recurring empirical signature across this literature is that misinformation exposure is not a mass phenomenon but a heavy-tailed one. [[Lyons2026-ca]] finds that low-credibility health content is rare in the aggregate but that the top 10% of users account for 77% of all exposure, concentrated among older, more conspiracist-minded, cross-domain consumers — echoing the age-skew literature on political misinformation but extending it to a new domain. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] makes the same point at platform scale: across all active US Facebook and Instagram users in 2020, untrustworthy-source exposure was low on average, concentrated among a minority, and experimentally reducing it produced no attitudinal effects. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] adds the diffusion-side complement, showing that virality itself is rare and unevenly distributed, with misinformation's reach shaped by a small set of highly active propagators rather than diffuse contagion. [[Oswald2025-km]] generalizes this asymmetry into a structural claim — a small minority produces most visible political content while the majority remain silent — arguing that both citizens and researchers mistake this "tip of the iceberg" for public opinion writ large. [[Schulz2026-ts]] gives this a psychological mechanism: ideologically extreme users self-select into posting, so observed online discourse appears more polarized than the underlying population actually is. Even coordinated inauthentic behavior turns out to follow this concentration logic in reverse: [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] shows that real coordinated accounts in UK election cascades achieve influence far below optimal or even resource-matched benchmarks, with placement patterns statistically indistinguishable from random — suggesting that alarm about strategic manipulation often outruns the actual leverage such campaigns achieve.

### Interventions and causal effects: modest, null, or context-dependent

A parallel strand tests whether manipulating exposure or messaging changes minds, and mostly finds limits. [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s large field experiment removing political ads from Facebook and Instagram before the 2020 election found no detectable effects on knowledge, polarization, or perceptions. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows that media narratives *about* election interference do not increase polarization in the aggregate, only among partisans whose out-group is portrayed as the beneficiary — a much narrower and more conditional harm than "interference discourse divides society" narratives suggest. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messages finds real but small attitudinal shifts, no partisan heterogeneity, and no effect whatsoever on costly behavior like donations — a sobering corrective to over-claimed persuasion literatures that this collection treats as structurally analogous to misinformation-effects research. [[Brown2026-br]] dismantles the YouTube radicalization narrative specifically, finding only mild, user-driven echo-chamber effects and no algorithmic radicalization pathway, even while documenting a genuine "rabbit hole" dynamic and a platform-wide conservative tilt. Against this pattern of null or bounded effects, [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] is the interesting positive case: a 19-second Instagram prebunking ad produced a durable, scalable 21-point gain in recognizing emotional manipulation — suggesting that where interventions target a narrowly specified cognitive skill rather than diffuse "misinformation susceptibility," effects can be real and persistent.

### Redefining the object: everyday, mainstream, and irreducibly complex

Several papers argue the recalibration must go further than exposure statistics — the very category of "misinformation" needs re-description. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds that everyday misinformation is mundane, text-based, topically diffuse (business and economics rather than politics), and most often attributed to mainstream and alternative news outlets rather than fringe sources — a picture starkly at odds with scholarship fixated on exotic, AI-generated, or hyperpartisan content. [[Goel2025-iq]] complements this by showing that domain-level blocklists miss a larger phenomenon: factually accurate mainstream articles are disproportionately co-shared to support misleading narratives, with such repurposed content reaching roughly twice the audience of "fake news" proper. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] steps back to argue that the field's dominant analogies — virus, warfare, pollution — each capture only one level of a multilevel complex system, and that single-analogy thinking (including oversimplified media-literacy fixes) can itself backfire. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies makes a structurally similar point about a neighboring construct: much of the field's disagreement about whether echo chambers exist reflects incompatible operationalizations rather than a settled empirical fact one way or the other.

### The evidence base has its own biases

Two papers turn the recalibrating lens on the research enterprise itself. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] documents that roughly half of high-profile social media research has undisclosed industry ties, that industry-tied work is topically skewed toward user-focused misinformation-sharing questions (and away from platform-dynamics questions), and that this tied research receives outsized citations and attention — a structural reason to expect the literature to overstate certain kinds of harm and understate others. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century, 115-country analysis of trust in news similarly complicates declinist narratives: trust decline is real but not universal, predates the internet, and even rose in countries lacking press freedom, cautioning against attributing a single "misinformation era" causal story to what is a long, uneven, context-dependent trend.

### Complications that resist easy reassurance

Not every paper in this set lands on the side of reassurance, and their inclusion keeps the picture honest. [[Vincent_undated-re]]'s repeated measurement finds TikTok's mis/disinformation prevalence rising (from ~20% to ~25%) and low-credibility accounts retaining or widening their engagement advantage — a reminder that concentration and low averages elsewhere do not mean every platform or every metric is trending toward reassurance. [[Ventura2025-sw]] similarly pushes back against complacency by relocating the site of concern: its WhatsApp deactivation experiment in Brazil suggests that encrypted messaging platforms, largely invisible to the feed-centric measurement paradigms that produce most of this collection's low-exposure findings, may carry real informational and attitudinal consequences that public-facing platform audits simply cannot see. Together with [[Goel2025-iq]]'s mainstream-narrative-laundering finding, these papers suggest that the corrective to alarmism is not a blanket claim that misinformation is harmless, but a demand that both scholars and the public look for its effects in the right places — concentrated, structurally dependent on measurement choices, and often hiding in plain, mainstream, or private sight rather than where the loudest narratives point.
