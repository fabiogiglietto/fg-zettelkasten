# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## The scale question: concentrated exposure, not pervasive crisis

The founding empirical move of this literature is to ask, at ecosystem scale, how much misinformation people actually encounter — and the answer, repeatedly, is "much less than feared, and overwhelmingly among a small minority." [[Allen2020-nj]] situates fake news within the full multimedia diet and finds it occupies roughly 0.15% of Americans' daily media consumption, dwarfed by television and by news avoidance itself. [[Allcott2017-yz]] and [[Grinberg2019-ua]] make the equivalent case for the 2016 election specifically: the average voter saw only about one memorable fake article, and on Twitter 1% of users accounted for 80% of fake-news exposures. [[Guess2020-rr]] extends this to web-browsing data, describing exposure as "deep but narrow" — concentrated in the most conservative decile of the population — while [[Guess2019-ym]] shows sharing itself is rare and driven less by ideology than by age. [[Eady2023-xg]] applies the same concentration logic to the Russian IRA campaign, finding exposure dwarfed by domestic news and politicians, with 1% of users absorbing 70% of exposures and no detectable attitudinal consequences. [[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] bring this diagnostic to new domains — health information and untrustworthy sources on Facebook/Instagram in 2020 respectively — confirming that skewed concentration among older, heavier, more conspiracist-leaning users is a structural feature of exposure, not an artifact of the 2016 case. [[Appel2026-qr]]'s analysis of 49 deceptive networks reinforces the pattern at a platform-operations level: three networks drove 70% of total reach, and most of that reach arrived indirectly through ordinary users resharing, not through the networks' own posting. [[Budak2024-ef]] synthesizes this body of work explicitly as a corrective, arguing that public and scholarly discourse has drifted from what the evidence shows about prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm.

## From correlation to causation: why exposure rarely moves attitudes

A second cluster uses experimental and quasi-experimental designs to test whether this limited exposure nonetheless matters, and consistently finds null or negligible effects once genuine causal identification is applied. The Meta-academic collaboration on the 2020 US election is central here: [[Guess2023-ai]] shows that removing reshares drastically cuts exposure to political news and low-quality sources yet produces no detectable shift in polarization or most attitudes; [[Guess2023-ur]] finds the same for replacing algorithmic ranking with chronological feeds; [[Nyhan2023-gb]] shows that cutting like-minded exposure by a third changes feed composition but moves none of eight preregistered attitudinal outcomes; and [[Allcott2025-jb]] finds that removing political ads altogether has no measurable effect on knowledge, polarization, or perceptions. [[Appel2026-qr]] extends this logic to deceptive networks, showing that naive correlations between exposure and factual discernment or election-legitimacy beliefs evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. Against this backdrop of precisely estimated nulls, [[Berlinski2023-rj]] is an important counterpoint, demonstrating that unsubstantiated elite claims of voter fraud — a different genre of "misinformation" than viral junk news — can meaningfully erode confidence in elections, especially among co-partisans, and that fact-checks do little to repair the damage. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] offers a rare positive intervention result at scale, showing a 19-second prebunking ad on Instagram durably improves recognition of emotional manipulation — suggesting the recalibration literature's skepticism about *harm* need not extend to skepticism about the *tractability* of well-targeted interventions.

## Algorithms, curation, and the architecture of amplification

A parallel strand interrogates the mechanisms presumed to manufacture these harms — echo chambers, filter bubbles, and radicalizing recommender systems — and generally finds user choice, not platform architecture, doing the heavy lifting. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Flaxman2016-lm]] are foundational here, showing that algorithmic ranking modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure while individual clicking behavior reduces it more, and that most news consumption is direct browsing to mainstream outlets rather than algorithmically mediated discovery. [[Brown2026-br]] updates this debate for YouTube, using an audit design to show only mild, choice-driven echo-chamber effects, genuine algorithmic "rabbit holes," but no evidence of radicalization pathways — with a modest conservative tilt applied uniformly regardless of user ideology. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates the measurement baseline itself, showing that domain-level partisanship scores mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, so apparent "curation bubbles" reflect networked identity performance more than source-level bias. [[Schulz2026-ts]] (cited as [[Schulz2026-ts]]) and [[Oswald2025-km]] push the recalibration further upstream, arguing that visible online discourse is falsely polarized and disproportionately produced by a small, vocal, non-representative minority — meaning that both ordinary citizens and researchers risk mistaking the "tip of the iceberg" for the whole.

## Reassessing coordination, virality, and the mechanics of spread

Several papers apply similarly skeptical, measurement-driven scrutiny to claims about coordinated inauthentic behavior and viral contagion. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formally models the influence coordinated accounts could achieve and finds real-world coordinated placements resemble random assignment far more than strategic optimization, implying CIB is less pivotal than prevailing narratives suggest. [[Pante2025-pq]] extends this skepticism to claims of inter-state collaboration, arguing that without modern coordination detection and proper control datasets, earlier findings of cross-state coordination do not hold up. These sit in productive tension with [[Vosoughi2018-at]], the canonical finding that falsehood spreads farther, faster, and deeper than truth on Twitter — a result this literature does not so much overturn as contextualize: [[Allcott2019-gn]] shows that the Facebook-to-Twitter engagement ratio for fake news fell by roughly 60% after 2016, suggesting virality advantages are platform- and moment-specific rather than fixed laws of informational physics. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] adds texture by characterizing how misinformation diffusion trees on Facebook during 2020 compare to other content types, reinforcing that even "viral" misinformation reaches mass audiences only rarely and through identifiable structural pathways.

## Widening the lens: everyday, narrative, and partisan complications

A final set of papers argues that fixating on discrete "fake news" domains has caused the field to miss where everyday misinformation and its harms actually live. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds that Australians' lived experience of misinformation is dominated by mainstream and alternative news outlets, economic anxieties, and clickbait headlines — not fringe, political, or AI-generated content — and that independent verification often finds flagged claims are not objectively false at all. [[Holt2026-zq]] reaches a congruent conclusion from Facebook's user-reporting data: flagged "false news" tends to be polarized political content people disagree with, not falsehood per se, raising doubts about crowdsourced moderation as fact-checking's replacement — a concern [[Renault2025-uh]] partially answers by showing that X's Community Notes nonetheless flags Republican-authored posts at over twice the rate of Democratic ones, undercutting claims of fact-checker bias. [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[Allen2024-av]] converge on a related insight: domain-level "misinformation" measures miss the larger phenomenon of true, mainstream content being repurposed or framed to support misleading narratives — with [[Allen2024-av]] estimating that unflagged vaccine-skeptical mainstream content had 46 times the aggregate impact on hesitancy than fact-checked misinformation. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] reframes disinformation itself as recurring cross-media narratives rather than discrete claims, and [[Nenno2025-xa]] shows that the news-value signatures of user-flagged misinformation are only modestly distinct from ordinary news and vary importantly outside WEIRD contexts. [[Yang2023-cg]] and [[Rogers2026-zz]] push back partially against the recalibration consensus from within: visual, image-based misinformation on Facebook is far more prevalent (20%+) and partisan-skewed than link-based studies suggest, and borderline content, while declining in overall traction by 2024, remains almost exclusively a right-wing phenomenon obscured by Meta's shifting metrics and shrinking data access. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] and [[Pennycook2021-jq]] supply the cognitive and institutional mechanisms tying these threads together — showing that belief and sharing are driven less by partisan motivation than by inattention and heuristics, and that mainstream media's own corrective coverage may inadvertently be fake news's largest amplifier. [[Vincent_undated-re]], tracking rising mis/disinformation prevalence on TikTok across measurement rounds, and [[Allen2021-ai]], exposing how Social Science One's censoring threshold inflates apparent fake-news prevalence by roughly fourfold, together serve as methodological bookends: a reminder that recalibration is itself an ongoing empirical project, vulnerable to both platform-specific exceptions and measurement artifacts, rather than a settled conclusion. [[Ventura2025-sw]], examining WhatsApp deactivation in Brazil, gestures toward the geographic and platform blind spots — private messaging, the Global South — that this entire literature has only begun to map.
