# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

# Recalibrating Misinformation Exposure and Harms Narratives

## The empirical corrective to alarmism

A core thread running through this literature is a direct challenge to the "infodemic" imaginary that has dominated public and scholarly discourse since 2016. [[Budak2024-ef]] names the problem explicitly: public understanding of online misinformation has drifted from what the evidence actually shows, on three fronts — prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm. The remaining papers in this cluster function largely as the evidentiary backbone for that claim. [[Allen2020-nj]] recalibrates the unit of analysis itself, showing that when misinformation is measured against the *entire* media ecosystem (including television, which still dwarfs online news), fake news amounts to roughly 0.15% of the average American's daily media diet. [[Allcott2017-yz]], the foundational economic treatment of 2016-era fake news, similarly concludes that the average adult saw and remembered only about one fake article — far too little to plausibly explain electoral outcomes, especially once placebo-headline recall rates reveal how much "exposure" is actually false memory.

This recalibration continues on social platforms specifically. [[Grinberg2019-ua]] and [[Guess2019-ym]] establish the now-canonical finding that fake news sharing on Twitter and Facebook was concentrated in a tiny, demographically distinct slice of users (older, more conservative), while the bulk of political news exposure for everyone remained mainstream. [[Guess2020-rr]] extends this to passive browsing data, showing untrustworthy-site visits are "deep but narrow" — concentrated in the most conservative decile — and that fact-checks almost never reach the people who saw the original claims. [[Eady2023-xg]] applies the same logic to the Russian IRA campaign, finding exposure overshadowed by domestic political content by an order of magnitude and no detectable attitudinal effect. [[Allcott2019-gn]] tracks the trend over time, documenting a post-2016 decline in Facebook misinformation engagement relative to Twitter, suggesting platform-specific mitigation rather than an ever-worsening crisis. [[Lazer2018-mm]] and [[Tsfati2020-uo]] sit slightly earlier in this arc, cautioning that science cannot yet speak confidently to prevalence or long-run effects, while reframing mainstream media itself as a major (if inadvertent) amplifier of fake news narratives.

## Concentration as the master pattern

If one empirical regularity unifies this topic, it is *concentration*: harmful content is not evenly distributed but clustered among a small subset of "supersharers" and susceptible consumers. [[Allen2024-av]] crystallizes the analytic move required to take this seriously — impact requires both exposure *and* persuasive influence — and shows that COVID vaccine-skeptical content from mainstream, unflagged sources had 46 times the societal impact of flagged misinformation, precisely because of where exposure concentrates. [[Lyons2026-ca]] finds the identical pattern in health misinformation: top 1% of users account for 37% of exposure, with older adults disproportionately represented via cross-domain referral chains rather than search or social media. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] confirms this for untrustworthy Facebook/Instagram sources in 2020, pairing concentrated exposure with null attitudinal effects. [[Appel2026-qr]] extends concentration logic to coordinated influence operations, showing three of 49 networks accounted for 70% of reach, and that correlational links to downstream beliefs evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Oswald2025-km]] generalizes the mechanism into a production-consumption gap: a small minority of highly active posters generate the discourse that both citizens and researchers mistake for public opinion, a point sharpened by [[Schulz2026-ts]]'s cross-national evidence that selective posting by ideological extremes manufactures "false polarization" untethered from underlying attitudes.

## Null results from platform-scale experiments

A parallel strand uses randomized, platform-partnered field experiments to test whether plausible causal mechanisms — algorithmic ranking, reshares, like-minded exposure, political advertising — actually move attitudes. The 2020 U.S. Election Studies collaboration anchors this: [[Guess2023-ur]] finds that replacing Facebook/Instagram's algorithmic feed with reverse-chronological ordering dramatically altered on-platform experience but produced no detectable change in polarization or knowledge; [[Guess2023-ai]] finds the same for removing reshares; [[Nyhan2023-gb]] finds reducing like-minded exposure by a third left eight preregistered attitudinal outcomes essentially untouched; [[Allcott2025-jb]] finds six weeks without political ads changed nothing detectable. Together these precisely-estimated nulls — not underpowered non-findings — push back hard against folk theories that algorithmic curation is the proximate cause of polarization. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky registered report nuances this picture: engagement-based ranking does amplify intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distort perceived social norms, but even here, individual engagement behavior itself proved stubbornly resistant to algorithmic reengineering over eight weeks, and toxic engagement was itself extremely concentrated (top 5% of users = 75% of toxic engagement). [[Brown2026-br]] complements this with an audit design disentangling supply from demand on YouTube, finding real but modest echo-chamber effects driven by user choice, strong "rabbit hole" dynamics, but no radicalization pathway. [[Bail2018-fk]], [[Flaxman2016-lm]], [[Bakshy2015-rn]], and [[Messing2014-jc]] form an earlier generation of this skepticism toward strong filter-bubble/echo-chamber claims, variously showing that cross-cutting exposure exists but is suppressed more by individual choice than by algorithms, that cross-partisan exposure can backfire, and that social endorsement cues override partisan source cues in news selection.

## Where real effects do appear — and their modest scale

The corpus does not deny that misinformation can matter; it insists on specifying mechanisms and bounding magnitudes. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]]'s SIRVA model estimates antivaccine tweets caused roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and 8 deaths — a real but small causal chain relative to the ~27 million Americans who became broadly hesitant. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] shows unsubstantiated voter-fraud claims measurably erode election confidence among already-sympathetic partisans, but leave support for democracy itself untouched, and fact-checks fail to repair the damage — a finding echoed by [[Renault2025-uh]]'s discovery that crowdsourced Community Notes flag Republican-authored misinformation 2.3 times more often than Democrat-authored content, undercutting claims that fact-checker bias (rather than actual asymmetry in misleading content) drives partisan disparities. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] remains the most-cited demonstration that falsehood genuinely out-diffuses truth on Twitter, attributable to novelty and human sharing rather than bots — a finding in some tension with the exposure-concentration literature, since virality and population-level exposure are not the same thing. [[Yang2023-cg]] similarly complicates the "small problem" narrative by showing image-based misinformation is far more prevalent (20-25% of political images) than link-based measures suggest, implicating a methodological blind spot in prior reassuring estimates — a useful corrective-to-the-corrective.

## Methodological reckonings: what we think we're measuring

Several papers interrogate the infrastructure of misinformation measurement itself. [[Allen2021-ai]] shows that Facebook's privacy-driven censoring of low-share URLs inflates fake-news prevalence estimates by roughly 4x relative to a representative panel, a cautionary tale about treating "big data" as unbiased simply because it is big. [[Guess2021-ym]] offers a calibrated descriptive baseline from the same Condor dataset, finding low-credibility content is a real but minority share (~15%) of news exposure, concentrated among older and more conservative users. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Vincent_undated-re]] extend this measurement tradition to Europe, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares in Germany and Italy around elections, yet relatively stable *View* shares — exposure and sharing diverge — while flagging that NewsGuard/URL-dataset methodologies developed for the U.S. travel awkwardly abroad. [[Mosleh2024-op]] pushes the same point further, arguing that single-platform (especially Twitter-centric) studies cannot generalize across an increasingly fragmented platform ecosystem. [[Rogers2026-zz]] historicizes this methodological terrain, tracing how Meta's shrinking data-access regimes and successive redefinitions of "fake news" → "misinformation" → "borderline content" have shaped — and arguably constrained — what researchers can even see, while still finding that borderline content which *does* perform well skews heavily right-wing. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s audit of Grokipedia extends this measurement-skepticism to AI-generated knowledge infrastructures, finding heavy derivation from Wikipedia alongside systematically elevated citation of blacklisted and unreliable sources on controversial topics.

## Redefining what "misinformation" and "harm" even mean

A final cluster questions whether the field has been asking the right questions at all. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds that everyday perceived misinformation is overwhelmingly about mundane topics like business and economics, mostly textual, and attributed to mainstream rather than fringe outlets — a picture far more banal than scholarly focus on health/political "hot button" topics suggests. [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[De_Leon2025-qn]] both argue that domain-blocklist approaches to measuring misinformation miss how *true* content from mainstream and "ordinary" sources (Wikipedia articles, legitimate news) gets repurposed or weaponized within misleading or extremist narratives — exposure is "networked" rather than direct. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century trust data complicates declinist narratives by showing trust in news was never universally high, declined well before the internet, and is even rising in press-unfree states — undermining nostalgic baselines against which "misinformation harms" are often measured. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] provide the cognitive-psychological ballast for this recalibration: poor truth discernment is driven more by inattention and lack of reflective reasoning than partisan motivation, sharing-belief gaps are large, and corrections are harder and more fragile than intuition suggests — tempering both alarmism about susceptibility and overconfidence in simple fixes. Several more speculative or methodological pieces — [[Frischlich2025-vn]] on the limits of virus/warfare analogies, [[Spampatti2026-kx]] on lessons from climate psychology, [[Hameleers2026-mc]] on the overstated exceptionalism of AI-generated versus low-tech visual disinformation, [[Choi2026-bz]] on carryover effects of fake-news authentication difficulty, [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] on uneven AI Overview guardrails for conspiracy queries, [[Ramos2026-qo]] on narrative- versus claim-level correction, and [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s megastudy showing even the most-cited climate messages produce only small, behaviorally inert attitude shifts — collectively argue that the field's conceptual toolkit (single analogies, claim-level fact-checking, text/political framing, lab-scale persuasion estimates) remains poorly matched to the actual, more diffuse and modest scale of real-world informational harm. [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] gesture toward where this recalibration must go next — into private messaging platforms and fine-grained diffusion-tree analysis — suggesting that the empirically grounded, exposure-concentrated, effect-bounded picture built here is still incomplete rather than final.
