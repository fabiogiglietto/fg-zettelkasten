# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

The papers filed here converge on a single corrective move: they replace impressionistic, screenshot-driven alarm about misinformation with population-scale, naturalistic measurement, and in doing so consistently find a smaller, more concentrated, and less exotic phenomenon than public and much scholarly discourse assumes. [[Budak2024-ef]] states the thesis most directly, arguing that journalistic and academic narratives about the prevalence, algorithmic drivers, and societal harms of online misinformation have diverged from the evidence base, and that exposure is concentrated in a small subset of users rather than broadly diffused.

### Exposure is rare on average, concentrated among the few

Several papers supply the large-scale exposure data that grounds this corrective. [[noauthor_undated-bm]], analyzing Meta's own designated deceptive-network takedowns around the 2020 US election, finds that a handful of networks drove the overwhelming majority of reach (three networks produced over 70% of total exposure), that ordinary resharers—not the networks themselves—did most of the amplification, and that deceptive content constituted only a sliver (0.3% on average) of users' political content diet. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] reaches a structurally identical conclusion for untrustworthy-source content on Facebook and Instagram: low average exposure, heavy concentration among a minority of heavy users, and—crucially—no detectable attitudinal effect when exposure is experimentally reduced. [[Lyons2026-ca]] extends the concentration finding to a new domain, health misinformation, showing that the top 10% of users account for 77% of exposure to low-credibility health content, with older adults, poor discerners, and conspiracist-leaning users driving the skew, and with exposure pathways running through habitual cross-site browsing rather than algorithmic recommendation or search. Together these three papers establish concentration as a robust, cross-platform, cross-topic empirical regularity rather than an artifact of any one dataset or method.

This regularity of concentration also appears at the level of *production* rather than exposure: [[Oswald2025-km]] argues that the "production-consumption gap"—a tiny minority of highly active users generating most visible political content while most users lurk—systematically distorts both citizens' and researchers' inferences about public opinion from social media traces, since what is visible online is merely the "tip of the iceberg." [[Schulz2026-ts]] gives this a sharper mechanism: self-selection into posting by more ideologically extreme users produces "false polarization," making online discourse look more divided than the underlying population actually is. Read together with the exposure-concentration papers, these two pieces suggest that both the *content* seen and the *opinions* inferred from social media are subject to the same statistical trap—small, unrepresentative slices masquerading as the whole.

### Naturalistic tests of harm: effects are limited, not absent, but modest

A second cluster interrogates whether concentrated exposure nonetheless translates into meaningful downstream harm, generally finding weak or null effects once naturalistic or causal designs are used. [[noauthor_undated-bm]]'s bivariate associations between exposure and reduced truth discernment or increased partisan news consumption attenuate to non-significance after adjustment and prove highly sensitive to unobserved confounding—an explicit methodological warning against causal overreach from correlational exposure data. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]]'s experimental manipulation similarly detects no attitudinal shift from reduced exposure. [[Voelkel2026-lc]], working in the adjacent domain of climate persuasion, offers a parallel lesson at the intervention end: even the most-cited, well-validated messaging strategies move attitudes only by a few percentage points and fail to shift costlier behaviors like actual donations, undercutting assumptions that communication interventions (the flip side of misinformation interventions) produce large real-world effects. [[Ventura2025-sw]] complicates this picture by showing that a causal manipulation *can* matter when it targets an under-examined channel: deactivating WhatsApp in Brazil shifts political knowledge and belief outcomes, suggesting that null findings from feed-based platforms should not be over-generalized to encrypted messaging environments that operate by different logics.

Not all evidence points toward null effects, and the corrective stance does not mean disinformation is inert. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] finds that repeated cross-outlet exposure to recurring disinformation *narratives*—as opposed to discrete false claims—does increase belief, and [[Goel2025-iq]] shows that domain-level ("fake news" blocklist) measures miss a larger and mechanistically distinct phenomenon: mainstream, factually accurate articles are disproportionately co-shared to support misleading narratives, reaching roughly twice the audience of fake-news articles proper. These two papers push back against a naive "nothing to see here" reading: even if classic fake-news exposure is concentrated and low-yield, the persuasive traction of misinformation may lie elsewhere—in narrative recurrence and in the repurposing of legitimate journalism—phenomena invisible to source-level prevalence metrics.

### Everyday misinformation is broader, blander, and closer to home

A third strand argues that even where misinformation is common, it looks nothing like the exotic, foreign-troll-farm imagery that dominates public imagination. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study of Australian adults finds that perceived misinformation is dominated by mundane text-based news headlines from mainstream and alternative domestic outlets—not fringe or anonymous sources—and spans everyday topics like business, economics, and celebrity gossip rather than only politics or health; independent verification further shows that much user-flagged content is not objectively false, complicating the very category of "misinformation" as ordinarily operationalized. [[Vincent_undated-re]] complements this from the platform-monitoring side, showing that even where prevalence is measurably rising (as on TikTok, from ~20% to ~25% exposure-weighted mis/disinformation) and low-credibility accounts retain an engagement advantage, this is documented through repeated, comparable measurement rather than anecdote—modeling the kind of infrastructure needed to track genuine trends rather than assume them.

### Contextualizing decline narratives and charting a way forward

[[Fletcher2026-lv]] supplies essential long-run context for the entire topic: trust in news has indeed declined globally since the 1980s, accelerating after 2000, but the decline is neither universal nor primarily an internet-era phenomenon, and even fifty years ago majority trust in news was the exception rather than the rule. This descriptive corrective—majority trust was never the baseline from which we have "fallen"—performs for trust exactly the recalibration the other papers perform for exposure and harm, cautioning against nostalgic or presentist alarm about an idealized past information environment.

Finally, [[Spampatti2026-kx]] turns the corrective outward, proposing that misinformation research mature by importing lessons from climate psychology: attend to real-world behavioral outcomes (currently measured in under 1% of studies), integrate individual- and system-level analysis, generalize across countries and offline contexts, and organize collectively against the same vested-interest pressures documented elsewhere in this topic's company-funded research. Read as a set, these fourteen papers do not claim misinformation is harmless or unworthy of study; rather, they insist that its scale, its channels, its content, and its effects be established empirically—and that once this is done, the picture is one of concentrated, everyday, and often modest-impact phenomena that call for more precise, less alarmist theorizing and intervention design.
