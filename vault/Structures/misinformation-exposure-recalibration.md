# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## The Empirical Turn Against Alarmism

The papers filed here converge on a single methodological wager: that claims about the scale and consequences of online misinformation should be settled by large-scale behavioral data rather than by intuition, anecdote, or small convenience samples. [[Budak2024-ef]] serves as the manifesto for this turn, explicitly cataloguing where public and scholarly discourse has diverged from evidence—on prevalence, on the algorithmic origins of exposure, and on the causal weight misinformation bears for societal harms. Nearly every other paper in this collection can be read as a data point supporting, qualifying, or extending one of these three claims.

## Prevalence Is Low, Concentrated, and Selectively Distributed

The most consistent empirical finding across this literature is that misinformation exposure, while real, is nowhere near as pervasive as alarmist narratives suggest, and what exposure exists is radically concentrated among a small subset of users. [[Grinberg2019-ua]] and [[Guess2019-ym]] established this pattern for Twitter and Facebook sharing during the 2016 election, showing that a tiny fraction of "supersharers"—disproportionately older and more conservative—account for the overwhelming majority of fake news traffic, while most people's news diets remain dominated by mainstream sources. [[Guess2020-rr]] extends this to browsing data, finding untrustworthy sites made up only ~6% of the average diet, concentrated among the most conservative decile ("deep but narrow" echo chambers). [[Allen2020-nj]] pushes the lens outward to the entire media ecosystem, showing fake news is a vanishingly small share (~0.15%) of total daily media consumption once television is included—a corrective to the field's disproportionate focus on online sources. [[Allcott2017-yz]] and [[Allcott2019-gn]] track this quantitatively over time, showing average exposure was modest even at the 2016 peak and that Facebook engagement with fake news fell sharply afterward. This concentration finding replicates well beyond political news: [[Lyons2026-ca]] shows the identical pattern for low-credibility health content (top 10% of users account for 77% of exposure), and [[Eady2023-xg]] finds the same for exposure to Russian IRA influence operations, where exposure was dwarfed by domestic political content and concentrated among strong Republicans least in need of persuasion. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] corroborate this concentration pattern using platform-scale European and US data, while cautioning that trends can still shift in politically salient years.

## Effects Are Smaller and Harder to Detect Than Assumed

A parallel cluster of papers uses experimental and quasi-experimental designs to test whether the (already limited) exposure that does occur translates into attitudinal or behavioral change—and mostly finds it does not, or does so only weakly. [[Guess2023-ai]] and [[Nyhan2023-gb]], both large field experiments on Facebook during the 2020 election, find that manipulating reshares or like-minded exposure changes feed composition dramatically but produces no detectable shifts in polarization or most political attitudes. [[Allcott2025-jb]] finds the same null pattern for political advertising. [[Bail2018-fk]] complicates any simple "more exposure helps" story by showing that cross-partisan exposure can backfire, increasing polarization rather than reducing it—a reminder that null effects are not universal and that naive interventions carry risk. [[Allen2024-av]] offers perhaps the most surgical dissection of the exposure-times-persuasion logic: fact-checked misinformation was more persuasive per view but reached so few people that unflagged, "merely" vaccine-skeptical mainstream content had 46 times the aggregate impact—a finding that both tempers alarm about flagged misinformation and relocates concern toward subtler mainstream framing. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] shows genuine but bounded effects (unsubstantiated fraud claims erode election confidence among susceptible partisans, but do not dent support for democracy itself), illustrating that "limited effects" need not mean "no effects"—precision and scope conditions matter more than blanket claims either way. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes back somewhat against pure null-effects orthodoxy, using causal epidemic modeling to estimate a modest but real toll from antivaccine tweets, suggesting recalibration should not collapse into denial of any effect, only right-sizing it.

## Structural Fragility of the Evidence Base

Several papers turn the recalibration lens onto the data infrastructure itself, showing how methodological artifacts can manufacture the very alarm the field seeks to explain. [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] dissect Meta's URL Shares Dataset, demonstrating that the 100-public-share censoring threshold—not underlying reality—inflates apparent fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold and produces spurious cross-country "trends" driven by feed-ranking changes rather than local events. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a comparable audit of NewsGuard, the source-trustworthiness metric underlying much of this literature, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications (rather than continuous scores) can single-handedly flip conclusions when one major outlet crosses the cutoff. [[Hartmann2025-px]] generalizes this methodological critique to echo chamber research writ large, arguing that inconsistent operationalization—not divergent empirical reality—explains why studies disagree about whether echo chambers even exist. Together these papers argue that "misinformation is worse/better than we thought" claims are often artifacts of measurement choices, and that recalibration requires methodological transparency as much as new data.

## Complicating the Picture: What Counts as Misinformation, and Where

A related strand questions whether the field's very categories—fake news domains, link-sharing, political topics—capture the phenomenon it claims to study, generally finding that conventional operationalizations understate the problem's breadth while overstating its exoticism. [[Yang2023-cg]] shows that link-based misinformation measures miss a huge reservoir of image-based political misinformation on Facebook, with right-leaning images especially prone to being misleading. [[Goel2025-iq]] shows that domain-blocklist approaches miss true content from mainstream outlets that is co-opted into misleading narratives, a phenomenon reaching roughly twice the audience of flagged fake news. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] extends this qualitatively, using diary methods to show that everyday, user-perceived misinformation is overwhelmingly text-based, sourced from mainstream and alternative news rather than fringe actors, and concentrated in mundane topics like business and economics rather than the "hot button" issues misinformation research fixates on—while independent verification suggests much user-flagged content isn't objectively false at all, complicating the very definition of the problem. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] adds that mainstream media themselves are a major (if inadvertent) amplifier of fake news narratives, likely reaching more people than the original fake sites. [[Marwick2025-vx]] similarly disrupts the "exposure equals belief equals radicalization" model, showing ethnographically that far-right belief adoption is a gradual socialization process rather than a discrete conversion event—another instance of the field's preferred causal story being empirically too simple.

## Echo Chambers, Polarization, and Perception versus Reality

A cluster of papers interrogates the echo-chamber/polarization narrative specifically, generally finding effects to be real but far more modest, mixed, or perceptual than assumed. [[Flaxman2016-lm]] found only modest ideological segregation from search and social media, with the bulk of consumption remaining direct visits to mainstream outlets. [[Brown2026-br]] applies a rigorous audit design to YouTube's recommendation algorithm and finds no evidence of algorithmically driven radicalization pathways, only mild echo-chamber effects (mostly attributable to user choice) and genuine but bounded content "rabbit holes." [[Oswald2025-km]] and [[Schulz2026-ts]] relocate the distortion from platform algorithms to user self-selection: a small, more extreme minority produces most visible political content, creating "false polarization" that misleads both citizens and researchers who treat visible discourse as representative of public opinion. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] documents a parallel perceptual distortion specific to misinformation: citizens systematically believe their own political side is targeted by misinformation more than their opponents, a "hostile misinformation effect" that may itself corrode trust independent of actual misinformation prevalence. [[Renault2025-uh]] shows that even bias-resistant, crowdsourced moderation (Community Notes) still finds a real partisan asymmetry in misleading content, undercutting claims that observed asymmetries are artifacts of fact-checker bias—an important complication for any simplistic "it's all measurement bias" reading of the recalibration literature. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] locates fake-news sharing within ordinary partisan motivations (especially out-party animus) rather than ignorance, reinforcing that fake-news behavior is continuous with, not exceptional to, everyday partisan communication.

## Broader Institutional and Comparative Context

Several papers widen the aperture beyond individual exposure metrics to institutional and cross-national context. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] offers a five-decade global perspective showing that trust in news has been declining since well before the internet, is far from universal, and even reverses in unfree-press countries—cautioning against attributing trust decline solely to digital misinformation. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] and [[Ventura2025-sw]] push the field beyond its WEIRD, feed-based defaults, calling for and providing cross-national and cross-platform (e.g., WhatsApp) evidence that current alarmist narratives are disproportionately built on US/Facebook/Twitter data. [[Mosleh2024-op]] reinforces this by documenting genuine cross-platform divergence in engagement with partisan and low-quality news, arguing single-platform studies are structurally incomplete. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] contributes further platform-scale diffusion evidence at the 2020 election, situating misinformation's virality within the broader (mostly non-viral) distribution of Facebook content reach.

## Theoretical and Field-Level Reflections

Finally, a set of conceptual papers steps back to ask how the field should reorganize itself given this recalibration. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that dominant metaphors—virus, warfare, pollution—each capture only one level of a genuinely multilevel, complex system, and that policy built on any single analogy will misfire. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] proposes borrowing methodological and organizational lessons from climate psychology, especially the need to measure real-world behavioral outcomes (currently under 1% of studies) and resist vested-interest capture of the research agenda. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] supply the cognitive-psychological groundwork underlying much of this recalibration: susceptibility to misinformation is driven more by inattention and heuristic processing than by motivated partisan reasoning, and corrections are harder than assumed but not futile with the right techniques—both papers modeling the kind of careful, mechanism-specific claims this literature as a whole is pushing toward, in contrast to the sweeping, causally underspecified claims of "infodemic" discourse. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] and [[Lazer2018-mm]] represent the earlier, more alarmed baseline against which this whole structure note's papers push back—establishing the falsehood-spreads-faster and echo-chamber findings that the concentration/limited-effects literature has since substantially qualified.

## Emerging Frontiers: AI and Automated Systems

A final, more recent cluster asks whether generative AI changes this calculus. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] finds LLMs are poor fact-checkers without curated retrieval, meaning automation is unlikely to either solve or dramatically worsen the misinformation landscape on its own. [[Triedman2025-uy]] documents how an AI-generated encyclopedia (Grokipedia) reproduces Wikipedia's content while systematically increasing citations to low-quality and blacklisted sources on controversial topics—a case study in how AI mediation can introduce new asymmetries even while remaining largely derivative. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] shows AI Overviews in search are inconsistent guardians against conspiratorial queries, effective for established conspiracies but sometimes legitimizing emergent ones. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Choi2026-bz]] turn to audience psychology, finding quality perceptions of AI-generated news are largely indistinguishable from human-written content pre-disclosure, and that difficulty in truth discernment carries over across encounters with synthetic media—suggesting the recalibration project must extend its careful, evidence-first posture to this new technological terrain rather than reflexively importing alarm from the misinformation debates that preceded it.
