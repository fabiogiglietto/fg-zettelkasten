# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## The gap between narrative and evidence

Public and scholarly discourse about online misinformation has largely settled into a set of stock assumptions: that false content is everywhere, that platform algorithms relentlessly funnel it to unsuspecting users, and that this exposure is measurably corroding democratic attitudes and behavior. The three papers filed here converge on a single corrective move — using empirical, large-scale, or naturalistic data to test these assumptions directly, and finding them wanting in similar ways, even though they approach the problem from very different methodological angles.

[[Budak2024-ef]] states the recalibration most explicitly, functioning as a kind of position paper for the cluster. It names three specific misperceptions circulating among journalists and commentators — about prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm — and marshals existing empirical literature to argue that each is overstated relative to what the evidence actually supports. Its central diagnostic claim, that exposure to problematic content is concentrated among a small subset of users rather than broadly distributed, sets the empirical template that the other two papers independently confirm through original data collection.

## Concentration as an empirical regularity

[[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] supplies the most direct test of Budak et al.'s claims, using platform-wide Facebook and Instagram data for all active US adult users in 2020 — as close to a population-level measurement as this literature gets. The findings are striking in their alignment with the "misunderstanding" thesis: average exposure to untrustworthy-source content was low, the distribution was heavily skewed toward a small minority of heavy consumers, and — crucially — an experimental reduction in exposure produced no detectable shift in political attitudes. This last point pushes beyond mere prevalence to address the causal-harm plank of Budak et al.'s argument directly, using experimental rather than observational design to show that even where exposure exists, its downstream attitudinal consequences may be negligible. Together these two papers model a kind of empirical pincer: one synthesizing the field's evidence base, the other producing new confirmatory evidence at platform scale.

## Misinformation without the alarm: everyday life as counter-evidence

[[Hourigan2026-oc]] approaches the same recalibration from an entirely different methodological register — not large-N platform data but a small-N digital diary study that lets Australian adults define misinformation on their own terms rather than through researcher-imposed categories. Its contribution to the shared argument is less about *quantity* of exposure and more about *character*: where the other two papers show that alarmist claims about scale and effect are exaggerated, Hourigan et al. show that alarmist claims about *content* are also skewed. Rather than exotic, fringe-sourced, audiovisual disinformation, what people actually flag as misleading in daily life is mundane — text-based news headlines from mainstream and alternative outlets, often about business, economics, or celebrity news, and driven by clickbait and sensationalism rather than fabrication. Notably, when the authors independently verified a subsample of flagged claims, only a small fraction were objectively false, reinforcing a theme that runs through all three papers: subjective perceptions of "misinformation," algorithmic exposure metrics, and objective falsity are three different things that public discourse tends to collapse into one.

## A shared corrective arc

Read together, these papers trace an arc from macro to micro, from causal testing to lived experience, all converging on the same deflationary conclusion. [[Budak2024-ef]] supplies the theoretical and evidentiary indictment of alarmist narratives; [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] operationalizes that indictment at platform scale, showing concentrated exposure and null attitudinal effects; [[Hourigan2026-oc]] complements this by showing that even where misinformation is encountered "in the wild," it looks less like the fringe, AI-generated, hyperpartisan content that dominates scholarly and journalistic imagination and more like ordinary journalistic sensationalism embedded in a broader crisis of media trust. The cumulative effect is not a claim that misinformation is unimportant, but a call — echoed across all three — to recalibrate research and policy attention toward where the evidence actually points: concentrated, heterogeneous, everyday, and causally modest, rather than uniformly pervasive and catastrophic.
