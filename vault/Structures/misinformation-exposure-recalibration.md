# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## The empirical pushback against alarmism

The anchor text for this cluster is [[Budak2024-ef]], which names three misperceptions distorting public discourse about misinformation: that problematic content is pervasive, that algorithms are its primary engine, and that social media is a demonstrated cause of large-scale societal harm. Much of the rest of the corpus can be read as either substantiating or stress-testing this thesis with large-scale, naturalistic data. The most direct corroborations come from platform-scale studies of exposure concentration. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds that Facebook and Instagram exposure to untrustworthy sources in 2020 was low on average, heavily concentrated among a small minority, and produced no detectable attitudinal effects when experimentally reduced. [[Lyons2026-ca]] extends this logic to health misinformation, showing that low-credibility exposure is rare overall but concentrated among older, more conspiracist-minded users, and travels through habitual cross-domain browsing rather than algorithmic or social referral. [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s large field experiment on political advertising similarly finds null effects on knowledge, polarization, and perceptions from six weeks of ad removal before the 2020 election — a companion data point to the "limited effects" argument, even as the authors caution about power to detect small turnout effects. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] complicate the picture with diffusion-level evidence: reach is concentrated in a small set of viral posts and diverges meaningfully by platform, suggesting that "misinformation" is not a monolithic exposure category but one whose dynamics depend heavily on where and how content circulates.

## Measurement artifacts as a hidden driver of alarm

A second strand interrogates whether apparent prevalence and harm are partly artifacts of how researchers measure exposure, trust, or ideological structure. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] shows that the 100-share threshold used to anonymize Meta's URL Shares Dataset produces near-identical cross-country trajectories that are almost certainly driven by feed-ranking changes rather than local political events — a methodological warning that cross-national or diachronic misinformation comparisons using this dataset may be chasing an algorithmic artifact. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a parallel audit of NewsGuard, finding that continuous trustworthiness scores are stable and usable but that binary "untrustworthy" cutoffs can flip conclusions when a single major outlet crosses the threshold — directly relevant to studies like [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], which documents rising *shares* of untrustworthy URLs in Germany and Italy's Facebook newsfeeds around election years even as the *share of views* attributable to those sources stays comparatively stable, and finds exposure and sharing skewed toward older cohorts, echoing [[Lyons2026-ca]]. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of echo chamber research reaches a structurally similar conclusion for a neighboring construct: much of the field's disagreement about whether echo chambers exist stems from inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than divergent empirical reality. [[Brown2026-br]] applies this same discipline to YouTube radicalization claims, using an audit design that separates algorithmic from user-driven effects and finding mild, user-driven echo chambers, strong content "rabbit holes," but no evidence for algorithmically-induced radicalization pathways — directly countering a canonical alarmist narrative with a more granular causal decomposition. [[Oswald2025-km]] and [[Schulz2026-ts]] push the measurement critique upstream of exposure entirely, arguing that the visible content researchers and citizens observe is skewed by a production-consumption gap and self-selection into posting, such that "false polarization" and misperceived public opinion are downstream of who chooses to speak, not what the underlying population believes.

## Misinformation is broader, more mundane, and less exotic than assumed

A third line challenges the *content* assumptions embedded in alarmist narratives — that misinformation is exotic, fringe, or overwhelmingly political/health-related. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds that everyday perceived misinformation is dominated by mundane text-based news headlines from mainstream and alternative outlets (not fringe sources), spans business/economics and celebrity content as much as politics, and is often not even objectively false when independently verified — suggesting that public concern is substantially about clickbait and trust erosion rather than exotic falsehoods. [[Goel2025-iq]] makes a structurally similar point from the supply side: source-level blocklists miss the larger phenomenon of *true* mainstream content being selectively co-shared to support misleading narratives, with such repurposed articles reaching roughly twice the audience of outright fake news. [[Marwick2025-vx]] extends the "less exotic than assumed" argument to radicalization itself, showing through qualitative analysis that most Far-Right belief adoption is a gradual socialization process rather than a single "redpilling" exposure event — undercutting the simple exposure-equals-belief model that underlies much alarmist rhetoric about extremist content.

## Where the recalibration meets its limits

Not every paper in this set supports a purely deflationary reading, and their inclusion sharpens rather than undermines the overall arc by showing where concentrated, real-world harms remain plausible. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] uses a compartmental epidemic model to estimate a causal, if modest, effect of antivaccine tweets on vaccination refusal, cases, and deaths — a rare attempt to causally connect online speech to offline epidemiological outcomes, and a reminder that "limited effects" claims should be domain- and context-specific rather than universal. [[Ventura2025-sw]] similarly finds that WhatsApp, an encrypted messaging platform largely invisible to feed-based studies, has measurable effects on political knowledge and belief in Brazil, arguing that the recalibration literature's near-exclusive focus on public feeds (Facebook, Twitter/X) may itself be a blind spot obscuring where real harm concentrates. Read together with [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue framing — which explicitly critiques the WEIRD-centric bias of misinformation research — these papers suggest that the "exposure is limited, harms are overstated" conclusion is itself partly an artifact of *where* researchers have looked (US, English-language, public feeds), and that broader geographic and platform coverage may complicate the picture. [[Vincent_undated-re]]'s ongoing measurement of mis/disinformation prevalence on major platforms in Europe, which finds TikTok's exposure-weighted share rising toward 25% and a persistent interaction advantage for low-credibility accounts, stands as a cautionary counterpoint: at least on some platforms, in some moderation regimes, the "concentration and limited exposure" story has not yet been confirmed.

## Trust, polarization, and asymmetry: recalibrating who is targeted and why

Several papers recalibrate not the scale of misinformation but the narratives around *whose* misinformation and *whose* trust is at stake. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century, 115-country analysis finds that trust in news has genuinely declined, but unevenly, predates the internet, and was never majority-held even fifty years ago — directly countering nostalgic narratives that treat pre-digital news trust as a lost golden age, and showing that the US is an outlier rather than representative. [[Renault2025-uh]] shows that Republican users are flagged substantially more often than Democrats even under X's crowdsourced Community Notes system, undercutting claims that fact-checker bias explains partisan asymmetries in misinformation sanctioning. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] complements this with a psychological account of why citizens *feel* targeted regardless of actual asymmetries: a "hostile misinformation effect" whereby people believe their own political in-group is disproportionately targeted, moderated by political interest, identity strength, and ideology — suggesting that public alarm about misinformation targeting is itself partly a motivated perception rather than a direct read of the empirical landscape.

## New frontiers: does the recalibration hold for AI-generated content?

A cluster of recent papers asks whether the "limited exposure, concentrated harm" conclusions generalize to generative AI, or whether new technologies reset the calculus. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s large-scale audit of Grokipedia finds it heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically more likely to cite unreliable and blacklisted sources on controversial topics, suggesting AI-mediated knowledge platforms can introduce measurable quality degradation even while appearing superficially comprehensive. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] finds that off-the-shelf LLMs are poor political fact-checkers unless given curated retrieval context, with search-enabled variants showing an ideological skew toward left-leaning credible sources — a caution against assuming AI tools will neutrally resolve, rather than reshape, misinformation dynamics. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] shows Google's AI Overviews respond unevenly to conspiratorial queries, robustly debunking established theories like chemtrails but sometimes legitimizing newer ones like "15-minute cities" by framing them as live debates. [[Choi2026-bz]] and [[Gilardi2026-hw]] turn to audience-side effects: the former finds carryover effects of deepfake-detection difficulty on subsequent susceptibility, while the latter finds experimentally that audiences cannot distinguish AI-assisted from human-written news on quality grounds, though disclosure prompts curiosity-driven rather than durable engagement. Together these papers suggest the recalibration project must be redone, not merely extended, for generative AI — the concentration and limited-effects findings established for legacy misinformation cannot yet be assumed to hold.

## Toward a more complex, self-reflective field

Finally, two conceptual papers step back to ask what kind of science misinformation research should become given this recalibration. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that dominant analogies — infodemic, information warfare, information pollution — each illuminate only part of a genuinely multilevel, complex-adaptive system, and that portfolio-style, complexity-aware interventions (rather than single-mechanism fixes) are needed. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] draws parallels to climate psychology, noting that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world behavioral outcomes and urging the field toward system-level, cross-context, and institutionally organized research resilient to the same vested-interest pressures climate science has faced. Read as a set, these two papers supply the meta-argument implicit throughout the corpus: recalibrating exposure and harm narratives is not simply a matter of correcting overstated claims, but of building the methodological and institutional infrastructure — better measurement, broader geographic and platform coverage, causal designs, and complexity-aware theorizing — needed to know what is actually true about misinformation's place in society.
