# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

### A corrective thesis

[[Budak2024-ef]] functions as something like a manifesto for this literature: it names three specific misperceptions—about the prevalence of problematic content, the centrality of algorithms, and the causal weight of social media in societal harms—that have become entrenched in public and even scholarly discourse despite running against the empirical record. Nearly every other paper in this collection can be read as supplying evidence for one or more of these three claims, using data and designs that move well beyond anecdote, small convenience samples, or post-hoc narrative.

### Measuring prevalence at the scale of the whole ecosystem

A recurring methodological move is to stop measuring "fake news" in isolation and instead place it within the full media diet. [[Allcott2017-yz]] was an early instance, estimating that the average American adult saw and remembered only about one fake news article during the 2016 campaign—modest exposure that would require implausible persuasive power to have swung the election. [[Allen2020-nj]] generalizes this logic across the entire information ecosystem (TV, desktop, mobile), finding that news itself is a small share of daily media consumption and that fake news amounts to roughly 0.15% of it, dwarfed by television and by ordinary news bias and avoidance. [[Grinberg2019-ua]] and [[Guess2021-ym]] bring the same ecosystem framing to platform-level data, showing that credible sources vastly outnumber low-credibility ones in both views and shares, even as fake news achieves nontrivial circulation in absolute terms. [[Allcott2019-gn]] extends the timeline forward, documenting that Facebook engagement with fake news fell sharply after 2016 relative to Twitter, undercutting narratives of an ever-worsening platform-driven crisis, even as it cautions that absolute volumes remain non-trivial. Together these papers reorient the unit of analysis from salient anecdotes to population-level diets, repeatedly finding that misinformation is a small slice of a much larger pie.

### Concentration, not ubiquity

A second, closely related thread shows that where exposure does occur, it is radically concentrated among a small subset of users rather than evenly distributed—a pattern so consistent across platforms, countries, and topics that it begins to look like a structural regularity of the information environment. [[Grinberg2019-ua]] finds that 1% of Twitter users accounted for 80% of fake-news exposures in 2016; [[Guess2020-rr]] shows untrustworthy-website consumption concentrated among the most conservative decile of Americans, making echo chambers "deep but narrow"; [[Eady2023-xg]] finds that 1% of users absorbed 70% of all exposure to the Russian IRA's Twitter campaign, concentrated among strong Republicans least in need of persuasion; [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] replicates this pattern for Facebook and Instagram in 2020; and [[Lyons2026-ca]] extends it to an entirely different domain—online health content—showing that the top 10% of users account for 77% of exposure, concentrated among older adults with poorer discernment and conspiracist worldviews. [[Guess2019-ym]] adds the sharing-side mirror image: over 90% of Facebook users shared zero fake-news articles, with age (not general shareyness) the most robust predictor of the rare exceptions. [[Oswald2025-km]] supplies a structural explanation for why visible discourse so badly misrepresents this underlying concentration: a small minority of highly active producers generates most content, creating a "production-consumption gap" that distorts inference for ordinary citizens and researchers alike—an argument that helps explain why concentrated-exposure findings are so often mistaken for ubiquity.

### Experimental and quasi-experimental null effects

Where earlier work establishes that exposure is limited and skewed, a cluster of large field experiments asks directly whether such exposure, however distributed, causally matters—and mostly finds that it does not. [[Nyhan2023-gb]] experimentally reduced exposure to like-minded Facebook content by roughly a third for over 23,000 users and found no detectable effect on any of eight preregistered polarization and attitude measures. [[Guess2023-ai]] similarly stripped reshared content from feeds during the 2020 election—substantially cutting political news and untrustworthy-source exposure—yet again found no measurable shift in polarization or most attitudes, with only mixed evidence of reduced news knowledge. [[Eady2023-xg]] shows the same pattern for foreign influence operations: despite real exposure to IRA content, there is no detectable relationship with attitude change or vote choice. The most nuanced entry is [[Allen2024-av]], which insists on separating exposure from persuasive impact and finds that flagged misinformation, while individually more persuasive, reached so few people that unflagged-but-skeptical mainstream content had a 46-fold larger aggregate effect on vaccine hesitancy—a finding that both confirms the "limited effects" pattern for narrowly defined misinformation and complicates it by relocating the harm elsewhere, in ordinary journalism rather than exotic falsehood.

### Big data, bigger methodological pitfalls

Several papers interrogate the infrastructure of misinformation research itself, showing that purportedly comprehensive "big data" can still mislead if its construction is not scrutinized. [[Allen2021-ai]] demonstrates that Facebook's heavily used Social Science One URLs dataset overestimates fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold because of a 100-public-share censoring threshold that disproportionately excludes less-shared, non-news content—a sobering methodological caution for a field that has leaned heavily on exactly this kind of data. [[Oswald2025-km]], discussed above, makes a parallel point about inference from visible online discourse more broadly.

### Rethinking the psychology of susceptibility

[[Pennycook2021-jq]] performs an analogous corrective at the individual-cognition level, challenging the popular assumption that motivated partisan reasoning is the primary driver of belief in false news. Instead, poor truth discernment is better explained by inattention and a lack of careful reasoning, and the well-documented gap between what people believe and what they share is attributable mostly to inattentiveness rather than deliberate spread of falsehoods—a finding that dovetails with the ecosystem-level results above by suggesting that interventions aimed at exotic ideological motives may be misdirected relative to interventions that simply redirect attention to accuracy.

### Widening—and complicating—the definition of "misinformation" itself

A final strand questions whether the field has even been studying the right object. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] uses digital diaries to show that everyday perceived misinformation is overwhelmingly text-based, attributed to mainstream and alternative news outlets rather than fringe sources, and concentrated in mundane domains like business and economics rather than the "hot button" political/health topics that dominate the literature—with independent verification finding only a small fraction of flagged content objectively false, suggesting perceived untrustworthiness often diverges from strict falsity. [[Lyons2026-ca]] makes a structurally similar point for health misinformation, showing that exposure pathways run through cross-domain, habitual browsing rather than the algorithmic social-media/search pipelines usually blamed. Read together with [[Allen2024-av]]'s finding that mainstream outlets drove most of the vaccine-hesitancy effect, these papers jointly suggest that the field's fixation on fringe, overtly false, socially-mediated content may have missed where everyday informational harm—such as it is—actually accumulates.

### A new frontier: AI-generated knowledge

[[Triedman2025-uy]]'s analysis of Grokipedia extends this recalibration project to a genuinely novel object: an AI-generated encyclopedia that is largely derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically shifts sourcing toward lower-quality and ideologically charged domains, especially on controversial topics. It serves as a bridge from the "is misinformation exposure exaggerated" question that dominates the rest of this collection to an emerging one—whether AI-mediated knowledge production introduces new, measurable forms of distortion that existing concentration- and prevalence-based findings have not yet had to reckon with.
