# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## Recalibrating Misinformation Exposure and Harms

### The founding corrective: exposure is rare and concentrated

The empirical backbone of this literature is a cluster of studies showing that, contrary to post-2016 alarm, exposure to "fake news" and untrustworthy content is both a small share of people's media diets and radically concentrated among a minority of heavy consumers. [[Allcott2017-yz]] and [[Grinberg2019-ua]] set the template: the average American saw only a handful of fake articles during the 2016 campaign, and engagement with fake news sources was dominated by the top 1% of users. [[Guess2020-rr]] extends this with web-tracking data, showing untrustworthy sites made up only ~6% of the average news diet, concentrated in a "deep but narrow" conservative sliver. [[Allen2020-nj]] widens the aperture further, situating fake news within the whole media ecosystem (television still dwarfs online news) to show it constitutes roughly 0.15% of Americans' daily media diet. [[Guess2019-ym]] and [[Eady2023-xg]] replicate the concentration logic for sharing behavior and for exposure to the Russian IRA campaign, respectively, finding that over 90% of users shared no fake news at all and that IRA content was both rare and dwarfed by domestic political content. [[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] show the same concentration pattern generalizes beyond political news—to health misinformation and to untrustworthy sources on Facebook/Instagram more broadly—reinforcing that concentration, not ubiquity, is the signature empirical fact.

### Null and muted effects from platform-scale experiments

A second pillar comes from large field experiments—many conducted in partnership with Meta—that manipulate actual exposure and find strikingly limited downstream effects. [[Nyhan2023-gb]], [[Guess2023-ai]], and [[Guess2023-ur]] together show that even substantial, randomized reductions in like-minded content, reshares, or algorithmic ranking during the 2020 election changed what people saw but left polarization, knowledge, and offline behavior essentially unmoved. [[Allcott2025-jb]] finds the same null pattern for political advertising removal. [[Allen2024-av]] complicates the picture instructively: fact-checked misinformation is more persuasive per view but so rarely seen that its aggregate impact is dwarfed—by a factor of 46—by unflagged, technically-true but "vaccine-skeptical" mainstream content, a finding that reframes the harm question around reach rather than falsity per se. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] and [[Bail2018-fk]] offer partial counterpoints, showing that elite fraud claims and cross-partisan exposure can produce real, if conditional, attitudinal effects—evidence that the "limited effects" conclusion is not absolute but moderated by partisan congeniality and susceptibility.

### Echo chambers and algorithms: real but overstated

A parallel strand interrogates the filter-bubble/echo-chamber thesis directly. [[Flaxman2016-lm]] and [[Bakshy2015-rn]] find modest, not extreme, ideological segregation, with user choice outweighing algorithmic curation in limiting cross-cutting exposure. [[Hartmann2025-px]] synthesizes 129 such studies, arguing definitional and methodological heterogeneity—not divergent realities—explains why the field seems so divided. [[Brown2026-br]] applies this corrective to YouTube, finding no evidence of algorithmic radicalization pathways, only mild, user-driven echo effects and strong "rabbit hole" dynamics. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] is a notable exception demonstrating a real, asymmetric algorithmic effect on X, reconciling prior Meta-study nulls through a persistence mechanism (users keep following accounts the algorithm introduced them to)—a reminder that platform and feature matter for whether "limited effects" findings generalize.

### Measurement artifacts and the perils of datafied misinformation research

Several papers turn reflexively on the infrastructure of misinformation research itself, showing how data and metrics shape apparent findings. [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] dissect Meta's URL Shares Dataset, showing that privacy-preserving thresholds and feed-ranking changes can inflate fake-news prevalence estimates fourfold or produce spurious cross-national "trends" that are really platform artifacts. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a comparable audit of NewsGuard, showing binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can flip conclusions even when continuous scores are stable. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] applies the URL-sharing paradigm cross-nationally, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares in Germany and Italy but stable *View* shares—again showing that headline statistics about "more misinformation" can mask stable exposure. [[Rogers2026-zz]] historicizes this terrain, tracing Meta's successive redefinitions of the "problem" (fake news → false news → misinformation → borderline content) alongside shrinking data access, arguing that depoliticized categories and metric choices (reach vs. engagement) themselves manage what counts as the problem. [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[Green2025-ap]] push the critique further, arguing that source-level (domain) classifications used throughout this literature systematically miss story-level repurposing of true content and story-level partisan heterogeneity—suggesting the entire source-blocklist paradigm underestimates a different, less exotic form of misleading communication.

### Virality, coordination, and visual content: testing the mechanisms of alarm

A cluster of studies interrogates specific mechanisms thought to drive misinformation's outsized harm. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] remains the canonical finding that falsehood spreads faster and farther than truth—largely via human novelty-seeking rather than bots—while [[Allcott2019-gn]] shows this Facebook-specific diffusion advantage subsequently declined sharply post-2016, even as it persisted on Twitter. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and [[Pante2025-pq]] apply rigorous counterfactual and control-based methods to show that coordinated inauthentic behavior and inter-state coordination claims are often weaker than assumed once benchmarked against random or matched baselines. [[Yang2023-cg]] complicates the "overstated prevalence" narrative by showing that image-based misinformation—neglected by link-based measures—is far more prevalent (20%+) and partisan-skewed than text-based estimates suggest, illustrating that recalibration can cut in either direction depending on what is measured. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] similarly tempers alarm about AI/visual disinformation, finding video and AI-image formats outperform text only in specific, high-salience contexts, not universally. [[Wack2026-bt]] and [[Emilio2026-ik]] extend this to the generative-AI moment, arguing deepfake impact is socially negotiated (through audience sensemaking) or systemic (eroding trust in "synthetic realities") rather than a fixed artifact-level threat—complicating both alarmist and dismissive readings of GenAI harms.

### Everyday, broad, and user-defined misinformation

Several papers argue the field's very definition of misinformation is too narrow, overly focused on politically "exotic" falsehoods. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] uses diary methodology to show ordinary Australians flag business/economics and celebrity content, mostly text-based mainstream headlines, as "misinformation"—and that independent verification often cannot confirm objective falsity, suggesting perceived misinformation diverges from fact-checked misinformation. [[Holt2026-zq]] reaches a structurally similar conclusion for Facebook's user-reporting system: flagged links mostly concern polarized political contextualization rather than outright falsehoods, implying user reports reflect disagreement more than deception. [[Nenno2025-xa]] globalizes this inquiry, finding only small, culturally variable differences in news-value prevalence between flagged and unflagged content across 24 countries, cautioning against WEIRD-centric assumptions. Together these papers suggest "misinformation" is a socially constructed, perception-laden category whose boundaries are contested by the very publics researchers study.

### Psychological and political mechanisms: reframing who, why, and with what effect

A final set of papers recalibrates not prevalence but the underlying psychological and political narrative. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Osmundsen2021-et]] jointly push back on "ignorance" and pure-partisanship accounts, respectively emphasizing inattention-driven sharing and out-party animus as primary mechanisms—both more mundane than the specter of systematically irrational believers. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] grounds the correction literature's limits in cognitive science, foreshadowing later empirical findings that debunking is harder, and less consequential, than assumed. [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] and [[Messing2014-jc]] show that selective exposure and social endorsement—not source partisanship per se—structure diffusion, pointing again to social-structural rather than content-level drivers. At the macro-descriptive level, [[Fletcher2026-lv]] shows that declining trust in news is real but gradual, pre-dates the internet, and is far from universal, undercutting narratives of sudden platform-driven epistemic collapse. [[Oswald2025-km]] and [[Schulz2026-ts]] identify a complementary distortion: the production-consumption gap and self-selection into posting make online discourse look more extreme and polarized than the underlying public, a lens that reframes "misinformation effects" research as partly a problem of visibility bias rather than belief change. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] shows a parallel perceptual distortion at the level of partisans' beliefs about who is targeted by misinformation, while [[Karlsson2026-hd]] finds that *media narratives about* interference can polarize more than interference itself—suggesting discourse about misinformation may itself be a non-trivial harm vector distinct from exposure.

### Toward synthesis: complexity, humility, and alternative agendas

Several theoretical and reflexive pieces attempt to resolve the tension between alarmist public narratives and this accumulating evidence of limited, concentrated, and often misattributed harms. [[Budak2024-ef]] states the thesis most directly: public discourse has diverged from evidence on prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that reductive analogies (virus, warfare, pollution) obscure the genuinely multilevel, complex-systems nature of misinformation dynamics, while [[Spampatti2026-kx]] and [[Tsfati2020-uo]] propose adjacent correctives—borrowing from climate psychology's handling of organized denial, and highlighting mainstream media's paradoxical role in amplifying the very falsehoods it seeks to debunk. Case studies of specific political contexts—[[Adam2026-tz]] on COVID conspiracy formation in Germany/Switzerland, [[Rossini2026-jn]] on Brazilian electoral misinformation and intolerance, [[Ventura2025-sw]] on WhatsApp in Brazil, [[Marwick2025-vx]] on far-right "redpilling" as gradual socialization rather than exposure-triggered conversion, and [[Renault2025-uh]] on partisan asymmetries surviving crowdsourced moderation—demonstrate that recalibration does not mean denying harm exists, but relocating it: in slow socialization processes, in mainstream media's own narrative choices, in perception and discourse effects, and in structural/platform-specific dynamics rather than in raw volume of fabricated content. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] remind us that even as the overall thesis holds for Facebook and Twitter/X, cross-platform heterogeneity (notably TikTok's rising prevalence) means the recalibration project must remain empirical and ongoing rather than settling into a new orthodoxy of reassurance.
