# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

The papers filed here converge on a single methodological complaint: much of what the public, journalists, and even researchers believe about online misinformation rests on engagement proxies, convenience samples, and rhetorically loaded analogies rather than on measured exposure and rigorously tested effects. [[Budak2024-ef]] states this thesis most directly, arguing that discourse on prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm has drifted from the evidence base, and it functions as something of a manifesto for the rest of the cluster—each subsequent paper supplies a piece of the empirical corrective that this position paper calls for.

### Concentration, not ubiquity

The most consistent empirical finding across this literature is that exposure to problematic content is real but radically concentrated among a small minority of users, not diffusely spread across the population. [[noauthor_undated-bm]]'s analysis of Meta's 2020 election deceptive-network data finds that three networks drove over 70% of total reach, that 1% of Instagram viewers accounted for 96% of network content views, and that deceptive content made up only 0.3% of political content viewed by the average user before the election—while also showing that associations with downstream political outcomes evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are accounted for. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds the same concentration pattern for untrustworthy-source exposure on Facebook and Instagram more broadly, adding the crucial experimental result that reducing this exposure produced no detectable attitudinal effects. [[Lyons2026-ca]] extends the concentration finding into a domain rarely examined empirically—health misinformation—showing that the top 10% of users account for 77% of exposure, concentrated specifically among older adults with weaker discernment and more conspiracist worldviews, and crucially that this exposure flows through habitual cross-domain browsing (other low-credibility sites) rather than search or social referral, undercutting platform-algorithm-centric narratives. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] applies a similar deflationary logic to coordinated inauthentic behavior itself, showing with a graph-theoretic cascade model that real coordinated accounts achieve influence far below optimal or even resource-matched benchmarks and are statistically indistinguishable from randomly placed nodes—suggesting CIB is less pivotal to diffusion than assumed. [[Oswald2025-km]] supplies the structural explanation common to all of these: a production-consumption gap in which a tiny active minority generates the visible discourse that both citizens and researchers mistake for public opinion, meaning skewed exposure is not an artifact of any one platform or year but a structural feature of social media itself.

### Complicating the causal chain from exposure to harm

Several papers push further, questioning not just whether exposure is common but whether it does anything. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s climate-messaging megastudy shows that even among the most-cited, ostensibly effective persuasive strategies, real-world effects on attitudes are small (1-4 percentage points) and effects on costly behavior (donations) are null or backfire—a sobering benchmark for how much any single piece of content, true or false, can be expected to move minds. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complicates the picture from the intervention side, showing that even a demonstrably truthful, persuasive corrective (an LLM correcting economic misconceptions) loses much of its power once users perceive the source as politically biased, implying that harms-and-remedies narratives which treat "correct information" as a stable, portable intervention ignore how source-trust conditions uptake. [[Brown2026-br]] dismantles the algorithmic-radicalization narrative specifically for YouTube, formally distinguishing echo chambers, rabbit holes, and radicalization pathways and finding that apparent ideological sorting is driven by user choice rather than the algorithm, that rabbit holes are real but do not compound into escalating extremism, and that the algorithm's only systematic bias is a mild, non-escalating pull toward moderate conservative content for everyone.

### Broadening and de-exoticizing what counts as "misinformation"

A second thread questions the definitional and methodological choices that made misinformation look both more exotic and more novel than it is. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds that everyday perceived misinformation is mostly mundane, text-based, mainstream-sourced clickbait about business and celebrity rather than fringe conspiracy content—and that independent verification confirms only 9% of user-flagged items as objectively false, suggesting a large gap between perceived and actual misinformation. [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[Green2025-ap]] jointly indict source-level (domain-list) measurement, the dominant operationalization in the field: Goel shows that mainstream, factually accurate articles are routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives and reach roughly twice the audience of "fake news" proper, while Green shows that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, especially for moderate-scoring outlets, meaning the whole ecosystem of research built on blocklists may be systematically mismeasuring exposure. [[Luhring2025-od]] turns this critique into a practical audit of NewsGuard itself, the source underlying much of this literature, showing that continuous trust scores are fairly stable since 2022 but that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs are highly sensitive to a handful of borderline sources crossing the threshold—a methodological warning that directly qualifies findings elsewhere in this cluster that rely on such classifications. [[Nenno2025-xa]] extends the de-exoticizing move cross-culturally, finding that flagged misinformation differs from ordinary news mainly in modestly elevated conflict framing, with effect sizes generally small and WEIRD-derived typologies of newsworthiness failing to travel well outside the US and Brazil. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies makes the parallel case for that adjacent construct: much of the field's disagreement about whether echo chambers exist reflects incompatible operationalizations rather than divergent realities, echoing the volatility Luhring documents for source-trust measures and the domain/story mismatch Green documents for partisan exposure.

### Conceptual overreach and its costs

Rounding out the cluster, two papers examine how the field's guiding metaphors and case studies themselves inflate perceived threat. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that the dominant analogies—infodemic, information warfare, information pollution—each capture only one level of a genuinely multilevel complex system and that interventions built on a single analogy (e.g., media literacy training modeled on "inoculation") can backfire by increasing generalized distrust, a caution that resonates with DiGiuseppe's finding that trust, not information content, often gates persuasion. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a timely case study of a new kind of information source—Grokipedia—showing empirically that even a putatively AI-native alternative to Wikipedia is mostly derivative of the platform it claims to supersede, with elevated citation of low-quality and blacklisted sources concentrated specifically in controversial and political articles, illustrating how alarm about novel technologies (AI-generated encyclopedias) should be calibrated against fine-grained empirical comparison rather than assumed a priori.

Together, these papers trace an arc from measurement critique (domain lists, NewsGuard, echo-chamber operationalizations) through exposure recalibration (concentration among small user subsets, limited reach, non-algorithmic pathways) to effects skepticism (small or null attitudinal impacts, fragile causal claims) and finally to a call for conceptual humility (complexity-aware framing, broader and more mundane definitions of misinformation). The cumulative message is not that online misinformation is harmless, but that its true shape—who encounters it, how much, through what channels, and with what consequences—is narrower, more concentrated, and less mechanistically straightforward than prevailing alarmist narratives assume.
