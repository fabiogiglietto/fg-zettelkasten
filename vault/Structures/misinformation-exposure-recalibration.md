# Recalibrating misinformation exposure and harms narratives

## The revisionist turn: from alarm to measurement

This body of work traces a sustained empirical corrective to what has become received wisdom about online misinformation — that it is everywhere, that algorithms relentlessly funnel it to unwitting users, and that it single-handedly warps elections, vaccination rates, and democratic trust. The foundational moves are almost archaeological: [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], and [[Allcott2017-yz]] established, at the dawn of "fake news" panic, that consumption and sharing were rare behaviors overwhelmingly concentrated among small, older, ideologically extreme subpopulations rather than diffuse society-wide phenomena. [[Allen2020-nj]] pushed this further by insisting that fake news be evaluated against the full information ecosystem — television, browsing, all content — rather than cherry-picked platform snapshots, finding it a vanishingly small share of the total media diet. [[Budak2024-ef]] crystallizes this arc into an explicit thesis: public discourse has drifted from the evidence on three fronts — prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm — and now warrants correction. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] extends the self-critique reflexively, arguing the field itself over-relies on cognitive proxies rather than real-world behavioral outcomes and could learn measurement discipline from climate psychology's encounters with organized denial.

## Concentration as the master pattern

A recurring empirical signature threads almost every paper here: exposure to problematic content is not evenly spread but pathologically concentrated among a minority. [[Lyons2026-ca]] shows that low-credibility health content exposure is dominated by the top 10% of users (77% of exposure) and skews toward older, more conspiracist-minded audiences, with pathways running through habitual cross-domain browsing rather than social media or search referrals. [[Eady2023-xg]] finds the same concentration logic for Russian IRA influence-campaign exposure on Twitter — heavily partisan-skewed, and with no measurable attitudinal consequence. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] replicates this for untrustworthy Facebook/Instagram sources in 2020: low average exposure, concentrated among a subset, no detectable attitudinal shift from experimentally reducing it. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends the concentration-plus-stability finding to Europe, showing untrustworthy-URL *shares* rising in electoral years while *views* remain comparatively flat — a dampening effect suggesting platform dynamics do not mechanically translate sharing into exposure. Even outside strictly political misinformation, [[Oswald2025-km]] frames this concentration as a general structural fact of social media: a production-consumption gap in which a tiny vocal minority generates the content that both citizens and researchers mistake for public opinion, a mechanism echoed in [[Schulz2026-ts]]'s account of "false polarization" via self-selected posting.

## Null effects: the experimental backbone

Where the concentration studies are observational, a cluster of large-scale field experiments supplies causal teeth to the recalibration. The 2020 Facebook/Instagram election studies — [[Guess2023-ai]] (removing reshares), [[Guess2023-ur]] (removing algorithmic ranking for chronological feeds), [[Nyhan2023-gb]] (reducing like-minded exposure), and [[Allcott2025-jb]] (removing political ads) — collectively demonstrate that even substantial, verified changes in what users see produce no detectable shifts in downstream political attitudes, polarization, or behavior. [[Ventura2025-sw]] pushes this experimental logic into messaging apps via a WhatsApp deactivation design in Brazil, testing whether the null-effects pattern generalizes beyond feed-based platforms. [[Brown2026-br]] applies an analogous disentangling logic to YouTube's recommender, finding mild-to-no algorithmic echo chambers or radicalization, though a subtler platform-wide conservative tilt and genuine "rabbit hole" dynamics remain. Together these experiments argue against a strong causal chain from exposure to belief change — a direct challenge to canonical diffusion-focused work like [[Vosoughi2018-at]], whose finding that falsehood spreads farther and faster than truth is frequently invoked as the alarmist baseline this literature revises (with counter-evidence on partisan asymmetry offered instead by [[Renault2025-uh]]'s Community Notes analysis).

Not all causal work points toward null effects, though, and the corpus resists over-correcting into a blanket "nothing matters" narrative. [[Allen2024-av]] complicates the picture by showing that *unflagged*, non-false vaccine-skeptical content had a far larger aggregate effect on vaccination intentions than explicitly flagged misinformation — relocating harm rather than dismissing it. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]]'s SIRVA epidemic model estimates a modest but real causal chain from antivaccine tweets to reduced vaccination and excess COVID deaths, illustrating that "limited" effects are not zero effects when aggregated across a whole population. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], an older touchstone, reminds the field that continued-influence effects are cognitively real even after correction, tempering any wholesale dismissal of misinformation's psychological purchase.

## Widening the definitional aperture

A second thread interrogates whether the field has been measuring the right thing at all. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] (companion to [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]], which appears to be a duplicated/renamed entry of the same diary study) uses digital-diary methodology to show that everyday perceived misinformation is dominated by mainstream and alternative news outlets — via clickbait and headline-article mismatch — rather than fringe sources, and spans mundane topics like business and economics rather than only politics and health. This directly complicates source-list approaches to measurement. [[Goel2025-iq]] makes a parallel methodological argument from the opposite direction: domain-level blocklists miss the substantial phenomenon of factually true mainstream content being repurposed to support misleading narratives, a mechanism invisible to any classification scheme built solely on publisher reliability. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] anticipates this by arguing mainstream media's amplification of fake news stories, not direct fake-news website traffic, may be the primary vector of public awareness and belief. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] steps back to critique the conceptual vocabulary itself — "infodemic," "information warfare," "information pollution" — arguing that no single analogy captures the multilevel, complex-adaptive-system nature of misinformation, and that alarmist single-analogy framings distort intervention design.

## Infrastructure and measurement critique

Underlying much of this recalibration is a sober reckoning with the data infrastructure the field depends on. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]] both interrogate Meta's URL Shares Dataset, showing that the 100-public-share censoring threshold and differential-privacy noise injection can generate spurious cross-country "trends" that are artifacts of platform algorithm changes (e.g., Meaningful Social Interactions shifts) rather than genuine social phenomena — a cautionary tale for any comparative or longitudinal misinformation claim built on this data. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a parallel audit of NewsGuard, the dominant source-trustworthiness database, showing that continuous scores are reasonably stable since 2022 but that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can flip results dramatically depending on whether single high-traffic outlets cross the threshold — a methodological trap for any study, including many cited here, that dichotomizes "misinformation" sources. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies reaches an analogous verdict for that adjacent literature: much of the field's disagreement about whether echo chambers even exist is an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than a real empirical dispute, complementing earlier optimistic-vs-pessimistic evidence from [[Flaxman2016-lm]] on search and social exposure to diverse viewpoints. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], reporting industry-style platform measurement, sit at the empirical coalface this methodological literature is trying to discipline, while [[Mosleh2024-op]] pushes for cross-platform comparison to avoid the field's chronic overreliance on Twitter/X and Facebook as stand-ins for "social media" writ large.

## Structural and comparative correctives

A final set of contributions insists that recalibration must also be geographic and temporal, not just methodological. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] and [[Humprecht2020-gd]] argue explicitly against the WEIRD-centric bias of misinformation scholarship, calling for comparative, cross-national frameworks of "resilience" to disinformation. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century, 115-country analysis of trust in news performs a similar service for the "crisis of trust" narrative: decline is real but non-universal, predates the internet, and even rises in unfree-press contexts — undercutting simple "social media broke trust" stories. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a "hostile misinformation effect," showing that perceptions of being disproportionately targeted by misinformation are themselves partisan and possibly more consequential for democratic trust than misinformation exposure itself. At the technological frontier, [[DeVerna2025-dl]], [[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Triedman2025-uy]], [[Kasianenko2026-tn]], and [[Choi2026-bz]] extend the recalibration project to generative AI and AI-mediated search — testing, rather than assuming, whether LLM fact-checkers, AI-generated news, AI encyclopedias, and AI Overviews behave as alarmingly as commentary suggests, generally finding uneven, context-dependent, and partially correctable shortcomings rather than uniform catastrophe. [[Marwick2025-vx]] closes the loop on the "exposure equals belief" model directly, showing qualitatively that even committed far-right adherents describe radicalization as gradual socialization rather than sudden informational conversion — reinforcing, from an ethnographic angle, what the quantitative null-effects literature suggests structurally: that misinformation's harms are real but diffuse, mediated, unevenly distributed, and considerably harder to locate than the dominant public narrative assumes.
