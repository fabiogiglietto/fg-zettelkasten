# Reviews diagnosing conceptual fragmentation in communication/media research

## A Shared Diagnosis: Growth Without Cumulation

Across these three reviews, a common pathology emerges: subfields of communication and media research are expanding rapidly in output but not in conceptual coherence. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] names this directly in its study of algorithm literacy, noting that a "rapidly growing but fragmented field" has produced 169 studies with no unified construct of what algorithm literacy even is. [[Guo2026-fi]] diagnoses the same condition in political communication's treatment of the "non-political," observing that scholarly interest has outpaced conceptual clarity, leaving the political/non-political boundary vaguely gestured at as "blurred" rather than theorized. [[Bravo2026-ue]] extends the pattern to climate change visualization research, where two decades of studies (2005–2024) have accumulated without converging on shared methods or scope. Read together, these reviews suggest that fragmentation is not incidental to fast-growing media-studies subfields but a structural feature of how such fields develop: empirical interest races ahead of conceptual infrastructure.

## Divergent Diagnostic Strategies

What distinguishes the three is *how* they locate and treat fragmentation. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] takes an integrative-synthesis approach, cataloguing the endogenous and exogenous factors scholars associate with algorithm literacy in order to build a cohesive framework from disparate parts — fragmentation here is a problem of scattered variables awaiting synthesis. [[Bravo2026-ue]] treats fragmentation more as a problem of *coverage*: imbalances in media type (traditional vs. social/digital) and geography (Western vs. non-Western) that leave the empirical map lopsided, with methodological innovation (especially computational image analysis) posed as the corrective. [[Guo2026-fi]], by contrast, refuses to treat fragmentation as mere inconsistency to be resolved through better definitions or broader sampling. Instead, it reframes the blurriness of the political/non-political boundary as *itself* theoretically meaningful — a relational phenomenon shaped by intellectual tradition, methodological paradigm (post-positivist vs. interpretive), and rhetorical convention. Where [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] and [[Bravo2026-ue]] implicitly assume that conceptual clarity is achievable and desirable, [[Guo2026-fi]] argues that the boundary-drawing itself is a site of power and should be studied as such rather than tidied away.

## Methodological and Geographic Imbalance as Recurring Symptom

A second throughline is the recurring observation that methodological habits track disciplinary paradigms rather than the phenomena under study. [[Guo2026-fi]] finds that categorical, binary operationalizations of "political" dominate quantitative work while contextual, fluid treatments dominate critical/interpretive scholarship — a split that reflects paradigmatic allegiance more than deliberate ontological reasoning. [[Bravo2026-ue]] identifies an analogous imbalance: traditional-media case studies concentrated in Western contexts, with social media imagery and non-Western media comparatively neglected, implying that methodological convention (and perhaps resource availability) has shaped what counts as a visualization worth studying. Both reviews gesture toward computational methods as a potential corrective — [[Guo2026-fi]] cites emergent spectrum/gradational computational measures of "politicalness" as a promising hybrid path, while [[Bravo2026-ue]] calls explicitly for computational, large-scale image analysis to fill the social-media and cross-cultural gap. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] does not foreground method imbalance in the same way but shares the aspiration toward a framework robust enough to organize future empirical and possibly computational work on algorithmic media use.

## Toward Integrative or Relational Frameworks

All three reviews ultimately call for frameworks that can hold a fragmented literature together, but they differ in what kind of framework they imagine. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] and [[Bravo2026-ue]] gesture toward synthesis-as-aggregation: pulling together scattered findings and filling empirical gaps (geographic, medium-based) so that knowledge can cumulate in the conventional sense. [[Guo2026-fi]] pushes further toward a meta-theoretical reorientation, proposing that the field adopt a relational rather than substantialist ontology of politics — treating (de)politicization not as an outcome to be measured but as a power-laden process to be theorized. This divergence suggests a broader methodological choice facing conceptually fragmented subfields of media research: whether cumulative knowledge-building is best pursued by filling in the map (more cases, more methods, more computation) or by interrogating why the map keeps coming out blurry in the first place. Taken together, the three reviews trace an arc from descriptive mapping of fragmentation ([[Bravo2026-ue]]), through integrative synthesis of fragmented variables ([[Gagrcin2024-dl]]), to a reflexive theorization of fragmentation as itself a meaningful object of study ([[Guo2026-fi]]).
