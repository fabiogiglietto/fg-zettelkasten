# Reviews diagnosing conceptual fragmentation in communication/media research

## A shared diagnosis across disparate subfields

Although these twenty papers address topics as different as climate imagery, radicalization, and Facebook's reaction buttons, they converge on a single diagnostic move: each argues that its subfield has grown explosively while its core concepts have not kept pace. [[Guo2026-fi]] names this most explicitly for the political/non-political boundary, [[Bravo2026-ue]] for climate-change visualization, and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] for algorithm literacy — three reviews that function as paradigm cases for the whole topic. Each treats its object (politics, visualization, literacy) as a contested boundary concept rather than a settled variable, and each concludes that inconsistent operationalization, not just empirical disagreement, is what prevents cumulative knowledge-building. This same structure of argument — rapid growth, conceptual looseness, call for integration — recurs, with local variation, across nearly every paper filed here.

## Boundary trouble: what counts as political, as data, as literate use

[[Guo2026-fi]] reframes the "non-political" not as residual noise but as a boundary object whose categorical or contextual treatment reveals scholars' implicit, often unexamined theories of what politics *is*. A structurally similar move appears in [[Annabell2026-ev]], where "data" in the EU's Digital Services Act is shown to be a "floating signifier" that platforms strategically narrow toward aggregated, quantified datasets, in tension with the plural, often qualitative meanings of data in academic research — a definitional indeterminacy with direct governance consequences for researcher access. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s algorithm literacy review sits between these two: it shows that a construct central to digital-platform research still lacks any unified conceptual architecture, leaving "literacy" as underspecified as "political" or "data."

## Geographic and medium imbalance: climate, misinformation, polarization, the internet itself

A second recurring fault line is geographic and infrastructural skew. [[Bravo2026-ue]] finds climate visualization research overwhelmingly focused on traditional Western media at the expense of social media and the Global South. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] makes the parallel case for political misinformation research's WEIRD bias, explicitly calling for comparative, cross-platform work. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] extends the same critique to polarization scholarship, arguing that its US-centric evidence base cannot support claims of global generalizability. Even [[Hegarty2026-qr]]'s historiographical analysis of "internet decline narratives" diagnoses a cognate problem: declinist accounts of the internet's trajectory recentre a handful of US corporations and a privileged, Anglophone "golden age," reproducing exactly the corporation- and West-centrism that the field of internet histories claims to resist. Across topics as different as imagery, belief, attitudes, and historical narrative, the same complaint surfaces — the evidence base is narrower than the phenomenon it claims to describe.

## Definitional proliferation in misinformation and disinformation research

Nowhere is conceptual fragmentation more visible than in the misinformation/disinformation cluster. [[Freelon2020-yp]] opens the modern debate by trying to stabilize distinctions among disinformation, misinformation, and propaganda, while recommending abandonment of "fake news" altogether — an early attempt at definitional discipline that the subsequent decade of research has not resolved. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] shows how the field's dominant analogies (infodemic, information warfare, information pollution) each illuminate one level of a multilevel system while obscuring others, producing partial and sometimes counterproductive interventions. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes the critique further into method, arguing that "narrative" has been used so loosely in disinformation studies that stories' meanings are treated as self-evident rather than interpreted, and proposes a hermeneutic-narratological corrective. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study operationalizes the fragmentation problem empirically: its expert panel explicitly worries that unresolved terminological debates about "misinformation" and "conspiracy theory" distract from more urgent, integrative, multi-level interventions. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] approaches the same immaturity from outside, using the more theoretically mature field of climate psychology to diagnose missing pieces in misinformation research — overreliance on cognitive proxies, neglect of systems-level and cross-national variation — that are symptomatic of a young field still converging on its basic constructs.

## The same pathology in adjacent subfields: echo chambers, polarization, radicalization, coordination

This diagnostic pattern repeats with striking consistency in neighboring literatures. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of echo chamber research argues that scholarly disagreement about whether echo chambers even exist stems largely from heterogeneous conceptualizations and operationalizations rather than from the phenomenon itself — an argument structurally identical to [[Guo2026-fi]]'s treatment of the political/non-political boundary. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] identifies a related but distinct form of incoherence in polarization research: recommendations for depolarization are rarely grounded in the evidence the same studies produce, resting instead on unexamined normative commitments to deliberative democracy. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review of online radicalization finds that fewer than half of publications even define their central construct, compounding Western geographic skew with conceptual underspecification. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] diagnoses the same problem in coordinated online behavior research, showing that both industry and academic definitions are operationally driven and theoretically thin, and proposes a general definition built from actors, actions, and intent as a corrective — one of the volume's clearest examples of a review not just diagnosing fragmentation but modeling integration.

## Structural causes: platforms, industry ties, and the limits of empirical social science

Several papers locate the sources of fragmentation not merely in scholarly habit but in the political economy of platform research itself. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] documents how undisclosed industry ties concentrate attention on user-focused topics like misinformation sharing while neglecting platform-level dynamics, systematically distorting which questions the field even asks. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s metascientific critique of the Meta2020 partnership shows that even a landmark, well-funded collaboration could not achieve conceptual coherence — different papers within the same project operationalized "echo chamber" differently — while its findings' rapid temporal decay suggests that reactive empirical social science cannot keep pace with platform change. [[Annabell2026-ev]]'s DSA analysis extends this structural critique into regulation, showing how platforms' narrow definitions of "data" constrain what integrative, cross-disciplinary research is even possible under new researcher-access regimes. Together these three papers suggest that fragmentation is not only a conceptual failure but an artifact of who controls data, funding, and infrastructure.

## Toward integration: multi-level, computational, and reflexive frameworks

Nearly every review ends by gesturing toward the same remedy: more integrative, multi-level, or computational frameworks capable of absorbing fragmented findings into a cumulative structure. [[Mahl2026-hc]] organizes interventions across macro/meso/micro levels; [[Rothut2026-wt]] does the same using Lasswell's communication elements; [[Frischlich2025-vn]] proposes a four-level complexity model; [[Mannocci2026-kc]] offers a three-component definitional architecture. [[Bennett2025-xs]] makes the integrative move at the paradigm level, arguing that technocentric (disinformation/algorithmic) and institutionalist (parties/elites) explanations of democratic backsliding must be fused into a single "communication-as-organization" framework. Even a narrower, more descriptive review like [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] on Facebook Reactions enacts this ambition in miniature, synthesizing a methodologically fragmented literature (varying data sources, platforms, and cultural contexts) into thematic clusters that at least make cross-study comparison possible. Read together, these twenty papers trace an arc common to a field taking stock of itself: naming the disorder, tracing its geographic and structural roots, and — increasingly — proposing the integrative, multi-level, or computational scaffolding that might let communication and media research accumulate rather than merely multiply.
