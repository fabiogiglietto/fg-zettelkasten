# Reviews diagnosing conceptual fragmentation in communication/media research

## A field diagnosing itself

A striking number of recent reviews in communication and media studies no longer simply summarize a literature — they audit it, asking why a rapidly growing body of work has failed to converge on stable definitions, comparable measures, or cumulative findings. Three papers filed here make this diagnostic move explicit around the specific subfields named in the brief: [[Guo2026-fi]] interrogates how scholars draw the boundary between the "political" and "non-political," treating that boundary itself as a boundary object shaped by intellectual tradition, paradigm, and rhetoric rather than by any stable ontological fact; [[Bravo2026-ue]] maps climate-change visualization research and finds it skewed toward traditional media and Western case studies, with almost no computational or large-scale image analysis; and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] synthesizes 169 studies of "algorithm literacy" only to find the construct itself inconsistently conceptualized across disciplines. All three converge on a shared diagnostic template: rapid growth in publication volume, inconsistent or underspecified core concepts, skewed empirical coverage (geographic, medium-specific, or disciplinary), and a concluding call for more integrative — often explicitly computational — frameworks that would let findings accumulate rather than talk past one another.

## Misinformation and disinformation: the most crowded diagnostic site

The misinformation/conspiracy cluster is where this self-auditing impulse is most intense, perhaps because the subfield's policy stakes have outpaced its conceptual infrastructure. [[Mahl2026-hc]] uses an international Delphi panel precisely to counteract what its experts see as a fragmented, siloed, overly individual-level research agenda, arguing for integrative multi-level (macro/meso/micro) and multi-stakeholder frameworks rather than terminological debates about defining "misinformation" versus "conspiracy theory." [[Frischlich2025-vn]] makes a parallel argument from a different angle: the field's dominant analogies (virus, warfare, pollution) each capture only part of a genuinely multilevel complex-adaptive system, and treating any one analogy as sufficient produces incomplete or even counterproductive interventions. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] diagnoses a narrower but related imbalance — an overreliance on cognitive proxies and Western/English-language samples — and proposes borrowing conceptual and organizational lessons from the more mature field of climate psychology. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] names the geographic dimension of this fragmentation outright, framing an entire special issue around correcting the WEIRD-centric bias of misinformation scholarship. [[Bennett2025-xs]], finally, pushes the critique up a level of abstraction, arguing that the technocentric disinformation literature and the institutionalist democratic-backsliding literature have developed in isolation from one another, and that only a "communication-as-organization" framework bridging the two can explain phenomena like the networked illiberalization of the Republican Party. Read together, these pieces trace an arc from calls for better definitions, to calls for multi-level models, to calls for cross-paradigm integration — each successive paper raising the ambition of what "integrative" synthesis should mean.

## Polarization and its neighboring constructs

A structurally similar conversation plays out around polarization and echo chambers, though with its own internal fault lines. [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows, across 129 studies, that scholarly disagreement about whether echo chambers and filter bubbles even exist is substantially an artifact of divergent conceptualizations and operationalizations — a methodological-pluralism diagnosis that echoes [[Guo2026-fi]]'s boundary-object argument but applied to empirical measurement rather than discursive classification. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] turns the lens specifically onto depolarization recommendations, finding that scholars' practical advice is usually disconnected from their own evidence and instead smuggles in unexamined normative commitments (chiefly deliberative-democratic ideals) — a different kind of fragmentation, between what is measured and what is prescribed. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] then names the geographic dimension of this literature's fragmentation, calling for integrated, globally equitable polarization research to counter its near-total concentration on US democracy. Together these three pieces suggest that "polarization" fragments along at least three independent axes simultaneously: conceptual (what counts as polarization), normative (what follows from it), and geographic (where it has been studied) — a multiplicity that no single integrative framework has yet resolved.

## Adjacent diagnoses: radicalization, data infrastructures, and historiography

[[Rothut2026-wt]] applies the same diagnostic grammar to radicalization research, finding that fewer than half of 382 publications over 25 years even define their central term, and using Lasswell's communication-process elements crossed with a macro/meso/micro scheme to impose order on a literature splintered by platform, ideology type, and online/offline assumptions — while arguing that the online/offline distinction itself is no longer analytically tenable. [[Yang2026-tq]] diagnoses a more infrastructural kind of fragmentation: social media research has been disproportionately built on Twitter/X and Facebook data, misaligned with actual platform usage, and now faces disruption as API access closes, raising the meta-question of how data-access regimes themselves shape what a field even can know about itself. [[Hegarty2026-qr]] offers a reflexive, historiographical counterpart to these empirical reviews, examining how "internet decline narratives" have become a totalizing organizing device for recent internet history — itself a form of conceptual over-consolidation (declinism as master narrative) that the article argues risks eclipsing the pluralism internet-history scholarship otherwise prizes. [[Wang2025-zy]], comparing AI imaginaries across four national presses, sits more loosely in this set: it does not explicitly diagnose field-level fragmentation, but its comparative method — operationalizing a qualitative framework (Cave and Dihal's hopes/fears) for cross-national quantitative comparison — exemplifies exactly the kind of integrative, computational translation of humanistic concepts that the more explicitly meta-scientific reviews are calling for elsewhere in this set.

## The common arc

Across all fifteen papers, a recognizable arc emerges: rapid, often technologically driven growth in a subfield outpaces its conceptual consolidation; reviewers respond by systematically mapping definitions, methods, and geographic/platform coverage; they find persistent Western and single-platform biases alongside genuine conceptual disagreement (not mere terminological noise); and they close by calling — with varying degrees of specificity — for integrative, multi-level, or computational frameworks capable of supporting cumulative knowledge-building. What differs is the proposed remedy's ambition: some papers (Gagrcin, Bravo, Hartmann) ask mainly for shared definitions and operationalizations; others (Mahl, Frischlich, Bennett) ask for genuinely multi-level or cross-paradigm synthesis; still others (Humprecht, Falkenberg, Spampatti) locate the deepest fix in geographic and infrastructural decolonization of the evidence base itself. Taken together, these reviews constitute less a settled consensus than a shared meta-scientific moment in communication research — one in which diagnosing fragmentation has itself become a recognizable genre of scholarly contribution.
