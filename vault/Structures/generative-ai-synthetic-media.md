# Generative AI & Synthetic Persuasion

## The gambling case as anchor: synthetic imagery as industrial-scale persuasion

The empirical core of this collection is [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]'s analysis of over 2,300 AI-generated images circulating through 223 coordinated Facebook gambling groups. It documents an exponential, statistically discontinuous surge in posting volume coinciding with ChatGPT's public launch, and shows that generative AI does not invent new persuasive tactics so much as intensify and recombine established ones — aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity likeness, cultural localization — into hyper-real, immersive visual grammars that scale far beyond what human-produced content or influencer endorsement could achieve. Crucially, the paper locates the harm not simply in the images themselves but in Meta's asymmetric governance of paid versus organic content, which lets synthetic imagery exploit a regulatory grey zone. This diagnosis — that generative AI's danger lies in amplification infrastructure as much as content authenticity — recurs as a structuring motif across the topic, echoed in [[Bouchafra2026-ts]]'s account of the Sweden Democrats deliberately privileging TikTok's memetic affordances over their larger Facebook/X followings to stage visual securitization for younger audiences, using decontextualized non-EU footage in ways that blur into disinformation even without generative synthesis proper.

## Quantifying persuasion: the "levers" research program

A second cluster treats AI persuasiveness as an object of direct experimental measurement rather than qualitative alarm. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] establishes, across nearly 77,000 conversations, that post-training and prompting strategy — not model scale or personalization — drive persuasive gains, with information density (the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims) as the operative mechanism, and reveals a troubling persuasion–accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim: frontier AI out-persuades even elite, coached human debaters and professional canvassers, converting attitude change into real donation behavior, with the same fact-density mechanism explaining the gap — an advantage that collapses once AI is throttled to human typing speed. [[Lin2025-xp]] extends this into live electoral contexts across four countries, finding AI persuasion driven by central-route facts rather than psychological manipulation, though right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less accurate than left-leaning advocacy — a partisan asymmetry inherited from the information environment rather than manufactured by the model. Read together, these three papers converge on a counterintuitive picture: AI persuasion is less about manipulation-by-personalization (the Cambridge Analytica-style fear) than about brute informational throughput, but that very throughput is what erodes factual reliability. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] closes the loop from a governance angle, showing that disclosing an AI's *persuasive intent* — not merely its machine identity, as under EU AI Act Article 50 — is what meaningfully blunts this effect, directly challenging the adequacy of identity-based transparency regulation implied by the "levers" findings.

## Coordination and scale: from botnets to swarms

A parallel strand asks what happens when persuasive AI is embedded in coordinated, multi-agent architectures rather than deployed as a single conversational partner. [[Orlando2025-ul]] simulates LLM agents running information operations and finds that mere mutual awareness among agents — without explicit deliberation — produces coordination (network density, narrative homogeneity, synchronized amplification) nearly equivalent to full collective decision-making, suggesting emergent coordination is a cheap, structural property of networked LLMs rather than something requiring sophisticated orchestration. [[Schroeder2026-im]] generalizes this into a policy-facing argument about "malicious AI swarms," mapping pathways — manufactured consensus, training-data poisoning ("LLM grooming"), coordinated harassment — through which persistent, adaptive agent networks could erode democratic deliberation, and arguing that symmetric "pro-social" swarms cannot reliably counter them given the asymmetric incentives of the attention economy. Both papers push the topic's optics beyond single-artifact deception (a fake image, a fake voice) toward the systemic, self-organizing dynamics of AI-populated platforms — a concern that dovetails with [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]'s finding that gambling promotion operates through *coordinated groups*, not lone bad actors.

## Deepfakes, fraud, and the audience as interpretive site

Where the swarm literature emphasizes production-side coordination, a third cluster interrogates reception. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates the assumption that visual/synthetic disinformation is inherently more persuasive than text, showing effects are issue-bound: decontextualized *real* video outperforms AI-generated stills only when authentic footage already exists to exploit, suggesting low-tech decontextualization may remain more consequential than generative synthesis in some contexts. [[Wack2026-bt]] pushes this reception-focused logic further with a Kenyan electoral deepfake experiment, arguing that a fake's impact is not fixed at production but "socially negotiated" through surrounding audience commentary — credulous comments amplify harm, incredulous ones partially neutralize it, moderated by partisanship. This reframes deepfakes as "flexibly deceptive cues" embedded in collective sensemaking rather than self-executing weapons, a claim with particular force in low-moderation, low-labeling Global South contexts. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extends the deception frame into financial harm, mapping the "scam ecosystem" of platforms, telecoms, and money-laundering channels that convert deepfake-enabled impersonation into billions in fraud losses, and arguing — consonant with [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]'s regulatory-gap diagnosis — that liability should shift from individual victims to gatekeeping intermediaries.

## Political economy: states, propaganda, and structural variation

A distinct set of papers locates generative AI's persuasive effects within pre-existing structures of media and political power rather than treating AI as an autonomous causal force. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows that state-coordinated Chinese media is memorized by commercial LLMs and measurably skews their outputs toward pro-regime valence when queried in languages tied to low-media-freedom countries — evidence that authoritarian information control is "laundered" into ostensibly neutral AI outputs through training data, a mechanism distinct from direct regulatory capture of models. [[Beacken2026-zb]] complements this with a comparative study across six democratically weakened states, arguing against technologically deterministic narratives: GenAI propaganda adoption is highly uneven, shaped by local political, economic, and infrastructural conditions rather than following one global trajectory. Together these papers caution against treating "generative AI persuasion" as a uniform, placeless phenomenon — a caution that sits productively alongside the topic's more universalizing threat papers.

## Theorizing the new epistemic and cultural terrain

Several papers step back to ask what generative AI does to the categories through which persuasion, truth, and media itself are understood. [[Emilio2026-ik]] synthesizes deepfake, fraud, and impersonation cases into a "synthetic reality" stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and names the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic content proliferates, societies may rationally discount *all* digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself — a diagnosis that gives theoretical shape to the practical harms documented in [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]], [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]], and [[Wack2026-bt]]. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] approaches the same rupture from fact-checking epistemology, arguing that AI outputs constitute a new category of "emergent facts" — plausible, prompt-dependent, not reducible to evidence-based, interpretative, or institutional fact categories — that demand relational rather than binary verification. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] situates all of this within a broader claim that "social media" itself is dissolving into algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private enclaves, and AI-mediated communication, with synthetic content (its "Shrimp Jesus" example) circulating precisely because platforms lack incentive to distinguish human from AI-authored material — a structural condition that explains *why* phenomena like the gambling case can scale unchecked. At the more vernacular end, [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Tang2026-gu]] examine the cultural residue of this ecology: "AI slop" and "brainrot" as an aesthetic and affective genre that Galip theorizes through Ngai's gimmick (working simultaneously "too little" and "too hard"), and which Tang documents empirically as an industrialized Chinese creative-labor practice that reverse-engineers virality — effectively the demand-side production logic underlying the same platform incentives that make [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]'s gambling images so effective.

## Governance responses and the democratic stakes

Finally, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] offers the collection's most constructive counter-note, arguing that LLMs could enable a second paradigm shift in digital public squares toward more deliberative, bridging-based discourse — via collective dialogue systems, bridging rankers, and AI-assisted community moderation — while warning that synthetic participation in deliberation risks diluting public agency and legitimacy. Read against the persuasion-lever findings and the swarm and gambling case studies, this paper marks the normative horizon toward which the topic's more descriptive and alarmed papers implicitly point: if information throughput, coordination affordances, and regulatory grey zones are what make generative AI persuasive at scale, then meaningful intervention — as [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] both argue in their own registers — must target intent disclosure, platform incentive structures, and gatekeeper liability rather than artifact-level detection alone.
