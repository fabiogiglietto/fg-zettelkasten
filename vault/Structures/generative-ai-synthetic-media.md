# Generative AI & Synthetic Media Manipulation

## Coordinated persuasion at industrial scale

The empirical core of this topic is a set of studies documenting how generative AI has industrialized visual and textual persuasion within coordinated campaigns. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] provides the sharpest case study: analyzing over 2,300 images from 223 coordinated Facebook gambling groups, it shows posting volume exploding by over 13,000% after ChatGPT's late-2022 launch, with a detectable structural break in mid-2023. Crucially, the paper argues that GenAI does not invent new persuasion logics so much as intensify and recombine existing ones—aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement, cultural localization—through hyper-real, dreamlike aesthetics that let synthetic spokescharacters evade the accountability mechanisms that constrain human influencers. This finding about *amplification infrastructure* as the real lever of harm dovetails with [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]]'s policy-oriented mapping of the deepfake financial-fraud "scam ecosystem," which likewise locates responsibility not in isolated fraudulent artifacts but in the platforms, telecoms, and financial intermediaries that enable scam content to reach victims and money to be laundered. Both papers converge on a regulatory diagnosis: individual-level detection and disclosure (labeling, media literacy) are necessary but insufficient, and governance must target gatekeepers and amplification systems rather than end users—Giglietto's "regulatory grey zone" between paid and organic content is a visual-persuasion analogue to Schiffrin's critique of Section 230 and jurisdictional fragmentation.

[[Beacken2026-zb]] complicates any assumption that this industrialization is uniform. Its six-country comparison (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey) argues that GenAI-enabled propaganda is mediated by local political, economic, and social structures rather than following a single deterministic trajectory—an important corrective to the more totalizing narratives found elsewhere in this cluster (notably [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s "synthetic reality" framework, discussed below). Read alongside [[Hackenburg2025-dj]]'s large-scale experimental work on conversational AI persuasion, the picture becomes more mechanistic: Hackenburg shows that persuasive power comes chiefly from post-training and prompting strategies—especially "information density," the sheer volume of fact-checkable-sounding claims—rather than from model scale or personalization. This offers a partial explanation for *why* synthetic gambling imagery and scam messaging are so effective: they saturate audiences with plausible-seeming, information-dense cues, even as Hackenburg's persuasion-accuracy trade-off suggests that the same techniques degrade factual reliability, a tension that echoes throughout the epistemics-focused papers below.

## The epistemics of synthetic media: trust, fact, and audience sensemaking

A second cluster asks what happens to truth, trust, and discernment once audiences must constantly negotiate synthetic content. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] offers the most systematic epistemological intervention, arguing that GenAI outputs escape the three traditional fact categories used in fact-checking (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) and proposing "emergent facts"—plausible, context-dependent, opaque outputs requiring relational rather than binary evaluation. This reframing usefully generalizes what [[Emilio2026-ik]] dramatizes at a systems level: Ferrara's "synthetic reality" stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and the "Generative AI Paradox" describe a world where the *cost* of verifying truth rises so steeply that societies may rationally discount all digital evidence, enabling strategic denial. Both papers share a structural intuition—that the problem is no longer discrete fake artifacts but an ambient epistemic condition—though Dierickx approaches it through philosophy of knowledge while Emilio compiles a case bank (Arup deepfake fraud, Biden robocalls, non-consensual imagery) to argue for a "defense-in-depth" mitigation stack and a research agenda around "epistemic security."

Empirical audience research grounds these theoretical claims. [[Choi2026-bz]]'s two-wave experiment finds a "modality-congruent carryover effect": difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes confidence and vulnerability toward subsequent fake content of the *same* modality (text-to-text, video-to-video), suggesting that epistemic damage from synthetic media is not just cumulative but format-specific. [[Wack2026-bt]] extends this audience-centered lens to a high-stakes political setting—the Kenyan Ruto-Odinga rivalry—showing experimentally (N=7,008) that deepfake impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated through surrounding comments and partisan priors: credulous commentary amplifies harm, incredulous commentary partially restores it, and cross-partisans are especially swayed by skeptical framing. This "collective sensemaking" argument pushes back against purely individual-cognition models of deepfake vulnerability and, notably, does so from a low-resource, Global South context that the mostly Western-centric epistemics literature (Dierickx, Ferrara) tends to underspecify—a gap that [[Beacken2026-zb]]'s comparative framing also flags. Zooming out, [[Baym2026-tr]] situates all of this within a longer political economy: her decade-later retrospective argues that generative AI's appropriation of user data and its role in "a general crisis of authenticity" are simply the newest instantiation of structural harms (wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction) she diagnosed in 2015, urging researchers to move from diagnosis toward interdisciplinary intervention—a call that resonates with the gatekeeper-focused regulatory proposals of Schiffrin and Giglietto.

## Cultural critique: slop, labor, and the aesthetics of generative content

A third, more culturally oriented strand examines the aesthetic and labor dimensions of everyday GenAI content rather than its use in coordinated deception. [[Galip2026-ix]] theorizes "AI slop" and "brainrot" through Sianne Ngai's theory of the gimmick, arguing these forms work simultaneously "too little" (labor-saving, deskilling) and "too hard" (straining to capture attention), embodying the contradictions of cultural production under "too late capitalism." This ambivalence—simultaneously degenerative and possibly resistant—provides a conceptual vocabulary for understanding the very aesthetic markers (hyper-real lighting, dreamlike saturation, improbable symbolic juxtapositions) that [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] identifies empirically in gambling-promotion imagery, suggesting a productive bridge between cultural theory and coordinated-campaign analysis. [[Tang2026-gu]] grounds this labor question empirically in the Chinese short-video ecosystem, showing how "AI slop" creators reverse-engineer viral formats and run matrix-account systems to turn virality from a serendipitous outcome into an engineered, systematized one—an industrialization of creative labor that mirrors, on the content-creation side, the industrialization of persuasion that Schiffrin and Giglietto document on the distribution side.

## Synthesis

Together these eleven papers trace an arc from *mechanism* (Hackenburg's granular experimental account of what makes AI persuasive, and the accuracy costs it entails) through *application* (Giglietto's gambling networks, Schiffrin's scam ecosystem, Beacken's comparative propaganda study) to *consequence* (Dierickx's and Ferrara's epistemological reckonings, Choi's and Wack's audience-level findings on discernment and social negotiation) and finally to *cultural diagnosis* (Baym's structural retrospective, Galip's and Tang's accounts of slop as both aesthetic and labor regime). A recurring tension threads all four registers: the same properties that make generative AI content persuasive, viral, or attention-capturing—density, plausibility, hyper-real aesthetics, scalability—are precisely what erode accuracy, trust, and the possibility of shared verification. The literature converges, sometimes explicitly (Schiffrin, Giglietto) and sometimes implicitly (Baym, Ferrara), on the claim that individual media literacy and detection tools are inadequate responses, redirecting attention toward gatekeepers, amplification infrastructures, and—per Beacken and Wack—the local political and social contexts that determine how synthetic persuasion actually lands.
