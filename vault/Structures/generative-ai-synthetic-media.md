# Generative AI & Synthetic Media Manipulation

## The platform substrate: from social graphs to synthetic feeds

The broader backdrop for this topic is a diagnosed structural shift in social media itself. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues that the "social media" paradigm — user-generated content circulating through social graphs — is dissolving into three post-social formations: algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication, with generative AI enabling "platforms without users." [[Helmond2026-ll]] traces the same transition from the infrastructural side, showing how Meta's retirement of the Like button marks a move away from social-graph signal-capture toward AI-driven inference, and reframes platformization as the ongoing work of making new domains (now including space-based AI compute) "platform-ready." [[Baym2026-tr]] supplies the normative stock-taking: revisiting her own decade-old diagnosis of social media's threats, she finds wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, and data extraction intensified rather than resolved, with generative AI adding non-consensual deepfakes and a general "crisis of authenticity" to the ledger. [[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes this further into political economy, arguing that scams are not peripheral but constitutive of platform capitalism — a frame that directly anticipates the gambling and fraud cases below, since anti-fraud infrastructures and AI tools co-evolve with the scam economy they purport to police. Together these four papers establish *why* synthetic promotional and deceptive content proliferates: it is not an anomaly bolted onto social media but a symptom of platforms' underlying reorientation around algorithmic inference, private communicative enclaves, and blurred legitimate/illegitimate economic activity.

## Coordinated commercial manipulation: gambling and scam ecosystems

The empirical core of the gambling-promotion strand is [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]], which documents an exponential, AI-coincident expansion of coordinated Facebook gambling networks after ChatGPT's late-2022 launch, showing how generative AI intensifies (rather than replaces) existing visual-persuasion drivers — aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement, cultural localization — while exploiting Meta's asymmetric governance of paid versus organic content. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] situates this gambling case alongside two structurally similar but motivationally distinct operations (a pro-Putin propaganda network and an unmoderated adult-content/fraud network) to propose "Deceptive Information Operations" as a unifying analytic category — arguing that what unites these cases is not shared intent but a shared operational logic of concealment, affordance exploitation, and coordination, which the authors term the "Zombie Internet." This reframing is important: it explicitly refuses to treat gambling promotion, state propaganda, and petty fraud as separate phenomena, instead reading them as symptoms of the same engagement-driven platform design. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] zooms out to the policy register, mapping the deepfake-enabled financial-fraud ecosystem (platforms, telecoms, banks, laundering networks) and arguing — in a move that rhymes with Giglietto and Giada's critique of Meta's governance gaps — that regulatory liability must shift from individual victims to gatekeeper intermediaries. Read together with [[Swartz2026-zb]]'s claim that scam/legitimate boundaries are discursively and infrastructurally co-produced, this cluster traces an arc from micro-level visual persuasion techniques, through meso-level platform-governance failure, to macro-level regulatory diagnosis.

## Information operations, propaganda, and coordinated inauthenticity

A second line of inquiry concerns explicitly political coordinated manipulation, old and new. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] provides a historical-organizational baseline, using leaked CVs to reconstruct the Internet Research Agency as a conventional media-labor organization rather than a purely covert cell — a reminder that "troll factories" long predate generative AI and were already industrialized propaganda-as-PR operations. [[Orlando2025-ul]] asks what happens when such coordination is automated end-to-end: simulating LLM agents in an information-operation testbed, it shows that even minimal "teammate awareness" among synthetic agents produces network cohesion, narrative convergence, and amplification synchronization nearly matching explicit top-down coordination — suggesting that generative AI could soon make the organizational labor documented by Poliakoff largely unnecessary. [[Beacken2026-zb]] complicates any assumption of uniform GenAI-driven propaganda diffusion, comparing six democratically weakened states and arguing that local political-economic structures, not technological affordance alone, determine how generative AI is taken up for propaganda. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Crichton2026-kn]] narrow in on the far right specifically: the former maps AI-generated multimedia in far-right 2024 European election campaigning on Instagram and X, while the latter argues, through a "re-generative" pipeline experiment, that commercial image models do not neutralize extremist imagery but sanitize its explicit markers while preserving ideological legibility for in-group audiences — a structural bias argument that echoes Beacken's warning against technologically deterministic readings by instead locating the problem in model design itself. [[Topinka2026-hb]] offers the clearest theoretical bridge between this cluster and the "slop" cluster below, coining "slopaganda" to describe AI political content that works not through deception but through affective attunement to feed ecologies — ideological intensification rather than persuasion.

## Reception and effects: how audiences make sense of synthetic deception

Where the previous cluster asks how synthetic propaganda is produced and coordinated, a complementary set of studies asks how it lands. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] runs a controlled comparison of textual, AI-generated image, and decontextualized video disinformation, finding that the presumed superiority of visual (and especially AI-generated) disinformation is issue-dependent — real decontextualized footage of a salient conflict outperforms both AI stills and text, while fact-checks suppress credibility fairly uniformly across modalities. This tempers alarmist assumptions found elsewhere in the topic (e.g., in [[Emilio2026-ik]]) about generative AI's unprecedented persuasive power. [[Wack2026-bt]] shifts focus to the social environment of reception: a large Kenyan survey experiment shows that a political deepfake's effect on candidate support is not fixed at production but negotiated through surrounding audience comments, with partisanship strongly moderating credulity — directly supporting the "collective sensemaking" framing that Topinka's slopaganda concept and Crichton's ambient-cue argument also gesture toward. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] rounds out this cluster from a platform-affordance angle, showing that on Canadian TikTok, pro-immigration content dominates anti-immigration content and that generative AI appears mainly as a tool for "speculative worldbuilding" in a minority of hostile videos — evidence that affordances and audience composition, not technology alone, shape outcomes, and a useful counterweight to techno-deterministic narratives of AI-driven manipulation.

## Theorizing synthetic media: slop, epistemic security, and the demise of verification

A final cluster steps back to theorize what generative AI's visual and textual outputs *are* and what their proliferation does to shared epistemic infrastructure. [[Emilio2026-ik]] offers the most totalizing account, formalizing "synthetic reality" as a four-layer stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and naming the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic content becomes ubiquitous, societies may rationally discount all digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself — a claim that resonates with Baym's and Tornberg's diagnoses of eroding shared epistemic ground. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Tang2026-gu]] examine the aesthetic and labor dimensions of "AI slop" from different angles: Galip, drawing on Ngai's theory of the gimmick, reads slop/brainrot as simultaneously deskilled and overworked cultural forms that expose contradictions of "too late capitalism," while Tang grounds the same phenomenon empirically in Chinese platform labor, showing how creators industrialize virality itself into a manageable, benchmarked production process rather than a stochastic event. Both studies push back against purely deception-centered framings of synthetic media, insisting that much AI content is not trying to fool anyone but is organized around attention-capture and engineered engagement — a point [[Topinka2026-hb]] and [[Crichton2026-kn]] make explicitly for political content ("obviously fake but affectively real") and that [[Galip2026-ix]] generalizes to generative culture at large. Taken together, this cluster suggests the topic's center of gravity is shifting: from a first-wave concern with deepfakes-as-deception toward a second-wave concern with slop, synthetic reality, and affective attunement as the more pervasive, harder-to-regulate modes through which generative AI reshapes promotional and political communication alike.
