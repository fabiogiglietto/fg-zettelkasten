# Generative AI and Synthetic Media

## From platform society to AI society: a shifting object of study

The starting point for this cluster is a double diagnosis: social media studies and disinformation research were built for an ecosystem that is dissolving. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues that the 'social media' paradigm — user-generated content circulating through social graphs — is giving way to three post-social formations: algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication, with generative AI severing the link between platforms and human authorship ("platforms without users"). [[Tornberg2026-im]] extends this into political-economic terms, tracing a tendential shift from the platform society to an "AI society" organized around cognitive lock-in, generative (rather than predictive) epistemics, and alignment politics as the new terrain of struggle over what models can say. [[Baym2026-tr]] supplies the affective and normative bookend: revisiting her own 2015 diagnosis of social media's threats, she finds wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, and data extraction all worse, now compounded by generative AI's appropriation of user content for training and its production of non-consensual synthetic imagery. Together these three pieces set the macro frame the rest of the collection works within — not "is generative AI bad for information integrity" but "what kind of mediated world is emerging, and what analytic and pedagogical vocabulary does it require." This framing question is also what structures the accompanying teaching materials on generative AI and media: the course treats the empirical studies below as case material for exactly the conceptual transition these three papers theorize.

## The mechanics and limits of AI persuasion

A tight empirical cluster asks, with unusual rigor, *how* and *how much* conversational AI actually persuades. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] isolates the levers: post-training and prompting strategies outperform scale or personalization, and the dominant mechanism is information density — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims — which comes bundled with a persuasion–accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the test, pitting AI against elite debaters and professional canvassers and finding AI reliably out-persuades even coached experts, again via throughput of claims rather than rapport; constraining AI to human writing speed erases its advantage, pinning down fact density as the causal mechanism. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates this in real electoral settings across four countries, showing AI dialogue beats traditional ads, persuades on both sides of the aisle, and relies on evidence rather than manipulation — while also documenting a partisan accuracy asymmetry (AI advocating right-leaning candidates makes more false claims). [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] then asks what governs this capacity: identity disclosures ("this is an AI") do nothing to blunt persuasion, but disclosing *persuasive intent* roughly halves it, suggesting current transparency regulation (e.g., EU AI Act Art. 50) targets the wrong variable. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates the picture from the visual side, showing that AI-generated images do not uniformly out-persuade text or low-tech decontextualized video — context, issue salience, and the availability of plausible real footage matter more than the technology itself. Read together, this group pushes back against both AI-hype and complacency: persuasion is real, measurable, and mechanistically specific (facts, not psychological trickery), but its magnitude is contingent on format, issue, and disclosure design in ways that should inform both policy and media-literacy pedagogy.

## Visual persuasion, slop, and the aesthetics of synthetic media

A second strand treats generative AI not as a persuasion *technique* but as a visual-cultural *form*. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] offers the collection's most concrete case study of synthetic visual persuasion: coordinated Facebook gambling networks that saw a 13,000%+ post-volume increase after ChatGPT's launch, deploying hyper-real AI aesthetics to intensify established persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO) while exploiting Meta's organic/paid governance gap. [[Manovich2026-ih]] provides the medium-theoretical counterpoint, bracketing ethics to analyze generative image models as a medium with built-in "media cognition," structurally conservative and anti-avant-garde, biased toward academic/photographic convention — a lens that helps explain *why* AI-generated gambling or propaganda imagery converges on familiar, hyper-real tropes rather than disrupting them. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Topinka2026-hb]] theorize the resulting genre as "slop": Galip, via Ngai's gimmick, reads slop/brainrot as working simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," exposing contradictions of cultural production under platform capitalism; Topinka's "slopaganda" reframes AI political content as an affective lure rather than a deceptive one — obviously fake but emotionally resonant, intensifying existing ideological formations rather than persuading anyone of a claim. [[Crichton2026-kn]] empirically operationalizes this intuition, showing that commercial image models sanitize explicit far-right markers while preserving or displacing the underlying ideology through ambient cues legible to in-group audiences — evidence, they argue, that the affinity between far-right visual culture and generative models is structural, not accidental. [[Tang2026-gu]] and [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] extend the genre analysis into production practice and campaign use respectively: Chinese "AI slop" creators who engineer virality as a manageable production process, and far-right European campaigns integrating AI multimedia into populist Instagram/X strategy. Across this strand, a consistent claim emerges that cuts against the persuasion-mechanics cluster above: visual synthetic media's power may lie less in truth-claims that could be fact-checked than in affective, aesthetic, and ideological resonance that resists the credibility/accuracy framework altogether.

## Coordinated and institutional manipulation at scale

A third cluster moves from individual persuasion and genre to infrastructural and institutional scale. [[Orlando2025-ul]] simulates LLM-agent swarms and finds that mere mutual awareness among agents — without explicit coordination instructions — produces network density, narrative convergence, and synchronized amplification nearly matching deliberate collective action, a sobering lower bound for how little design is needed to generate coordinated inauthentic behavior. [[Schroeder2026-im]] extrapolates this into a governance-facing argument about "malicious AI swarms," mapping pathways from manufactured consensus to training-data poisoning ("LLM grooming") and proposing layered, incentive-based defenses rather than naive detection or heavy state regulation. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] shifts from political to financial manipulation, mapping the deepfake-scam ecosystem (platforms, telecoms, banks, laundering networks) and arguing — in a move that parallels Schroeder's skepticism of voluntary compliance — that liability must shift from victims to gatekeepers. [[Waight2026-ts]] identifies a subtler, non-coordinated institutional pathway: state-controlled media entering training corpora and measurably shifting commercial LLM outputs toward pro-regime valence by query language, showing that political power can shape generative AI indirectly, through data, without any swarm or campaign at all. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a parallel case at the level of knowledge infrastructure rather than social platforms: Grokipedia's divergence from Wikipedia toward longer articles, more unreliable/blacklisted sources, and ideological skew on controversial topics, illustrating how a single actor's AI-generated encyclopedia can quietly re-architect what counts as reference knowledge. [[Beacken2026-zb]] provides the comparative corrective to all of the above, arguing against deterministic narratives of GenAI's uniform political effects by showing that propaganda uses vary across six democratically weakened countries according to local political-economic structure. Taken together, this group traces an escalating ladder of manipulation — from emergent agentic coordination, to organized scam ecosystems, to training-data-level state influence, to platform-level knowledge capture — while Beacken insists that none of these mechanisms plays out identically across contexts.

## Audiences, trust, and the epistemology of AI-generated content

The final strand turns from production to reception, asking how audiences and institutions make sense of (and decide to trust) AI-generated content. [[Choi2026-bz]] documents a "modality-congruent carryover effect," in which difficulty authenticating one piece of (deep)fake content shapes confidence and vulnerability toward later fakes in the same modality — evidence that trust calibration is dynamic and path-dependent rather than fixed per artifact. [[Wack2026-bt]] extends this into a social register with a large Kenyan field experiment: deepfake impact is not fixed at the point of production but "socially negotiated" through visible peer commentary, with partisanship moderating whether skeptical or credulous framing wins out. On the institutional-epistemology side, [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues that generative AI outputs break the three traditional fact categories used in fact-checking (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based), proposing "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category requiring relational, indicator-based assessment rather than binary verification — a conceptual move that [[Emilio2026-ik]] operationalizes at societal scale via the idea of "synthetic realities," a four-layer stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) culminating in the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic content proliferates, rational actors may discount all digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself. Two journalism-specific studies test these dynamics empirically: [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that undisclosed AI journalism is rated no differently than human-written news, but disclosure produces a short-lived curiosity bump rather than durable acceptance; [[Mattis2026-gu]] refines this by task, showing disclosure penalties scale with the journalistic function disclosed (fact-checking hit hardest, idea/image generation least), moderated by political position and AI literacy rather than topic. Together this strand suggests that the deepfake/disinformation "problem" is less a fixed deception-detection challenge than an ongoing, socially and institutionally mediated negotiation over what counts as verifiable at all — directly feeding back into the persuasion-mechanics and slop-aesthetics strands above, since both audience trust calibration and the affective resonance of slop operate on the same shifting epistemic ground that Dierickx and Ferrara attempt to theorize.
