# Generative AI & Synthetic Media Manipulation

## The empirical turn: measuring AI's persuasive power

A cluster of large-scale experiments anchors this topic empirically, establishing that conversational AI is not merely a *hypothetical* persuasion threat but a measured one. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] decomposes the "levers" of persuasion across 19 LLMs and tens of thousands of conversations, finding that post-training and prompting strategy matter more than scale, and that the active ingredient is information density — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims a model can produce per turn. Crucially, the same levers that boost persuasiveness degrade factual accuracy, establishing a persuasion–accuracy trade-off that recurs throughout this literature. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim by pitting AI against *expert* human persuaders — elite debaters, professional canvassers — and showing AI wins even there, with coaching barely closing the gap; only throttling AI to human throughput erases its edge, confirming that fact density, not rapport or personalization, is the mechanism. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates this in real electoral contexts across four countries, showing AI dialogue shifts voter preferences more than traditional ads, again via "central route" evidence-based argument rather than manipulation — though it surfaces a partisan asymmetry, with right-leaning advocacy producing less accurate claims. Together these papers push back against speculative "Cambridge Analytica"-style fears of psychological microtargeting, relocating the threat in sheer informational throughput. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] extends this programme into governance: identity disclosure (the EU AI Act model) does nothing to blunt persuasion, but disclosing *persuasive intent* roughly halves it — suggesting regulation should target what a system is trying to do, not merely what it is.

## Visual persuasion, coordination, and the gambling/deepfake economy

A second cluster shifts from text-based dialogue to *visual* and coordinated manipulation. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] offers this topic's paradigm case: an empirical study of coordinated Facebook gambling networks whose posting volume exploded exponentially after ChatGPT's launch, with generative AI not replacing but intensifying existing visual persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, cultural localization) and exploiting the regulatory gap between paid and organic content. This sits productively against [[Hameleers2026-mc]], which complicates assumptions about visual disinformation's special potency: AI-generated images do not universally outperform text, and old-fashioned decontextualized video can be *more* credible than synthetic imagery when ample authentic footage exists — a sobering corrective to generative-AI alarmism. [[Choi2026-bz]] adds a cognitive dimension, showing that difficulty authenticating one deepfake carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to weaken discernment of subsequent fakes — implying that repeated exposure erodes audience resilience cumulatively rather than fire having a single first impact. [[Wack2026-bt]], working in Kenya, relocates deepfake impact from the artifact itself to its social reception: comments and partisan framing around a deepfake substantially determine whether it damages a candidate, reframing deepfakes as "flexibly deceptive cues" whose power is negotiated collectively rather than fixed at production. [[Bosch2024-hj]] extends the modality question to sound, documenting TikTok's audio layer as an under-examined, platform-specific propaganda vector in Ukraine-war disinformation. Read together, this cluster argues for *context-dependence* over any uniform "deepfakes are devastating" narrative — the degree of harm depends on modality, platform affordance, audience predisposition, and surrounding social cues. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] closes the loop at the policy level, mapping deepfake-enabled financial fraud as an ecosystem problem (platforms, telecoms, banks) and arguing, in the same spirit as the Giglietto gambling study, that liability should fall on gatekeepers and amplification infrastructure rather than on individual victims.

## Coordination at scale: swarms, agents, and information operations

A third strand moves from single synthetic artifacts to *coordinated, agentic* manipulation. [[Orlando2025-ul]] simulates networked LLM agents running information operations and finds that mere mutual awareness among agents — without explicit top-down direction — produces coordination nearly as dense as deliberate collective decision-making, a striking demonstration that "emergent" coordinated inauthentic behavior may require little engineering. [[Schroeder2026-im]] generalizes this into a policy forum on "malicious AI swarms," arguing that fusing LLM reasoning with multi-agent architectures constitutes a qualitative leap beyond earlier botnets (which, per IRA precedent, had limited measurable effect), enabling persistent, adaptive, cross-platform manipulation that threatens democratic deliberation through manufactured consensus, training-data poisoning ("LLM grooming"), and institutional-trust erosion. Both papers converge on a key move: symmetric defenses (detection, pro-social counter-swarms) are structurally insufficient, so governance must target commercial incentives and provenance infrastructure rather than content moderation alone — a conclusion that echoes Schiffrin's gatekeeper-liability argument from the fraud literature.

## Capture, bias, and ideological inflection

A fourth line interrogates how political power and ideology become embedded *in* generative systems themselves, rather than merely weaponized through them. [[Waight2026-ts]] demonstrates empirically that state-coordinated media (notably Chinese) is memorized by commercial LLMs and measurably shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence when prompted in languages tied to low-media-freedom states — training-data capture as a novel, largely unintentional channel of institutional influence. [[Beacken2026-zb]] complements this geopolitically, arguing against deterministic narratives of GenAI propaganda by showing, across six democratically weakened states, that adoption patterns are structurally contingent rather than uniform. [[Crichton2026-kn]] and [[Topinka2026-hb]] push the capture argument into aesthetics and affect: Crichton's "re-generation" experiment shows commercial image models sanitize explicit far-right markers while preserving or displacing underlying ideological bias — a *structural*, not accidental, synergy between far-right visual culture and generative architectures. Topinka theorizes this as "slopaganda": AI political content that works not by deceiving viewers (the deepfake logic) but by affectively attuning to ambient platform moods via alignment and RLHF, intensifying existing ideological formations rather than disrupting them. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] provides a case study of this convergence in far-right populist campaigning on Instagram and X during the 2024 European elections. Across this cluster, the throughline is that bias and manipulation are not add-ons to generative models but emerge from training data, alignment procedures, and platform incentive structures — a structural claim that reframes "disinformation" as inseparable from the political economy of model-building itself.

## Epistemics, trust, and the crisis of verification

A fifth set of papers steps back to ask what generative AI does to the *concept* of truth and trust. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues that conventional fact-checking epistemologies (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot capture AI outputs, proposing "emergent facts" as a new category requiring relational, indicator-based evaluation. [[Emilio2026-ik]] scales this into the "Generative AI Paradox": the danger is not isolated deepfakes but coherent "synthetic realities" across content, identity, interaction, and institutional layers, producing a rational, society-wide discounting of digital evidence — an epistemic tax that benefits strategic deniers. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a complementary theoretical scaffold, proposing a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework for trust in generative AI drawn from mass- and social-media trust research. [[Mattis2026-gu]] grounds these abstractions empirically, showing that task-specific AI disclosures in journalism *uniformly* reduce perceived trustworthiness, with fact-checking disclosures penalized most — direct evidence that transparency, while normatively demanded, carries real reputational costs, echoing Rauchfleisch's finding that disclosure changes persuasive and evaluative dynamics. [[Baym2026-tr]] bridges this epistemic literature back to political economy, arguing that generative AI's data-extraction and authenticity-crisis effects are simply the latest intensification of problems (concentrated wealth, opaque algorithms, precarious labor) that critical platform scholarship identified a decade ago — a call to move from diagnosis to intervention that implicitly frames this entire topic's urgency.

## Theorizing the medium and its cultural residues

Finally, several papers pull back to ask what kind of *medium* and *cultural formation* generative AI constitutes, providing the conceptual vocabulary this topic's teaching dimension draws on. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues that generative AI is one of three forces (alongside algorithmic recommendation and platform privatization) dissolving the "social media" paradigm itself, enabling "platforms without users" and AI-mediated communication as a genuinely new post-social formation. [[Manovich2026-ih]] offers a deliberately non-ethical, media-theoretical account of generative AI as "a medium that thinks," with built-in cognitive capacity and structural conservatism — a framework useful for understanding *why* AI-generated persuasive and propagandistic imagery (as documented by Giglietto, Crichton, and Topinka) gravitates toward familiar, idealized, affectively legible aesthetics rather than defamiliarizing ones. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Tang2026-gu]] examine the cultural residue of this production at scale — "slop" and "brainrot" as both aesthetic genre and affective mode in Western contexts, and as an industrialized, engineered creative-labor practice among Chinese viral-video producers — showing that the same generative infrastructure fueling disinformation and propaganda elsewhere in this topic also underwrites a vast, formally adjacent economy of low-stakes synthetic content whose line between "slop" and "slopaganda" is often blurred only by intent and context.

Read as a whole, the structure note traces an arc from measuring generative AI's persuasive and visual-manipulative capacities, through its agentic and coordinated amplification, to its structural capture by state and ideological actors, and finally to the epistemic and medium-theoretic vocabulary needed to make sense of — and teach — a media environment in which synthetic content is no longer exceptional but ambient.
