# Generative AI & Synthetic Media

## The arc: from platforms to an "AI society"

The anchor text for this cluster is [[Tornberg2026-im]], which names the shift this whole topic is tracking: a tendential move from the *platform society* to an *AI society*, organized around cognitive lock-in, generative (rather than predictive) epistemics, and "alignment politics" as the new terrain of struggle over visibility and voice. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] supplies the companion diagnosis at the level of media form, arguing that "social media" as a unifying paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private enclaves, and AI-mediated communication — with chatbot use now rivaling or exceeding active posting. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits "platformization" itself a decade on, tracing the same handover: the retirement of Facebook's Like button and the turn to satellite-hosted AI compute mark Big Tech's reorientation from open-web infrastructure toward an integrated "Big AI." [[Baym2026-tr]] closes the loop affectively rather than conceptually, reprising her own 2015 manifesto to show that the harms she named — wealth concentration, opaque algorithms, precarious labor, data extraction — have not been superseded but *absorbed* into the generative-AI moment, with GenAI now feeding on the very user-generated corpus that built the platform era. Together these four pieces frame the topic's central wager: generative AI is not an add-on to platform capitalism but its next structural phase.

## Epistemic consequences: truth, trust, and facts under generative conditions

A second cluster asks what happens to factuality and trust once content is synthetic by default. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] proposes "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category — plausible, context-dependent, and opaque — needed because evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based notions of fact all fail against probabilistic generation. [[Emilio2026-ik]] scales this up into the idea of "synthetic realities," a four-layer stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) whose endpoint is a *Generative AI Paradox*: as fakes proliferate, societies may rationally discount all digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself. [[Suk2026-ai]] tries to domesticate this unease theoretically, proposing a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework for trust in generative AI that extends, rather than replaces, decades of mass-media and social-media trust research. Several empirical papers test pieces of this picture directly: [[Choi2026-bz]] finds a "modality-congruent carryover effect" in which difficulty authenticating one deepfake erodes confidence specifically within the same modality later on; [[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates assumptions about visual primacy, showing that AI-generated images are not uniformly more persuasive than text or decontextualized video — plausibility and issue salience matter more than synthetic origin per se; and [[Wack2026-bt]], working in Kenya, reframes deepfake impact as *socially negotiated* through surrounding audience commentary rather than fixed at the point of production. Journalism-specific audience studies — [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] — narrow the lens further, showing that AI disclosure in news does not change perceived quality but does depress trust and short-circuit engagement in task-dependent, politically moderated ways. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] supplies the media-framing backdrop to all of this, showing how news coverage itself anthropomorphizes LLMs unevenly, shaping the public epistemology these other papers presuppose.

## Deception, propaganda, and the sanitized extremist image

A third strand turns to generative AI as a tool of organized persuasion and political manipulation — the empirical heart of the topic's "deceptive visual content" brief. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] offers the paradigm case of AI-generated gambling imagery: a typology of persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, cultural localization) that generative AI does not invent but *intensifies and recombines*, riding an exponential post-ChatGPT surge through regulatory gaps between paid and organic content. [[Crichton2026-kn]] performs the mirror experiment for far-right visual culture, showing that commercial image models "sanitize" extremist markers without erasing ideological content — displacing rather than neutralizing hate, a structural rather than accidental affinity between far-right aesthetics and generative architectures. [[Topinka2026-hb]] theorizes the affective register underlying both cases via "slopaganda": content that is obviously fake yet affectively resonant, where models act as "ideological attunement machines" optimized to feel out ambient platform moods rather than to deceive. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] situates this within electoral far-right campaigning on Instagram/X during the 2024 European elections, while [[Beacken2026-zb]] insists on comparative specificity, showing that GenAI propaganda adoption across six democratically weakened states is structurally uneven rather than technologically inevitable. At the systemic end, [[Schroeder2026-im]] and [[Orlando2025-ul]] move from content to coordination: the former theorizes "malicious AI swarms" of agentic LLMs manufacturing consensus, the latter empirically demonstrates, via generative agent-based modeling, that mere mutual awareness among LLM agents is enough to produce real-world-like coordination signatures. [[Lin2025-xp]] complicates the alarmism from the persuasion side, finding that AI political dialogue moves voters more than conventional ads but does so mainly through facts and evidence rather than manipulation — tempered by the finding that right-leaning-advocating models are systematically less accurate. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extends the deception frame to financial harm, arguing that deepfake fraud is best regulated at the level of platform/telecom/bank gatekeepers rather than individual victims. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a case study in generative knowledge capture: Grokipedia as a Wikipedia-derivative encyclopedia skewed toward unreliable and blacklisted sourcing on controversial topics — propaganda by infrastructural rewrite rather than discrete fake image.

## Aesthetics, labor, and the cultural status of "slop"

A final grouping steps back from harm and persuasion to ask what kind of cultural object generative media *is*. [[Manovich2026-ih]] brackets ethics entirely to treat generative AI as a medium in its own right, identifying built-in "media cognition," style–content entanglement, and a structurally conservative, anti-avant-garde bias toward familiar academic-photographic aesthetics. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Topinka2026-hb]] converge on the cultural-theoretical register of "slop," with Galip drawing on Ngai's gimmick theory to read AI slop/brainrot as simultaneously working "too little" and "too hard" — a symptom of "too late capitalism" with ambivalent critical potential. [[Tang2026-gu]] grounds this in labor: Chinese AI-slop creators who engineer virality systematically via matrix accounts and format-testing, reframing authorship and creativity as managed production rather than spontaneous craft. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] shows a related dynamic on TikTok's immigration discourse, where generative AI appears as a minority but notable tool for "speculative worldbuilding" within an otherwise pro-immigration-skewed platform vernacular — a reminder that the aesthetic and propagandistic strands of this topic are not cleanly separable, but two faces of the same emerging AI-mediated public culture that [[Tornberg2026-im]] and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] name at the outset.
