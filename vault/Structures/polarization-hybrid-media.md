# Polarization & Hybrid Media Systems

## Two literatures, one problem

This cluster sits at the seam where research on *traditional media* — talk shows, newspapers, cable — meets research on *platforms* and their algorithmic, networked publics. The earliest anchor here, [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], already refuses the dichotomy: Twitter during the 2013 Italian elections operated as a "second screen" whose peaks and content mostly tracked broadcast logic rather than displacing it. A decade later, [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Knupfer2025-vt]] describe the same interpenetration from the other end — a niche cable channel (Sky News Australia) inserting itself into transnational YouTube/Facebook propaganda flows, and US Congressional factions co-producing agendas with hyper-partisan digital surrogates. The connective-action lineage running from Chadwick's hybrid media systems through [[Bennett2025-xs]] and [[F2020-6278a4aa]] provides the shared vocabulary: political communication is now organized *across* legacy and networked infrastructures, and polarization is a joint product of both.

## How the extremes get amplified

Several strands converge on the mechanics of amplification. On the *supply* side, [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate mainstream visibility for the Radical Right across five European countries in 2024, and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] shows UK newspapers sorted COVID scientists along political lines. On the *platform* side, [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds X's algorithm causally shifts users rightward — partly by inducing durable follows of conservative activists — while [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment shows engagement-based ranking systematically amplifies intergroup-moralized-emotional content and warps users' *perceived norms* of political dialogue. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] complicates the picture by documenting that Facebook actively dampens partisan reach and rewards journalistic quality, with sharp swings around the 2020 "break the glass" moment — platforms as active, time-varying curators, not neutral pipes. [[Brown2026-br]] adds a needed caveat: YouTube shows content rabbit holes but no evidence of algorithm-driven radicalization pathways, and the ideological echo-chamber effect is largely user-driven.

Affective dynamics recur as a distinct amplification channel. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] show that anger reactions predict sharing, especially for populist actors, while [[Elfes2026-jb]] extends "polarization" beyond opinion or interaction to *narrative* — divergent role-assignments to actors — and shows videos can be far more polarized than their comment sections.

## Selective exposure, echo chambers, and their discontents

The echo-chamber question has moved from asserting the phenomenon to interrogating how measurement shapes findings. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] established that individual choice suppresses cross-cutting exposure more than the algorithm does; [[Hartmann2025-px]] argues that much of the field's disagreement is definitional and operational rather than substantive. [[Green2025-ap]] presses this further methodologically: source-level partisanship scores mask enormous *story-level* heterogeneity, so "curation bubbles" form across sources rather than within them. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] echo the call to move beyond outlet-level, single-platform designs, and [[Dehghan2026-sy]] shows that on Reddit, cross-ideological overlap is minimal even between discursively similar communities — polarization as a *sedimented* precondition of platform activity. Alternative-media audience networks, meanwhile, look very different across national contexts: [[Zhu2026-tn]] finds their structural position depends on media-system type and the institutional access of populist parties, with Sweden's isolated enclave being the exception rather than the rule. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] frames the same question as one of "platform polarization."

## Alternative media, disinformation, and the epistemic contest

A related thread reframes polarization as a struggle over epistemic authority. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows "fake news" is now a rhetorical weapon deployed symmetrically by hyperpartisan outlets on left and right to reclassify opponents as deviant in Hallin's sense. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] traces the same contest into elite communication: Brazilian parliamentarians during the Manaus crisis pursued epistemic authority through derivative reporting, direct claims to state authority, or brokerage narratives — bypassing or mimicking journalism. [[Adam2026-tz]] gives this a mechanism, showing conspiracy belief emerges from a "marriage" of alternative-media contagion, mainstream-media mitigation, and populist predispositions that drive selective engagement and backfire. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] adds that repeated cross-outlet exposure to disinformation *narratives* — not discrete claims — is what shifts belief, dovetailing with [[Elfes2026-jb]]'s narrative-polarization framework. [[Rothut2026-or]] shows how the Querdenken protests functioned as a meso-level conduit routing sympathetic publics toward far-right and QAnon networks through anti-elite framing rather than explicit ideology.

## Institutions, governance, and the limits of intervention

The most recent papers foreground governance. [[Bennett2025-xs]] and [[Knupfer2025-vt]] argue that digital surrogate organizations have eroded party gatekeeping, especially on the right, producing "organizationally enabled connective action" toward illiberalism. [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s analysis of Brazil's X ban demonstrates that even well-intentioned platform regulation can produce a durable partisan "sorting ratchet." [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows that bridging-based moderation like Community Notes is designed in a way that systematically *undermoderates* the most polarizing content — precisely where moderation is needed. And [[Allen2025-ot]] highlights the closing methodological horizon: as platform access shrinks, platform-independent experimental tools become essential for adjudicating between studies whose divergent findings (e.g., FIES versus Piccardi et al.) may reflect intervention level, platform, or moderation regime more than substantive disagreement.

## The normative undertow

Running beneath the empirical work is an unresolved normative question. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s systematic review finds that scholars' depolarization *recommendations* are typically ungrounded in their own findings and lean instead on implicit deliberative-democratic ideals. [[Esau2025-tf]] tries to make this normative infrastructure explicit through "deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening." Between them they mark the field's most honest problem: after a decade of increasingly sophisticated evidence that legacy and platform media *jointly* produce polarization — through visibility asymmetries, algorithmic amplification, affective affordances, narrative contests, and institutional capture — we still lack a clear account, empirically or normatively, of what depolarization would even look like. [[Sarmiento2025-as]]'s call for unsupervised framing analysis suggests one path forward: taking the emergent, rhetorical texture of polarized discourse seriously before prescribing its cure.
