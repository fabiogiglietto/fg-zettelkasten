# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## The Shape of the Field: Definitions and Diagnostic Tools

Any synthesis of this literature has to begin with [[Rothut2026-wt]], the scoping review that maps 25 years of online-radicalization scholarship and offers the conceptual scaffolding the rest of these papers implicitly or explicitly work within. Its central move—insisting that "online" and "offline" radicalization can no longer be treated as separate domains, and that the internet functions as a catalyst rather than a root cause—reframes the entire topic away from platform-deterministic narratives. Its multi-level heuristic (macro/meso/micro, mapped onto Lasswell's communicator-content-medium-recipient schema) provides a useful lattice for organizing the empirical studies below, most of which cluster at the macro (discursive/algorithmic) and meso (group/network) levels, with comparatively few reaching the micro/recipient level the review flags as understudied.

[[Brown2026-br]] takes up the review's call for causal rigor directly, using an audit design to disentangle algorithmic supply-side effects from user-driven demand-side effects on YouTube. Its findings—no evidence for algorithmic "radicalization pathways," only mild and user-driven echo chambers, but strong content "rabbit holes" and a platform-wide conservative tilt in recommendations—complicate the popular narrative (echoed loosely in journalistic accounts) that recommender systems autonomously radicalize users. This paper functions as a methodological corrective within the topic, cautioning against over-attributing agency to algorithms without properly isolating user choice, a concern that resonates with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s complaint about the scarcity of longitudinal, causal work.

## Platform Affordances as Differentiators

A second cluster treats platform affordances not as background but as the object of analysis. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] offers the most systematic comparative treatment, tracing German far-right networks across five platforms and showing that identical actors produce structurally distinct networks depending on each platform's affordances for connectivity, replicability, and scalability—Twitter as sprawling broadcast arena, Telegram as inward-facing fringe ecosystem, Instagram as fragmented but reciprocal echo chambers, Facebook as hierarchically organized around party structure. This affordance-relational framing directly informs [[Karo2026-dn]]'s account of jihadist "everyday extremism" on TikTok, where audio manipulation, meme infiltration, emoji codes, and bait-and-switch sequencing exploit the platform's specific vernacular and moderation architecture; strikingly, Karo notes these evasion tactics are not ideology-specific, echoing Kakavand's claim that affordances—not ideological content—drive divergent circulation patterns. [[Bosch2024-hj]] extends this affordance-centered logic to TikTok's sonic dimension, arguing that sound itself is an underexamined vector of computational propaganda in coverage of the Ukraine invasion, again locating extremist/propagandistic effect in platform-specific technical features rather than textual content alone.

[[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] push this affordance analysis toward the register of affect and ambiance. Nangle's "affective-discursive ecology" of far-right Instagram Reels shows normalization proceeding through humor, positive affect, and memetic softening rather than hostility—an implicit rebuke to content-moderation frameworks premised on detecting hateful text. Rieder's study of the post-deplatforming "Tate-space" on YouTube makes a structurally similar argument at greater empirical scale: Tate's ideology persists and even intensifies in visibility through a decentralized ecology of interviews, remixes, and memeified formats that outpace YouTube's takedown capacity. Both papers converge on a claim that also threads through [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Kakavand2026-kt]]: moderation regimes built around discrete, identifiable violative content are structurally mismatched to how extremist meaning circulates—ambiently, ironically, and infrastructurally, rather than as isolated hateful utterances.

## Networks, Hybridization, and Mainstreaming

A third strand examines how far-right actors build durable cross-actor infrastructures that blur boundaries between extremism, mainstream media, and commercial influencer culture. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents this hybridization in the Swedish "alternative influence network," where guest appearances, hyperlinking, and in-video mentions fuse political propaganda with influencer-style monetization and self-branding, with YouTube operating as a sanitized gateway funneling audiences toward more extreme alt-tech spaces. [[Groebner2026-pc]] scales this logic transnationally, showing sustained commenter overlap across four English-language hyperpartisan YouTube channels (Fox News, Sky News Australia, Rebel News, GB News), with US-centered content anchoring a genuinely transnational far-right audience space organized around shared enemies like transphobia and anti-immigration framing. Both studies extend [[Kakavand2026-kt]]'s network-analytic method but shift the unit of analysis from single-country ecosystems to commercial-political hybrids and transnational audience flows, respectively.

[[Rothut2026-or]] supplies the connective theoretical concept binding this cluster together: "protest-facilitated mainstreaming." Analyzing Querdenken's Telegram discourse, it shows how a nominally heterogeneous, grassroots protest movement became structurally embedded within—though not initiated by—German and international far-right and conspiracist networks, with a small set of prominent far-right brokers occupying the network's actual center of gravity. This paper's distinction between content mechanisms (anti-elite, conspiratorial framing that is repeatable and shareable) and connective mechanisms (infrastructural routing toward extreme actors) offers a useful vocabulary for what Askanius and Groebner document empirically: mainstreaming as a gradual, boundary-shifting, infrastructurally mediated process rather than sudden ideological conversion.

[[De_Leon2025-qn]] complements this network-level work with a conceptual intervention at the level of content circulation itself, arguing that exposure research has focused too narrowly on directly extremist material and neglected how "ordinary" content—news stories, Wikipedia entries—gets weaponized once it enters ideologically charged networks. This concept of "networked exposure" dovetails with Rothut's connective mechanisms and helps explain how mainstreaming can occur even without a rise in explicitly radical content, since the meaning of circulated material shifts with its network context rather than its manifest textual content.

## From Discourse to Offline Action, and the Question of Belief Formation

Two papers push past circulation and network structure toward the harder question of effects—on behavior and on belief. [[Bailard2024-pj]] provides the volume's clearest causal-longitudinal contribution, using Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data to show that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) framing—but not explicit calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, with a reciprocal online-offline mobilization cycle. This finding directly answers [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s call for causal, longitudinal work and models one way to move "beyond a content-moderation lens" toward discursive-ecosystem analysis.

[[Marwick2025-vx]] addresses the individual-level, recipient side of the process that the scoping review identifies as understudied, using qualitative analysis of "redpilling" narratives to argue against the popular "exposure equals belief" model. Instead, belief adoption is typically a processual socialization in which disinformation is treated as "evidence" marshaled by self-styled rational actors, complicating any simple pipeline model of algorithmic exposure leading directly to radicalization—a conclusion that resonates with [[Brown2026-br]]'s algorithmic-audit finding that recommendation systems alone do not manufacture radicalization pathways, and reinforces [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s insistence on multi-factor, incremental process models over single-cause or single-exposure accounts.

Together, these thirteen papers trace an arc from definitional and methodological stock-taking, through affordance-specific and affect-laden analyses of how extremist content is produced and evades moderation, to network-level accounts of hybridization and transnational mainstreaming, and finally to the harder causal and psychological questions of how discourse translates into offline violence and how individuals actually come to internalize radical belief—consistently pushing the field away from content-moderation-centric, single-exposure models and toward socio-technical, ecological, and processual understandings of radicalization.
