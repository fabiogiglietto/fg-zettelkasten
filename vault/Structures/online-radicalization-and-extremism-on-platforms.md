# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## The Shape of the Field: Mapping Online Radicalization Research

Any synthesis of this literature must begin with [[Rothut2026-wt]], the scoping review that surveys 25 years and 382 publications on internet-mediated radicalization. Its central methodological intervention—organizing mechanisms along macro/meso/micro levels crossed with Lasswell's communicator-content-medium-recipient schema—provides a scaffold for situating the other eighteen studies filed here, nearly all of which can be read as deep, empirical probes into cells of that larger matrix. Its most consequential theoretical claim, however, is corrosive to older paradigms: the online/offline binary is "no longer tenable." Every empirical paper below, in its own way, confirms this by tracing how digital discourse translates into material consequence or vice versa.

## From Online Speech to Offline Violence

[[Bailard2024-pj]] offers the most direct test of the online-offline nexus, using Granger causality on half a million Proud Boys Telegram messages to show that *diagnostic* (grievance) and *motivational* (solidarity) framing—not explicit calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, with a reciprocal four-week cycle linking protest, online affect, and renewed violence. This finding productively complicates [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s call to move beyond single-cause models: it is not incitement content per se but affective ecosystem dynamics that mobilize. A structurally similar logic operates in Rothut2026-yo—wait, more precisely in [[Rothut2026-or]]'s study of Querdenken, which shows how a nominally non-extremist protest movement became a conduit for mainstreaming radical ideas not through ideological conversion but through shared anti-elite framing and network embedding alongside German and international far-right nodes. Both papers reframe "radicalization" as emergent from movement-level communicative dynamics rather than individual exposure—a meso-level argument that dovetails with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s observation that meso-level mechanisms (echo chambers, co-radicalization, emotional convergence) remain understudied relative to macro and micro work.

## Platform Affordances as Vectors of Evasion and Amplification

A second cluster interrogates how specific platform architectures are exploited. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist supporters on TikTok deploying audio camouflage, meme infiltration, emoji codes, and bait-and-switch tactics to evade moderation while embedding propaganda in "everyday" platform vernacular—explicitly noting these adversarial strategies are ideology-agnostic, echoing far-right and anti-vaccine evasion patterns. This resonates with [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s finding that TikTok's sonic affordances constitute an under-examined disinformation vector in Ukraine-war content, and with [[Cabbuag2024-me]]'s account of how TikTok's humor-oriented affordances normalize "legal-but-harmful" incivility among Filipino Gen Z influencers. Together these three TikTok studies suggest the platform's logics—algorithmic amplification, sound, meme culture, humor—create distinctive conditions for normalization that differ from the more text- or network-centric dynamics documented on Twitter/Telegram.

[[Kakavand2026-kt]] formalizes this comparative insight theoretically, showing across five platforms (Facebook, Twitter/X, Instagram, YouTube, Telegram) that identical German far-right actors produce structurally distinct networks depending on platform affordances—Twitter's broadcast scalability, Telegram's inward clustering, Instagram's fragmented echo chambers, Facebook's hierarchical party control. This affordance-relational framework substantiates [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s critique that single-platform studies (still dominant in the field, per its review) miss the ecosystem-level picture, and it directly complements [[Askanius2026-de]]'s account of the Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube, where guest appearances, hyperlinking, and in-video mentions construct a cohesive, commercially hybridized ecosystem that funnels audiences toward alt-tech platforms when moderated. [[Groebner2026-pc]] extends this transnationally, showing sustained commenter overlap across four English-language far-right hyperpartisan YouTube channels (Sky News Australia, Fox News, Rebel News, GB News), with US content occupying a gravitational center—evidence that platform affordances (recommendation, cross-linking) scaffold not just domestic networks but a transnational far-right information space.

## Affective and Aesthetic Softening

A distinct thread examines *how* content normalizes extremism affectively rather than through explicit ideological assertion. [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s concept of an "affective-discursive ecology" on Instagram—where humor, positive affect, and memetic form soften far-right messaging—finds its mirror in [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s notion of "ambient ideology" surrounding Andrew Tate on YouTube: a decentralized ecosystem of interviews, remixes, and memes that sustains influence without explicit endorsement, structurally outpacing moderation because recommendation algorithms amplify content faster than removal teams can act. [[Crichton2026-kn]] pushes this logic into the generative-AI era, arguing that commercial AI image models don't erase hateful content so much as sanitize its explicit markers while preserving ideological legibility for in-group audiences—a structural rather than incidental bias. Read together, [[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Rieder2026-pp]], and [[Crichton2026-kn]] suggest an emergent consensus: contemporary far-right normalization increasingly operates through aesthetic and affective softening rather than overt hostility, rendering content-moderation frameworks built around detecting explicit hate speech progressively obsolete.

## Disinformation, "Evidence," and the Rationality of Radicalization

[[Marwick2025-vx]] intervenes directly against the "exposure equals belief" model that [[Rothut2026-wt]] identifies as empirically unsupported, showing through redpilling narratives on Reddit, Gab, and Discord that radicalization is typically a gradual socialization process in which disinformation functions as "evidence" marshaled to construct a self-image of rational, scientific superiority. This qualitative account of belief formation complements [[Crichton2026-kn]] and [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] at the level of content production: the latter examines how far-right campaigns in the 2024 European elections deployed AI-generated multimedia on Instagram and X, suggesting that the "evidentiary" aesthetic Marwick describes is increasingly manufactured rather than found. [[De_Leon2025-qn]]'s concept of "networked exposure" provides a conceptual bridge here, arguing that even ostensibly neutral content (news articles, Wikipedia pages) becomes ideologically weaponized through circulation within extremist networks—extending Marwick's insight about "evidence" beyond disinformation per se to any content recontextualized by network position.

## Network Structure, Mainstreaming, and Platform Governance

The final arc concerns how fringe content crosses into mainstream politics and how platforms respond. [[Klinger2023-qu]] offers perhaps the most granular account of this laundering process, tracing how the Identitarian Movement's anti-migration campaign was adopted by the AfD via intermediary "superspreader" networks that obscured its extremist origin—a mechanism of "source-laundering" that operationalizes the mainstreaming dynamic [[Rothut2026-or]] describes more diffusely for Querdenken. Governance responses are tested empirically in [[Simeone2025-vo]], which shows that targeted deplatforming of a handful of central accounts produced network-wide collapse of the Arizona Election Review community's authority structure—evidence for deplatforming's efficacy that stands in tension with [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s finding that YouTube's moderation is structurally outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation, and with [[Askanius2026-de]]'s observation that moderated actors simply migrate to alt-tech platforms. This tension—deplatforming works at the network level but fails against ambient, diffuse ecosystems—is a central unresolved question across the corpus.

Two papers extend these governance questions to authoritarian and quasi-authoritarian contexts: [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] shows Twitter functioning as dual-use infrastructure for both protest coordination and state-aligned repression during China's COVID-19 protests, while [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents how Serbia's ruling party uses decentralized, mimetic coordinated inauthentic behavior that evades the bot-farm detection models built for more centralized manipulation. Both complicate the far-right/jihadist focus of most papers here, suggesting that the platform-exploitation playbook documented for ideological extremists generalizes to state and quasi-state actors engaged in information control.

## Synthesis

Across these nineteen papers, a coherent arc emerges: early concern with direct, explicit extremist content and single-platform dynamics ([[Karo2026-dn]], [[Bosch2024-hj]]) gives way to more structural accounts of ambient, affective, and networked normalization ([[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Rieder2026-pp]], [[De_Leon2025-qn]], [[Crichton2026-kn]]) that elude both detection and conventional moderation. Running throughout is the affordance-sensitive, multi-platform imperative crystallized by [[Kakavand2026-kt]] and the meso-level, process-oriented understanding of radicalization championed by [[Rothut2026-wt]], [[Marwick2025-vx]], and [[Bailard2024-pj]]. The collective trajectory suggests the field is moving—consistent with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s own diagnosis—away from content-centric, causal-exposure models and toward ecological, infrastructural, and temporal analyses of how platforms, algorithms, and affect jointly manufacture the conditions for extremism's circulation and legitimation.
