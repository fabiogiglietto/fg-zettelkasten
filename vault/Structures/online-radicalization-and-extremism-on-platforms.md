# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping a fragmented field

[[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of a quarter-century of scholarship supplies the vocabulary the rest of this cluster implicitly argues over: is the internet a root cause of radicalization, a mere catalyst, or a distinct socio-technical environment that has made the online/offline distinction untenable? The review's call to analyze mechanisms across macro (discursive), meso (group), and micro (individual) levels, and its warning that single-exposure models of radicalization are empirically thin, sets an agenda that several of the papers filed here pursue directly—some by supplying the longitudinal, mixed-method evidence the review finds scarce, others by pushing back against still-common "algorithm-as-culprit" narratives.

## Platform affordances as exploitable infrastructure

A cluster of studies treats specific platform architectures—not extremist content in the abstract—as the object of analysis. [[Karo2026-dn]] catalogs five concrete evasion tactics jihadist supporters use against TikTok's moderation (audio pitch-shifting, meme infiltration, blurred imagery, emoji codes, bait-and-switch openings), arguing that these tactics are platform-structural rather than ideology-specific and thus demand infrastructural rather than purely content-based governance. [[Bosch2024-hj]] extends this logic to TikTok's sonic affordances during the invasion of Ukraine, showing sound itself functions as an under-examined vector of computational propaganda. [[Nangle2026-yo]] locates a parallel dynamic on Instagram, where positive affect, humour, and memetic Reels—rather than overt hostility—constitute an "affective-discursive ecology" that algorithmic proximity helps circulate. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents how Swedish far-right YouTubers fuse influencer microcelebrity practices (branding, sponsorships, coded workarounds like "phantom videos") with political propaganda, using YouTube as a sanitized gateway that funnels audiences toward alt-tech platforms after moderation pressure. [[Rieder2026-pp]] pushes this furthest, showing that even after Andrew Tate's official channels were removed, a decentralized "Tate-space" of interviews, remixes, and native formats kept his ambient ideology circulating—evidence that recommendation-driven amplification structurally outpaces takedown-based moderation. Together these studies converge on a claim [[Karo2026-dn]] states explicitly: adversarial creativity is a feature of platform vernacular culture generally, observable across jihadist, far-right, and even anti-vaccine communities alike.

## Contesting the algorithm

Yet not every paper in this filing accepts that algorithms are the primary engine of radicalization. [[Brown2026-br]]'s audit study directly interrogates the "rabbit hole" thesis implicit in [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Askanius2026-de]], formally distinguishing echo chambers, content rabbit holes, and radicalization pathways. It finds strong evidence only for the second—recommendations are heavily path-dependent on the currently watched video—while ideological echo chambers turn out to be driven mainly by user choice, and no evidence emerges for an escalating radicalization pathway; if anything, YouTube's algorithm nudges all users toward moderately conservative content regardless of partisanship. This complicates the more qualitative, ecosystem-level claims elsewhere in the cluster: the "Tate-space" and Swedish AIN findings describe how circulation persists and diversifies, but [[Brown2026-br]] suggests the underlying algorithmic mechanism may be blunter and less ideologically directional than ethnographic accounts imply, reinforcing [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s point that causal, experimentally grounded evidence remains rare and often at odds with journalistic and qualitative narratives.

## Mainstreaming: from fringe to familiar

A second thread examines how radical content becomes normalized rather than merely amplified. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" via the German Querdenken movement, showing how anti-elite and conspiratorial framing—not explicit ideology—served as the discursive bridge connecting heterogeneous pandemic protesters to entrenched far-right and QAnon-adjacent Telegram networks, with a prominent far-right cluster (not Querdenken itself) occupying the network's brokerage position. [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s "affective-discursive ecology" describes an analogous softening process on Instagram, where humour substitutes for hostility. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] localizes this dynamic culturally, showing how Filipino Gen Z "dogshow" humor on TikTok normalizes legal-but-harmful incivility that blurs into cyberbullying and hate speech, extending the mainstreaming literature beyond the Western, English-language cases that dominate [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s review. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates the mainstreaming picture from the creator side: ConspiracyTok is populated disproportionately by young, non-White, and female creators who construct "populist knowledge production" through visual evidence and personal narrative, a "generous epistemology" that can carry both marginalized standpoint claims and disinformation—troubling any simple villain/normalizer binary.

## Identity, epistemology, and the interior life of radicalization

Where the previous cluster asks how platforms make extreme content encounterable, [[Marwick2025-vx]] and [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] ask how individuals come to inhabit extremist identities once exposed. Marwick and Furl's analysis of "redpilling" narratives on Reddit, Gab, and Discord directly undercuts the "exposure equals belief" model that [[Rothut2026-wt]] also flags as empirically weak: most self-narrated conversions are gradual socializations sustained by community participation and self-styled "evidence" (books, statistics, memes), with participants' self-image as rational skeptics doing the ideological work of legitimation. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] arrives at a structurally similar conclusion from a different angle, showing that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities across six platforms cohere less through shared ideological content than through symbolic boundary work—reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label, converting personal hardship into collective grievance, and uniting ideologically disparate factions (secular-libertarian and religious-Christian) against an imagined "mainstream other." Both papers argue, against techno-centric explanations, that relational identity work and affective solidarity—not platform mechanics per se—sustain radical communities, echoing [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s point that epistemic style and self-presentation are as consequential as content.

## Coordinated and state-linked manipulation

A distinct but adjacent strand addresses inauthentic, coordinated activity rather than organic radicalization. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] maps coordinated forwarding clusters among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus, showing how authoritarian regimes exploit platform affordances for narrative amplification. Bastos2025-ol approaches attribution from a different angle, using computer vision on the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium dataset to show that troll-farm accounts carry country-specific visual signatures in profile imagery—an underused detection dimension beyond text and network features. [[Kim2026-br]] offers the most methodologically ambitious contribution here, building an explainable classifier over two decades of Korean news comments to show that suspected state-linked trolls rely primarily on morally condemning rhetoric against domestic leaders across the political spectrum—rather than direct praise of foreign patrons—and that such condemning content is disproportionately amplified by engagement-based ranking, suggesting influence operations exploit polarization dynamics rather than promote a single faction.

## Moderation, deplatforming, and the limits of removal

Several papers converge on the question of what happens when platforms intervene. [[Simeone2025-vo]] finds that Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of Arizona election-audit accounts produced network-wide "ripple effects" well beyond the mechanical removal of banned nodes: the right-leaning cluster's hub/authority structure collapsed, mobilization language gave way to abstract grievance about censorship itself, and—crucially—these effects were not attributable to bot-driven coordination, suggesting real disengagement among human users. This offers a more optimistic reading of targeted network intervention than [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s account of YouTube moderation being structurally outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation, or [[Karo2026-dn]]'s demonstration that simple textual manipulation defeats TikTok's keyword filters. Read together, these three studies suggest that deplatforming's efficacy is contingent on network structure and moderation design: removing a small number of central, non-resilient hubs (as in the Twitter/AER case) can meaningfully disrupt mobilization, whereas platforms whose recommendation systems continuously regenerate exposure (YouTube, TikTok) resist containment through removal alone—reinforcing [[Karo2026-dn]]'s call to shift from content-level to infrastructural governance.

## From online discourse to offline action

Finally, two papers close the loop between online communicative dynamics and offline consequences. [[Bailard2024-pj]] uses Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data to show that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) collective-action framing—not explicit calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, with a roughly four-week reciprocal cycle linking nonviolent protest, online motivational messaging, and later violent events. This finding dovetails with [[Rothut2026-or]]'s account of protest as a meso-level conduit that channels broader publics toward extremist milieus: both studies insist that the unit of analysis must be the discursive ecosystem and its temporal dynamics, not discrete posts, and both implicitly answer [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s call for more longitudinal, causally-oriented work bridging online communication and offline mobilization.
