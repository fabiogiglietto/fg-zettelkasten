# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping the field

[[Rothut2026-wt]] provides the orienting scaffold for this entire collection: a scoping review of 25 years of online-radicalization scholarship that insists the internet functions as a catalyst rather than a root cause of radicalization, and that online/offline distinctions are no longer analytically tenable. Its call for multi-level analysis (macro/meso/micro) and attention to the Lasswell communication chain (communicator, content, medium, recipient) is effectively enacted by the other sixteen papers, which collectively populate every cell of that matrix—from platform-level affordance studies to individual-level narrative accounts of conversion.

## Platform affordances and algorithmic exploitation

A cluster of papers treats platform architecture itself as the primary explanatory variable. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] offers the most systematic comparative statement: tracing the same far-right actors across five German platforms, it shows that identical connective features (retweets, forwards, mentions) produce starkly different network topologies depending on each platform's relational affordances of connectivity, replicability, and scalability—Twitter as sprawling broadcast arena, Telegram as fringe-dominated echo chamber, Facebook as hierarchically organized party apparatus. This affordance logic is given ethnographic depth elsewhere. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents how jihadist supporters reverse-engineer TikTok's moderation and recommendation systems through a typology of evasion tactics (audio camouflage, meme infiltration, emoji codes, bait-and-switch), arguing that these are not ideology-specific but part of a broader "adversarial creative" repertoire shared across extremist milieus—a claim that resonates with [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s identification of *sound* as an under-theorized, platform-specific vector of computational propaganda on TikTok during the invasion of Ukraine. [[Nangle2026-yo]] extends this affordance-sensitivity to Instagram, showing how far-right actors exploit algorithmic proximity and memetic, humor-laden Reels to construct an "affective-discursive ecology" in which positive affect rather than overt hostility does the ideological work. Together these studies push back against content-centric models of extremism, insisting that *how* platforms route attention, enforce (or fail to enforce) moderation, and reward certain affective registers is as consequential as the ideological content itself.

## Networks, mainstreaming, and transnational circulation

A second cluster scales up from single actors to entire ecosystems, tracing how extremist and "merely" radical content becomes embedded in broader, often transnational networks. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to explain how the German Querdenken movement, though initially heterogeneous and grassroots, became structurally entangled with far-right and conspiracist Telegram communities via shared anti-elite framing rather than explicit ideology—a meso-level bridging mechanism that complements [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s call to attend to group-level co-radicalization. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents an analogous hybridization process among Swedish far-right YouTubers, who fuse influencer microcelebrity practices with political propaganda and use YouTube as a "sanitised" gateway funneling audiences toward alt-tech platforms when moderated. [[Groebner2026-pc]] pushes this outward into a transnational register, finding substantial commenter overlap among English-language hyperpartisan news channels (Fox News, Sky News Australia, Rebel News, GB News), with US-centric content occupying the structural center of a shared far-right information space. [[Rieder2026-pp]] closes this thread by showing what happens *after* an influencer (Andrew Tate) is formally removed: a decentralized "Tate-space" of interviews, remixes, and memeified content persists and even grows, constituting an "ambient ideology" that normalizes misogynist and far-right talking points without requiring explicit affiliation—demonstrating that mainstreaming and deplatforming are two faces of the same circulatory dynamic.

## From exposure to belief: narrative and micro-level mechanisms

Where the above papers emphasize structure and circulation, [[Marwick2025-vx]] and [[Bailard2024-pj]] interrogate the mechanisms linking online content to individual belief and offline action. Marwick and Furl's analysis of "redpilling" narratives directly challenges the "exposure equals belief" model that implicitly underwrites much platform-affordance research: most Far-Right adherents describe their conversion as a gradual, self-styled "rational" socialization process rather than sudden radicalization by a single piece of content, with disinformation functioning as cumulative "evidence" rather than a one-shot trigger. [[Bailard2024-pj]] complements this at the collective level, showing with the Proud Boys that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) framing on Telegram—not explicit calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, and that a reciprocal online-offline "moralizing" cycle operates on a roughly four-week lag. Both papers converge on [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s central methodological lesson: causal, longitudinal, and processual designs are needed to move past static content classification toward understanding radicalization as an unfolding socio-technical process.

## State and coordinated actors

A distinct strand examines orchestrated, often state-linked amplification rather than organic extremist communities. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] reconstructs the organizational life of Russia's Internet Research Agency through leaked CVs, recasting the "troll factory" not as a covert intelligence unit but as a conventional, Crimea-era media-labor-market actor whose staff moved fluidly between disinformation work and mainstream PR/journalism careers. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] complements this with a network-analytic account of coordinated pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus, showing how message-forwarding clusters mechanically amplify regime-aligned narratives. Both studies shift attention from grassroots extremist mobilization toward the infrastructural and organizational scaffolding of state-aligned computational propaganda—an important counterpoint to the bottom-up dynamics foregrounded elsewhere in this collection.

## Moderation, deplatforming, and their limits

Several papers directly interrogate the efficacy of platform governance responses. [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers the most optimistic empirical case for targeted intervention: Twitter's 2021 suspension of a handful of central Arizona "election audit" accounts produced network-wide ripple effects—collapsing hub/authority structure and shifting discourse from mobilization to abstract grievance—that could not be explained by node removal alone, suggesting deplatforming can function as an effective "network intervention" when it strikes central nodes. [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Karo2026-dn]] offer the pessimistic counterpoint: on YouTube and TikTok respectively, recommendation-driven circulation structurally outpaces moderation, and removal of explicit violators does little to curb the diffuse, vernacular, "ambient" persistence of extremist and misogynist content. Read together, these three papers suggest that moderation efficacy may hinge less on volume of removals than on topological precision—targeting structurally central nodes rather than chasing content at the margins.

## The generative AI frontier

The final cluster extends this topic into the newest technological terrain. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] documents far-right parties' adoption of AI-generated multimedia in 2024 European Parliament campaigning on Instagram and X, while [[Beacken2026-zb]] cautions against technologically deterministic readings of this trend, showing comparatively across six democratically weakened states that GenAI propaganda adoption is unevenly shaped by local political, economic, and infrastructural conditions rather than following a uniform trajectory. [[Crichton2026-kn]] provides the most theoretically pointed contribution, arguing through a "re-generative" media experiment that commercial image models do not neutralize far-right visual propaganda but *sanitize* its explicit markers while structurally preserving ideological content for in-group legibility—a finding that reframes AI "safety" interventions as potentially complicit in normalization rather than prevention. Together, these three papers suggest that generative AI does not introduce a wholly new mode of radicalization so much as it intensifies and reconfigures the affordance-exploitation, mainstreaming, and moderation-evasion dynamics already identified across platforms in this collection—confirming [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s thesis that the internet's role is best understood as an accelerant whose specific technological form keeps changing while its underlying socio-communicative logic persists.
