# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## The Shape of the Field: From Exposure to Ecology

The most synthetic entry in this collection, [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review, offers the scaffolding on which the other nineteen studies can be arranged. It proposes that radicalization is incremental and multi-causal, that the internet acts as catalyst rather than cause, and that the online/offline binary is no longer analytically tenable. Crucially, it identifies an "empirical turn" since 2018 and a persistent evidentiary gap: longitudinal, causal work remains rare, with most scholarship relying on proxies—exposure, engagement, content patterns—rather than demonstrated effects on belief or behavior. This diagnosis usefully frames the rest of the corpus, which splits roughly into (a) studies that try to close the causal gap by linking online discourse to offline action, (b) studies mapping how platform affordances and algorithms structure extremist circulation, and (c) studies of self-narrated radicalization and the socio-technical infrastructures (state and commercial) that manufacture and amplify extremist content.

## From Online Discourse to Offline Violence

[[Bailard2024-pj]] is the collection's most direct attempt at establishing the causal arrow that [[Rothut2026-wt]] finds lacking elsewhere in the field. Using Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data, it shows that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) framing—not explicit calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, and documents a reciprocal four-week online-offline mobilization cycle. This finding reorients the field away from content moderation's focus on discrete hateful posts toward discursive ecosystems and their temporal dynamics—a move paralleled by [[Rothut2026-or]]'s concept of "protest-facilitated mainstreaming." That paper, tracing the German Querdenken movement on Telegram, shows how anti-elite framing (present in 80% of posts) acts as a bridge connecting heterogeneous, initially apolitical protest publics to entrenched far-right and conspiracist networks—demonstrating mainstreaming as a connective, infrastructural process rather than a matter of individual conversion. Together these two papers suggest that protest and grievance discourse function as pumps that move audiences and affect between moderate and extreme poles, a dynamic also visible in [[Kulichkina2026-zk]]'s account of coordinated Twitter activity around China's COVID-19 protests, where the same platform infrastructure simultaneously enables protest mobilization and state-aligned counter-narrative.

## Platform Affordances as Structuring Force

A second cluster interrogates how specific platform architectures—not just content—shape extremist circulation, directly answering [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s call to treat the internet's role as catalytic rather than incidental. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] provides the most systematic cross-platform test, showing that identical German far-right actors produce structurally distinct networks across Twitter, Telegram, YouTube, Instagram, and Facebook—evidence that affordances like connectivity, replicability, and scalability are relational outcomes of platform-design-meets-user-practice, not fixed properties. This affordance-sensitivity recurs in platform-specific studies: [[Karo2026-dn]] documents how jihadist supporters exploit TikTok's algorithmic and vernacular logics (audio camouflage, emoji codes, bait-and-switch) to achieve "everyday extremism" that evades keyword-based moderation; [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound itself as an underexamined propaganda vector in TikTok disinformation about Ukraine; and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] shows how TikTok's affordances amplify "legal-but-harmful" humor into normalized incivility among Filipino Gen Z influencers. [[Nangle2026-yo]] extends this logic to Instagram, arguing that positive affect, humor, and memetic softening—rather than overt hostility—constitute an "affective-discursive ecology" that normalizes far-right ideology through algorithmic proximity to mainstream content. [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s study of the post-deplatforming "Tate-space" on YouTube makes the structural point starkest: recommendation-driven circulation outpaces moderation, so an "ambient ideology" persists and even grows through diffuse, non-coordinated replatforming—reframing deplatforming as insufficient against infrastructural dynamics, a conclusion that resonates with [[Karo2026-dn]]'s critique of content-centric moderation.

Against this current of algorithm-blamed radicalization stands [[Brown2026-br]], whose rigorous audit design—separating algorithmic from user-choice effects—finds no independent YouTube radicalization pathway and only mild, user-driven echo chambers, though it does detect strong "rabbit hole" effects and a platform-wide conservative tilt in recommendations. This paper serves as an important empirical check on claims elsewhere in the topic cluster, suggesting that some of the amplification dynamics documented qualitatively (e.g., in [[Askanius2026-de]] or [[Groebner2026-pc]]) may be partly attributable to audience selection and networked promotion rather than algorithmic push alone.

## Hybridization, Mainstreaming, and Transnational Networks

Several papers trace how extremist actors professionalize and transnationalize via influencer and media logics. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents the Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube, showing how activists fuse microcelebrity branding, monetization, and political propaganda, using YouTube as a "sanitized" gateway funneling audiences toward alt-tech platforms when moderated. [[Groebner2026-pc]] scales this up internationally, finding substantial transnational commenter overlap among four English-language far-right hyperpartisan YouTube channels (Fox News, Sky News Australia, Rebel News, GB News), with US-centered content and anti-trans discourse functioning as transnational connective tissue. Both studies extend [[Kakavand2026-kt]]'s affordance argument by showing that hybridization operates not only across platforms but across national and commercial boundaries, echoing [[Rothut2026-or]]'s mainstreaming framework at a transnational scale. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] offers a conceptual bridge connecting these network-level findings to exposure research, proposing "networked exposure" to capture how ordinary content (news articles, Wikipedia pages) acquires politicized meaning once circulated within extremist-aligned networks—a notion that helps explain how channels like those in [[Groebner2026-pc]] can launder mainstream material into ideologically charged circulation.

## Manufactured and Synthetic Extremism

A final cluster examines the deliberate manufacture of extremist or state-aligned content, moving from grassroots radicalization toward organized and automated production. [[Marwick2025-vx]] grounds this shift at the individual level, showing that Far-Right "redpilling" is rarely a single conversion event but a processual socialization in which disinformation is marshaled as pseudo-rational "evidence," legitimized by a self-image of scientific skepticism—directly complicating simple "exposure equals belief" models critiqued by [[Rothut2026-wt]] and [[Brown2026-br]] alike. At the organizational level, [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT analysis of the Internet Research Agency reframes troll farms as labor-market actors embedded in ordinary media economies rather than ideologically driven cells, while Bastos2025-ol and [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] extend computational detection to visual self-presentation and Telegram forwarding networks, respectively, showing that state-aligned influence operations leave identifiable structural and aesthetic signatures. [[Kim2026-br]]'s two-decade analysis of Korean troll comments adds a crucial nuance: suspected foreign-state trolls favor morally condemning rhetoric over direct praise, and such condemnation is disproportionately amplified by engagement-based ranking—demonstrating that platform ranking logics, not just troll authorship, shape which propaganda succeeds. Finally, [[Crichton2026-kn]] pushes this manufacturing logic into the generative-AI era, arguing that commercial image models do not neutralize far-right visual content but sanitize its explicit markers while preserving legible in-group ideological cues—suggesting that the "affordance" story told throughout this topic (by [[Kakavand2026-kt]], [[Karo2026-dn]], [[Nangle2026-yo]]) will need to extend to generative systems whose biases are structural rather than incidental.

## Synthesis

Read together, these twenty papers trace an arc from the general scoping claim that online radicalization is incremental, hybrid, and catalyzed-not-caused ([[Rothut2026-wt]]) toward increasingly granular demonstrations of *how* that catalysis operates: through collective-action framing cycles ([[Bailard2024-pj]]), protest-based mainstreaming ([[Rothut2026-or]]), platform-specific affordances ([[Kakavand2026-kt]], [[Karo2026-dn]], [[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Bosch2024-hj]]), influencer hybridization ([[Askanius2026-de]], [[Groebner2026-pc]], [[Rieder2026-pp]]), narrated self-socialization ([[Marwick2025-vx]]), and industrialized or synthetic content production ([[Poliakoff2026-fa]], Bastos2025-ol, [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]], [[Kim2026-br]], [[Crichton2026-kn]]). [[Brown2026-br]] and [[De_Leon2025-qn]] serve as important methodological correctives, insisting respectively that algorithmic effects be empirically disentangled from user choice and that exposure research account for how ordinary content becomes politically weaponized through network circulation. The collective thrust is a move away from content-centric, single-platform, single-exposure models of radicalization toward ecological, infrastructural, and processual accounts—precisely the shift that [[Rothut2026-wt]] identifies as the field's unfinished business.
