# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping a fragmented field

The most natural entry point into this cluster is [[Rothut2026-wt]], a scoping review of a quarter-century of research that gives the rest of the corpus its conceptual vocabulary. Its central move—insisting that "online" and "offline" radicalization can no longer be meaningfully separated, and that the internet functions as a catalyst rather than a root cause—sets the terms that nearly every other paper here implicitly argues with. Its call to move from macro-level content studies toward longitudinal, causal, multi-level analysis is effectively answered by several of the empirical papers below, even if few achieve true causal identification. The review's observation that research has undergone an "empirical turn" since 2018, with growing attention to phenomena that resist left/right categorization (manosphere, conspiracy-driven, anti-system radicalization), also previews several papers that sit awkwardly outside a strict far-right/jihadist binary, such as the work on state-linked trolling and coordinated inauthentic behaviour discussed below.

## Platform affordances and the architecture of extremist networks

A cluster of papers takes affordance theory as its organizing lens, asking not *whether* platforms enable extremism but *how* specific technical features shape its form. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] offers the most systematic version of this argument, comparing far-right networks across five German platforms and showing that the same actors produce structurally distinct networks on each—Twitter/X as an expansive, low-reciprocity broadcast arena; Telegram as a clustered, inward-facing fringe space; Instagram as fragmented, high-reciprocity echo chambers; Facebook as a hierarchical party apparatus. This relational view of affordances—as sociotechnical outcomes rather than fixed properties—is echoed empirically by [[Askanius2026-de]], which traces how Swedish far-right YouTubers weave a cohesive "alternative influence network" through guest appearances, hyperlinks, and in-video mentions, fusing activist and influencer logics (microcelebrity branding, monetization, coded evasion of moderation). [[Groebner2026-pc]] extends this affordance-sensitive, network-analytic approach transnationally, showing sustained commenter overlap across four English-language hyperpartisan YouTube channels and identifying the US far right as the structural center of gravity for a genuinely transnational audience. Together these three papers suggest that YouTube in particular functions less as a single radicalizing pipeline than as a connective tissue—a "gateway" platform, in Askanius's terms—linking mainstream conservative audiences to fringe ecosystems elsewhere. [[Karo2026-dn]] extends the affordance argument to a very different ideological milieu, showing how jihadist supporters exploit TikTok's recommendation system and vernacular culture (audio camouflage, meme infiltration, emoji codes) to achieve an "everyday extremism" that evades keyword-based moderation—an important reminder that the platform-exploitation dynamics documented for the far right are not ideology-specific but structural, a point Karo makes explicitly by drawing parallels to anti-vaccine and far-right adversarial tactics.

## Multimodality, affect, and the aesthetics of mainstreaming

A second thread shifts from network structure to content form, asking how extremist messaging is stylistically softened to pass as mainstream or benign. [[Nangle2026-yo]] coins the idea of an "affective-discursive ecology" to describe how far-right Instagram Reels deploy humor, positive affect, and memetic form rather than overt hostility, arguing that normalization works through emotional resonance and algorithmic proximity rather than explicit ideological content. [[Rieder2026-pp]] documents a parallel dynamic around Andrew Tate on YouTube: even after deplatforming, an "ambient ideology" persists across thousands of diffuse, loosely affiliated channels—interviews, remixes, memes—that YouTube's moderation structurally cannot outpace, because recommendation-driven circulation moves faster than removal. Both papers converge on the claim that mainstreaming is now less about discrete propagandistic utterances than about diffuse, affectively charged, platform-native vernacular. [[Marwick2025-vx]] supplies the audience-side complement to this picture: analyzing how Far-Right adherents narrate their own radicalization as "redpilling," it shows that belief change is rarely a single conversion event but a gradual socialization process in which disinformation is marshaled as "evidence" to construct a self-image of rational, scientific inquiry. This explicitly challenges simple "exposure equals belief" models and dovetails with Rothut's review-level critique of linear radicalization narratives, while also explaining *why* the softened, humor-laden content documented by Nangle and Rieder can be so effective—it is absorbed into a longer process of self-styled critical thinking rather than registering as a discrete ideological imposition.

## Protest movements, mobilization, and the online–offline nexus

Two papers push directly on the online/offline boundary that Rothut's review argues is untenable. [[Bailard2024-pj]] offers one of the field's few genuinely causal analyses, using Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data to show that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) framing—but not explicit calls-to-action—predict subsequent offline violence, and that a reciprocal "online messaging–offline action cycle" links nonviolent protest to online motivational surges to later violence. This empirically operationalizes the hybrid socio-technical model that Rothut's review calls for, and argues pointedly against a narrow content-moderation lens focused on discrete hateful posts. [[Rothut2026-or]] examines a structurally similar dynamic from the meso level: the German Querdenken movement's Telegram communication, dominated by anti-elite and conspiratorial framing, gradually embedded protest publics within a broader far-right and conspiracist network via "content" and "connective" mechanisms, with explicit far-right ideology rare but rising over time. Read together, these two papers suggest protest movements can function as mainstreaming conduits in two complementary ways: discursively (shared anti-elite grievance as a bridge) and structurally (network embedding via key brokers)—with Bailard showing how this discursive-structural coupling can tip into coordinated violence.

## Coordinated inauthentic behaviour and state-linked manipulation

A more detection-oriented cluster examines how state and party actors manufacture the appearance of grassroots extremist or populist sentiment. [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] and [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] both document authoritarian-context coordination—Chinese state-aligned repression narratives contesting COVID-19 protest mobilization on Twitter, and Serbia's SNS party mimicking authentic grassroots support in ways that evade standard bot-detection methods—suggesting digital authoritarianism is evolving toward more decentralized, harder-to-detect mimicry rather than centralized bot farms. [[Kim2026-br]] extends this into a two-decade computational study of Korean news comments, finding that suspected state-linked trolls favor morally condemning rhetoric over direct praise of foreign actors, and that such condemnation is disproportionately amplified by engagement-based ranking—implicating platform recommendation logics, not just troll authorship, in extremist/polarizing visibility, a finding that resonates with the amplification dynamics Rieder documents for Tate content on YouTube.

## Content moderation, deplatforming, and its limits

Several papers converge on a skeptical assessment of moderation-as-removal. [[Simeone2025-vo]] is the most affirmative case for targeted intervention: Twitter's 2021 suspension of eight accounts produced network-wide "ripple effects" that collapsed the Arizona Election Review community's hub structure and shifted its discourse from mobilization to abstract defiance, suggesting that *targeted* deplatforming of central nodes can meaningfully disrupt coordination (unlike the diffuse visibility-reduction strategies favored under subsequent ownership). This sits in some tension with [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Karo2026-dn]], both of which show moderation being structurally outpaced—by recommendation-driven circulation in Tate's case, by simple textual and visual evasion tactics in the TikTok jihadist case. The contrast suggests a possible resolution: moderation may be effective against centralized, hub-dependent mobilization networks (Simeone's case) but far less effective against diffuse, memetic, vernacular-embedded content ecosystems (Rieder's and Karo's cases)—a distinction the field would benefit from theorizing more explicitly.

## Generative AI as an emerging vector

A final, forward-looking cluster examines generative AI's entry into this ecosystem. [[Beacken2026-zb]] provides the comparative, structural counterpoint to technological determinism, showing across six democratically weakened countries that GenAI propaganda adoption is shaped by local political and economic conditions rather than following a uniform trajectory. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] narrows to far-right campaigning during the 2024 European elections, documenting the integration of AI-generated multimedia into populist Instagram and X messaging. [[Crichton2026-kn]] offers the most theoretically pointed contribution, arguing via a media experiment that commercial generative models do not neutralize extremist imagery so much as sanitize its explicit markers while preserving ideological content in subtler, ambient form—an argument that extends Nangle's and Rieder's accounts of affective softening into the domain of AI-mediated image production, and that reframes the "deepfake" threat model: the danger lies not in deception but in ideological transmission through aesthetically laundered content.
