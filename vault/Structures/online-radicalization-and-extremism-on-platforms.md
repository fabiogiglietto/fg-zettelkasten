# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## The Arc of Inquiry: From Content to Ecosystem

The papers gathered here trace a shared trajectory away from an older, content-centric model of online radicalization—where a single piece of propaganda "causes" belief—toward a socio-technical, ecological understanding in which platforms, affordances, algorithms, and identity work jointly produce durable extremist infrastructures. [[Rothut2026-wt]], the scoping review anchoring this collection, formalizes this shift explicitly: surveying 382 studies across 25 years, it argues that the internet functions as an accelerant rather than a root cause, that online/offline boundaries are analytically untenable, and that mechanisms operate simultaneously at macro, meso, and micro levels. Nearly every other paper in this set can be read as an empirical instantiation of one of these levels or as a challenge to the review's synthesis.

### Platform Affordances as Adversarial Terrain

A cluster of papers documents how extremist actors treat specific platform architectures as exploitable terrain. [[Karo2026-dn]] offers perhaps the sharpest case: jihadist supporters on TikTok deploy five distinct evasion strategies (audio camouflage, meme infiltration, blurred intent, emoji codes, bait-and-switch) that embed propaganda in the platform's participatory vernacular rather than presenting it as recognizable "terrorist content." This notion of *everyday extremism*—normalization through mundane platform genres rather than spectacle—resonates strongly with [[Nangle2026-yo]], where far-right Instagram Reels use humor and positive affect to soften ideological content into an "affective-discursive ecology," and with [[Rieder2026-pp]], which shows that Andrew Tate's YouTube presence survives deplatforming precisely because ambient, memeified, motivational content diffuses faster than moderation can act. Across these three papers, a consistent finding emerges: moderation systems built to detect explicit hate speech are structurally mismatched to content that is coded, humorous, or aspirational, and platforms' own recommendation systems often out-accelerate their removal capacities. [[Bosch2024-hj]] extends this affordance-specific logic to sound, showing that TikTok's audio layer functions as an under-examined vector for pandemic- and war-related disinformation—reinforcing the broader point that each platform's distinctive modality (audio, Reels, Shorts) generates its own propaganda techniques.

### Networked Infrastructures and Coordination

A second cluster shifts from individual content tactics to network-level infrastructure. [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube, showing how guest appearances, hyperlinking, and in-video mentions weave influencers, alt-media, and organized groups into a cohesive, commercially hybridized ecosystem that uses YouTube as a "sanitized" gateway toward more extreme alt-tech spaces. This gateway logic parallels [[Rothut2026-or]], which introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to explain how the German Querdenken movement's Telegram channels became structurally embedded—via anti-elite framing and shifting outlink patterns—within a broader far-right and conspiracist network, with prominent far-right nodes (not the protest movement itself) occupying the network's brokerage center. State-aligned coordination receives similar computational treatment in [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] (pro-government Telegram amplification in Russia and Belarus) and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] (dual-use coordination on Twitter around China's COVID protests), both demonstrating that coordinated inauthentic behavior is detectable at scale and that the same connective infrastructures serve both mobilization and repression. Bastos2025-ol pushes this computational-detection agenda into the visual register, showing that troll-farm profile imagery carries country-specific signatures—extending network-based attribution methods into multimodal territory.

### From Online Discourse to Offline Action

Where the above clusters address circulation and infrastructure, [[Bailard2024-pj]] tackles the causal question most directly, using Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data to show that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) framing—not explicit calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, with a reciprocal four-week online–offline cycle. This finding complicates simple "algorithmic radicalization" narratives by locating causal power in emotional and identity framing rather than incitement, and it dovetails with [[Brown2026-br]]'s rigorous audit study, which finds no evidence that YouTube's recommendation algorithm independently drives users toward ideologically extreme content (though it does produce robust "rabbit holes" and a platform-wide moderate-conservative tilt). Read together, these two papers push back against deterministic algorithmic-radicalization claims, redirecting attention toward framing dynamics and user agency, respectively—an empirical corrective that sits productively alongside [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s observation that causal, longitudinal evidence remains the field's greatest gap.

### Identity, Epistemology, and the Redpill

A final thread interrogates radicalization from the inside, asking how participants themselves narrate and legitimize belief change. [[Marwick2025-vx]]'s analysis of "redpilling" narratives directly challenges the "exposure equals belief" model that much algorithmic-radicalization discourse presupposes, showing instead a gradual socialization process in which disinformation is marshaled as "evidence" to construct a self-image of rational, scientific superiority. [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends this epistemological lens to ConspiracyTok, where young, diverse creators engage in "populist knowledge production" under a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering for marginalized standpoints and a vector for antisemitism and disinformation. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] generalizes this identity-centered approach across six platforms in the Great Reset case, arguing that symbolic boundary work—reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor, building affective solidarity around shared grievance—sustains conspiracy communities more than platform affordances or ideological content per se. Together, these three papers constitute a pointed counter-argument within the collection: against techno-centric explanations (echo chambers, algorithmic amplification), they insist that meaning-making, identity construction, and affective belonging are the primary engines of radicalization, with platforms serving as stages rather than causes.

### Synthesis

Taken together, these fifteen papers chart a field moving from asking *what content does* to asking *how ecosystems cohere*—across affordances (TikTok's audio and vernacular, Instagram's Reels, YouTube's recommendation architecture), infrastructures (alternative influence networks, coordinated amplification, protest-facilitated mainstreaming), and human meaning-making (redpilling, boundary work, populist epistemology). The causal and algorithmic-effects papers ([[Bailard2024-pj]], [[Brown2026-br]]) serve as an empirical check on both journalistic alarmism and platform-affordance determinism, while [[Rothut2026-wt]] provides the conceptual scaffolding that lets the other fourteen studies—spanning far-right, jihadist, conspiracist, and state-aligned actors—be read as complementary probes of a single underlying question: how do hybrid, multimodal, platform-native environments make radical ideas not just visible, but livable, shareable, and eventually normal.
