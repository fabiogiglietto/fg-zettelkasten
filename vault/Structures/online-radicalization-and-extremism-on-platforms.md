# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping the Field

[[Rothut2026-wt]] provides the scaffolding for this collection: a 25-year scoping review of 382 studies that reframes "online radicalization" as an incremental, multi-factor process in which the internet functions as catalyst rather than cause, and in which strict online/offline separation is no longer analytically defensible. Its call for hybrid, socio-technical, longitudinal analysis—and its observation that the field is diversifying beyond left-right binaries into misogynist, conspiracist, and anti-system strands—sets the terms against which the more granular, platform-specific studies filed here can be read. Nearly every other paper in this set can be located within its macro/meso/micro and Lasswellian (communicator/content/medium/recipient) grid, which is itself a useful diagnostic for what each individual study contributes and what it leaves out.

## Platform Affordances as Structuring Forces

A recurring argument across this literature is that extremist actors do not simply transplant the same content across platforms; they adapt to platform-specific affordances, producing markedly different network topologies and rhetorical styles. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] makes this explicit, comparing German far-right networks across five platforms and showing that even superficially similar "connectors" (retweets, forwards, shares) yield distinct configurations—Twitter/X as a sprawling, low-reciprocity broadcast arena; Telegram as a clustered, fringe-dominated inward network; Instagram as fragmented but tightly-knit echo chambers; Facebook as a hierarchical, AfD-dominated organization tool. This affordance-sensitive logic recurs in [[Karo2026-dn]], where TikTok's recommendation system and vernacular meme culture are exploited by jihadist supporters through five identifiable evasion strategies (audio camouflage, meme infiltration, blurred intent, emoji codes, bait-and-switch)—tactics the authors note are not ideology-specific but emerge from TikTok's structural design itself, echoed independently in far-right and anti-vaccine communities. [[Rieder2026-pp]] extends this affordance argument to YouTube's recommendation architecture, showing how Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology" persists and even grows post-deplatforming because recommendation-driven circulation structurally outpaces moderation—a finding that reframes deplatforming as a symbolic rather than a materially effective governance tool. Together these three studies argue for shifting analytic attention from discrete pieces of content to platform infrastructure and circulation dynamics.

## Softening, Humor, and the Aesthetics of Mainstreaming

A second cluster examines how extremist ideology is rendered palatable through affect, humor, and multimodal style rather than explicit hostility. [[Nangle2026-yo]] coins the notion of an "affective-discursive ecology" to describe how far-right Instagram Reels use positive affect, memes, and humor to normalize extremist worldviews, with algorithmic proximity reinforcing visibility. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] offers a non-Western complement, showing how Filipino Gen Z TikTok "dogshows" normalize legal-but-harmful humor that blurs into cyberbullying and hate speech—demonstrating that incivility-as-entertainment is a broader platform-cultural logic, not confined to explicitly ideological far-right content. [[Rothut2026-or]] theorizes a related but distinct process—protest-facilitated mainstreaming—arguing that movements like Querdenken lower the threshold for encountering extremist actors not primarily through ideological conversion but through shared anti-elite framing and structural embedding within far-right/conspiracist Telegram networks; anti-elitism, not overt ideology, functions as the connective tissue. [[Marwick2025-vx]] pushes this normalization logic to the level of individual narrative, showing through "redpilling" testimonies that belief adoption in far-right communities is rarely a single conversion event but a gradual socialization process in which disinformation is reframed as "evidence" and self-styled rational skepticism legitimizes hateful conclusions. Read together, these four papers suggest mainstreaming operates at multiple, mutually reinforcing scales: aesthetic/affective (Nangle, Cabbuag), structural/network (Rothut2026-or), and narrative/identity (Marwick)—complicating any simple "exposure causes belief" model, a point Marwick makes explicit and Rothut2026-wt corroborates at the review level.

## Networked Ecosystems, Influencer Economies, and Transnational Circulation

A third thread concerns how extremist and hyperpartisan actors are professionalized and interconnected as networked ecosystems rather than isolated nodes. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents a Swedish "alternative influence network" on YouTube in which far-right influencers fuse microcelebrity branding, monetization, and political propaganda, using YouTube as a "sanitized" gateway funneling audiences toward more extreme alt-tech platforms—a hybridization of activist and commercial logics. [[Groebner2026-pc]] scales this argument transnationally, showing sustained commenter overlap across four English-language far-right hyperpartisan YouTube channels (US, UK, Canada, Australia), with US content occupying a central gravitational position and the most transnational users being the most engaged—evidence of an integrated international far-right audience rather than discrete national publics. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] offers a conceptual corrective relevant to both: "networked exposure" captures how even ordinary, non-extremist content (news stories, Wikipedia entries) becomes politically weaponized once circulated within these ideologically-charged networks, suggesting that ecosystem-level analysis must account for content that is not intrinsically radical but becomes radicalized through its circulatory context—a notion that resonates with Rothut2026-or's connective mechanisms and Kakavand's network-structural approach.

## From Online Discourse to Offline Mobilization

Finally, [[Bailard2024-pj]] closes the loop between discursive analysis and real-world consequence, demonstrating via Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity/pride) framing—not explicit calls-to-action—predict subsequent offline violence, with a reciprocal online-offline mobilization cycle unfolding over roughly four weeks. This finding pointedly challenges content-moderation paradigms fixated on discrete "prognostic" or overtly violent posts, aligning with this collection's broader argument (echoed in Karo2026-dn's "everyday extremism," Rieder2026-pp's "ambient ideology," and Nangle2026-yo's "softening") that the mechanisms sustaining extremism are diffuse, affective, and infrastructural rather than isolated to identifiable violent content—precisely the shift toward ecosystem-level, longitudinal, and infrastructurally-aware analysis that Rothut2026-wt identifies as the field's necessary next horizon.
