# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping the terrain: definitions, scope, and the online/offline divide

Any synthesis of this literature must start from [[Rothut2026-wt]], whose scoping review of 382 publications spanning 25 years provides the conceptual scaffolding for everything else filed here. It proposes radicalization as an incremental, multi-factor process rather than a single event, insists that the internet functions as a *catalyst* rather than a root cause, and—most consequentially for this collection—argues that separating "online" from "offline" radicalization is no longer analytically tenable. This last claim is less a throwaway observation than the organizing tension of the corpus: almost every paper here is, in some fashion, an attempt to specify *how* digital infrastructures translate into real-world belief, affect, or action, and several explicitly push back against crude "exposure equals effect" models that the review identifies as still common but increasingly discredited.

## Platform affordances: the same actors, different networks

A cluster of papers treats "platform" not as a neutral backdrop but as a constitutive variable shaping extremist communication. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] offers the most systematic demonstration: tracing the same German far-right seed accounts across five platforms, it shows that connectivity, replicability, and scalability are *relational* outcomes of platform design meeting user practice, not fixed properties—Twitter/X produces an expansive, low-reciprocity broadcast arena; Telegram an inward-oriented fringe network; Instagram tight but fragmented echo chambers; Facebook a hierarchical party apparatus. This affordance-sensitive logic recurs throughout the topic: [[Karo2026-dn]] shows jihadist supporters decomposing TikTok's recommendation and keyword-filtering architecture into exploitable seams (audio manipulation, emoji codes, meme camouflage), framing their output as "everyday extremism" embedded in platform vernacular rather than traditional propaganda spectacle. [[Nangle2026-yo]] extends this to Instagram, where far-right Reels deploy humor and positive affect—an "affective-discursive ecology"—to soften ideology for algorithmic proximity rather than confrontation. Together these studies suggest a shift in how extremist actors operate: less as broadcasters of explicit hate, more as vernacular participants gaming platform-specific grammars of virality.

## Mainstreaming, hybridization, and transnational audiences

A second thread examines how extremist content migrates from fringe to mainstream visibility. [[Rothut2026-or]] coins "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to explain how Germany's Querdenken movement, though initially heterogeneous, became structurally entangled with far-right and conspiracist Telegram networks via shared anti-elite framing rather than explicit ideological commitment—a mechanism of content and connective diffusion that echoes the "gateway belief" logic Marwick and Furl later describe at the individual level. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents a parallel hybridization on Swedish YouTube, where influencers fuse activist tactics with microcelebrity branding and monetization, and where YouTube itself functions as a "sanitised" gateway funneling audiences toward alt-tech platforms. [[Rieder2026-pp]] pushes this further with the concept of "ambient ideology": even after Andrew Tate's deplatforming, a decentralized ecosystem of interviews and remixes keeps his worldview circulating, outpacing moderation because recommendation systems amplify content faster than removal teams can act. At a transnational scale, [[Groebner2026-pc]] shows sustained commenter overlap across four Anglophone far-right news channels (Sky News Australia, Fox News, Rebel News, GB News), with transphobia and US politics serving as connective tissue—evidence that mainstreaming operates not just vertically (fringe to mass) but horizontally across national borders.

## Algorithmic exploitation versus algorithmic agency

A recurring and productive disagreement concerns how much causal weight to assign the algorithm itself. Where [[Karo2026-dn]], [[Nangle2026-yo]], and [[Rieder2026-pp]] emphasize structural vulnerabilities and recommendation-driven amplification that outpace moderation, [[Brown2026-br]] offers a methodologically rigorous counterpoint: using an audit design that disentangles algorithmic from user-choice effects, it finds no evidence that YouTube's recommender independently drives users into echo chambers or radicalization pathways—only a general conservative tilt and strong "rabbit hole" effects driven by content proximity rather than ideological escalation. This tempers the more alarmist readings elsewhere in the topic and suggests the field needs more supply/demand-disentangling designs like this one before attributing radicalization dynamics to algorithms per se, rather than to adversarial creativity on the user side ([[Karo2026-dn]]) or to broader affective-discursive ecologies ([[Nangle2026-yo]]).

## Moderation, deplatforming, and its limits

Several papers interrogate what happens when platforms intervene. [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers the clearest causal evidence that targeted deplatforming can work: Twitter's 2021 suspension of eight Arizona election-fraud accounts collapsed the right-leaning cluster's hub/authority structure and shifted discourse from mobilization to abstract grievance, a genuine "network intervention" effect not attributable to bot activity. Yet [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Askanius2026-de]] complicate any optimism about moderation's efficacy at scale: Tate's YouTube ecosystem regenerates faster than it can be pruned, and Swedish far-right actors treat moderation itself as fuel for persecution narratives, adapting through coded language and "phantom videos" redirecting to alt-tech platforms. [[Karo2026-dn]] arrives at a similar structural diagnosis for jihadist content on TikTok. Read together, these papers suggest deplatforming's effectiveness is highly contingent on *where* in the network it is applied (central hubs, as in Simeone, versus diffuse ambient ecosystems, as in Rieder) and that infrastructural, rather than purely content-based, responses are needed—a conclusion [[Karo2026-dn]] states explicitly.

## Coordination, state actors, and authoritarian information ecosystems

A distinct but related set of papers moves from organic extremist mobilization to engineered coordination. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] identifies clusters of pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus coordinating via forwarding to amplify regime narratives, while [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] finds thousands of Twitter accounts engaged in coordinated activity around China's 2022 COVID protests, illustrating social media's "dual-use" character as simultaneously a protest-mobilization and a repression infrastructure. [[Kim2026-br]] extends this into a two-decade, large-scale computational analysis of Korean news comments, revealing that suspected state-linked trolls favor morally condemning rhetoric over direct praise—a polarization-amplifying strategy rewarded disproportionately by engagement-based ranking. These papers collectively suggest that the platform dynamics enabling grassroots far-right and jihadist mobilization elsewhere in this topic are structurally similar to those exploited by state actors, raising the question (not fully resolved in the corpus) of where organic extremist communities end and manufactured coordination begins—a question [[De_Leon2025-qn]] addresses conceptually by proposing "networked exposure" to capture how even ordinary, non-ideological content (news stories, Wikipedia entries) becomes weaponized once circulated within extremist-aligned networks.

## From online discourse to offline action

Bridging micro and macro levels, [[Bailard2024-pj]] provides the topic's most direct test of online-to-offline causality, using Granger causality on 500,000 Proud Boys Telegram messages to show that diagnostic (grievance) framing predicts subsequent offline violence, while motivational framing exists in a reciprocal four-week cycle with nonviolent protest activity. This complements [[Rothut2026-or]]'s meso-level mainstreaming account of Querdenken: both papers treat Telegram not merely as a recruitment channel but as a discursive ecosystem with its own temporal dynamics feeding back into real-world mobilization, challenging the discrete-post content-moderation paradigm that still dominates platform governance debates.

## Individual pathways and the limits of "exposure"

At the micro level, [[Marwick2025-vx]] directly challenges the "exposure equals belief" model implicit in much algorithmic-amplification scholarship. Analyzing redpilling narratives across Reddit, Gab, and Discord, it shows that most self-reported radicalization is processual rather than a single "eureka" conversion, driven by sustained community participation in which disinformation is marshaled as "evidence" to construct a self-image of rational, scientific superiority. This finding is a useful corrective to papers elsewhere in the topic that risk implying algorithmic or content exposure alone drives extremism; it instead foregrounds socialization, affect, and identity-work—aligning with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s observation that single short-term exposure rarely produces radicalization without prior dispositional factors.

## The emerging frontier: generative AI

Closing the topic, two papers gesture toward where this research agenda is heading. [[Beacken2026-zb]] offers a comparative account of generative AI's use in political propaganda across six democratically weakened countries, arguing against deterministic narratives of uniform AI impact and for structurally grounded, context-sensitive analysis. [[Crichton2026-kn]] provides a sharper empirical probe, demonstrating via a "re-generative" media experiment that commercial image models do not neutralize far-right visual propaganda but sanitize its explicit markers while preserving ideological content in subtler, ambient form—reframing the danger of AI-generated extremist media away from deception (deepfakes) and toward ideological transmission. Together with the algorithmic-exploitation and ambient-ideology themes above, these papers suggest the next phase of this research agenda will need to extend the same affordance-sensitive, infrastructure-critical analysis developed for recommendation systems and moderation to generative models themselves.
