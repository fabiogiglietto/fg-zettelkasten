# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## The arc of inquiry: from content to infrastructure to synthesis

This body of work traces a shift that mirrors the broader maturation of radicalization studies: early content-centric questions ("what do extremists post?") give way to infrastructural ones ("how do platform affordances, algorithms, and moderation regimes structure extremist circulation?") and finally to synthetic, conceptual work that tries to hold the whole socio-technical system in view. [[Rothut2026-wt]], a 25-year scoping review of 382 publications, provides the scaffolding for this collection: it argues that the field has undergone an "empirical turn" since 2018, that online/offline separation is conceptually untenable, and that radicalization is best studied as a multi-level (macro/meso/micro), multi-factor process rather than a single-cause effect of exposure. Nearly every other paper in this set can be read as filling in one cell of the macro-meso-micro, communicator-content-medium-recipient grid that review proposes.

## Platform affordances as the unit of analysis

A dominant strand treats the platform itself—not the ideology—as the primary explanatory variable. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] makes this explicit, showing that the same far-right actor networks produce radically different structures on Twitter/X (expansive, low-reciprocity broadcast), Telegram (fringe, inward-facing amplification), YouTube (loosely connected thematic gateways), Instagram (tightly clustered but fragmented echo chambers), and Facebook (hierarchical, party-controlled). This affordance-relational logic—that connectivity, replicability, and scalability are produced by the interaction of design and practice, not fixed platform properties—recurs throughout the collection. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents how jihadist supporters exploit TikTok's recommendation system and keyword-filter weaknesses through audio camouflage, meme infiltration, and emoji codes, framing this as "everyday extremism" that collapses spectacle into vernacular participation; strikingly, the paper notes these evasion tactics are not ideology-specific but recur across far-right, anti-vaccine, and jihadist communities alike, reinforcing the infrastructural-over-ideological framing. [[Bosch2024-hj]] extends this logic to the sonic register, arguing that TikTok's audio affordances constitute an underexamined vector for disinformation about the Ukraine invasion, distinct from text- or image-based propaganda. [[Nangle2026-yo]] performs a parallel move for Instagram, showing how positive affect, humour, and memetic "softening" — rather than overt hostility — normalize far-right worldviews within what the authors call an "affective-discursive ecology," with algorithmic proximity driving mobilization.

Deplatforming and moderation studies push this infrastructural lens toward governance questions. [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows that removing Andrew Tate's official channels did not reduce his visibility on YouTube but displaced it into a diffuse, ambient "Tate-space" of interviews, remixes, and memefied content that recommendation systems amplify faster than moderators can remove — reframing moderation as a problem of governing circulation, not just deleting content. [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers a contrasting, more optimistic data point: targeted deplatforming of a small number of hub accounts in the Arizona Election Review community produced disproportionate network collapse and a shift from mobilization to abstract defiance, suggesting that "network interventions" can work when they hit structurally central nodes rather than merely reducing visibility. Read together, these two papers suggest moderation efficacy may hinge on whether extremist visibility is sustained by decentralized algorithmic amplification (Tate-space) or concentrated hub authority (AER)—a distinction [[Kakavand2026-kt]]'s cross-platform network typology helps explain.

## Mainstreaming, hybridization, and the blurring of boundaries

A second cluster concerns how extremist content migrates from fringe to mainstream, often through actors and movements that are not themselves straightforwardly extremist. [[Rothut2026-or]] coins "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to describe how the German Querdenken movement, via Telegram, became entangled with far-right and conspiracist networks—not through explicit ideological conversion but through shared anti-elite framing that bridged moderate sympathizers and extreme milieus. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents a structurally similar dynamic in the Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube, where Covid-era instability catalyzed hybridization across activists, influencers, and alternative media, fusing political propaganda with commercial microcelebrity practices (sponsorships, merchandise, parasocial branding). [[Groebner2026-pc]] scales this up transnationally, showing sustained commenter overlap across Sky News Australia, Fox News, Rebel News, and GB News on YouTube, with US content and anti-trans framing operating as transnational connective tissue—evidence that far-right mainstreaming is not merely a national phenomenon but a networked, algorithmically-abetted information space. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] supplies a conceptual bridge for all of this: "networked exposure" captures how ostensibly neutral content (news articles, Wikipedia pages) becomes ideologically weaponized simply by virtue of its circulation within extremist-adjacent networks, complementing the mainstreaming literature's emphasis on process over singular content encounters.

## Individual-level belief formation and the limits of "exposure"

Against the platform/network focus, [[Marwick2025-vx]] returns attention to the individual, challenging the "exposure equals belief" model that implicitly underlies much algorithmic-harm research. Its qualitative analysis of "redpilling" narratives shows that most adherents describe gradual socialization—accumulating pseudoscientific "evidence" that lets them see themselves as rational truth-seekers—rather than sudden conversion by a single piece of content. This finding complicates the causal stories implied by papers like [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Nangle2026-yo]] (which emphasize ambient, affect-laden content as sufficient for normalization) and dovetails with [[Brown2026-br]]'s rigorous algorithm-audit of YouTube, which finds no evidence of an algorithmically-driven "radicalization pathway," only mild user-driven echo chambers and strong content-based "rabbit holes," alongside a platform-wide conservative tilt. Together, [[Marwick2025-vx]] and [[Brown2026-br]] serve as an important corrective within this topic cluster: they empirically test and largely reject the simplest causal narratives (single-exposure radicalization, algorithmic funneling) that motivate much of the platform-affordance scholarship, pushing toward [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s call for multi-factor, longitudinal models.

## Collective action, mobilization, and the online-offline link

Where most papers treat "radicalization" as belief change, [[Bailard2024-pj]] asks the harder question of behavioral translation: does online discourse predict offline violence? Using Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data, it finds diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) framing—but not prognostic calls-to-action—predict offline violent events, with a roughly four-week online-offline feedback cycle. This paper stands out for moving beyond the discrete-post content-moderation paradigm shared by much of this literature toward a dynamic, ecosystem-level account of mobilization, directly operationalizing the "catalyst not root cause" thesis of [[Rothut2026-wt]] and intersecting with [[Rothut2026-or]]'s meso-level mainstreaming mechanisms (both are concerned with how protest/movement online activity translates into broader ideological and behavioral effects).

## State actors, coordinated inauthenticity, and generative AI

A final strand shifts from organic extremist communities to state and AI-driven manipulation, which nonetheless shares the platform-exploitation logic running through the whole topic. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]], [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]], Bastos2025-ol, and [[Kim2026-br]] collectively map an evolving repertoire of coordinated inauthentic behavior: regime-amplifying Telegram clusters in Russia/Belarus, SNS's harder-to-detect mimicry of grassroots support in Serbia, visual-identity signatures of troll farms detectable via computer vision, and—in the most methodologically ambitious case—a two-decade, explainable-ML analysis of Korean news comments showing that state-linked trolling relies more on morally condemning rhetoric (which the ranking algorithm disproportionately amplifies) than on direct partisan praise. These papers extend the topic's platform-affordance logic to state-sponsored rather than grassroots extremism, showing that engagement-based ranking systems reward moral outrage regardless of whether its source is an organic movement or an intelligence operation.

Generative AI emerges as the newest frontier, extending rather than rupturing these dynamics. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] documents far-right adoption of AI-generated multimedia in 2024 European election campaigning on Instagram and X; [[Beacken2026-zb]] cautions against technologically deterministic readings by showing GenAI propaganda use varies substantially across six democratically weakened countries depending on local structural conditions; and [[Crichton2026-kn]] offers the most theoretically pointed contribution, arguing that generative models' tendency to "sanitize" explicit hateful imagery while preserving ideological cues in subtler, ambient form is a structural property of training data and architecture—not an accidental failure. This closes the loop with [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]]: across platforms and now across generative technologies, this literature converges on the finding that contemporary extremism increasingly operates not through overt spectacle but through ambient, affectively softened, algorithmically sustained normalization that evades both detection and simple causal measurement.
