# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping the field: from exposure to socio-technical ecosystems

The scoping review [[Rothut2026-wt]] provides the conceptual scaffolding for this entire cluster of work: after synthesizing 382 studies spanning 25 years, it insists that "online" and "offline" radicalization can no longer be treated as separate domains, that the internet functions as an accelerant rather than a root cause, and that mechanisms operate simultaneously at macro (discursive), meso (group), and micro (individual) levels. Nearly every other paper filed here can be read as an empirical instantiation of one or more of these levels—testing, refining, or complicating the review's claim that simple "exposure equals belief" models are inadequate and that phenomenologically diverse forms (misogynist, conspiracist, anti-system) increasingly resist classic left–right taxonomies.

## Platform affordances and the architecture of extremist circulation

A recurring move across the corpus is to treat platforms not as neutral pipes but as sociotechnical structures that shape what extremist communication can look like. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] makes this explicit at the network level, showing that the same German far-right actors produce structurally distinct networks on Twitter/X, Telegram, YouTube, Instagram, and Facebook—broadcast-style scalability on Twitter, fragmented echo chambers on Instagram, hierarchical party control on Facebook—demonstrating that affordances are relational outcomes of platform design meeting situated practice, not fixed properties. This affordance-sensitive lens recurs in studies of specific platforms: [[Karo2026-dn]] catalogues five concrete evasion tactics (audio camouflage, meme infiltration, blurred intent, emoji codes, bait-and-switch) that let jihadist supporters exploit TikTok's recommendation system and vernacular culture, arguing that such "everyday extremism" is not ideology-specific but a structural feature of adversarial creativity on algorithmically curated platforms. [[Nangle2026-yo]] extends the affordance argument to affect, showing how Instagram's algorithmic proximity and memetic Reels let far-right actors normalize ideology through humor and positive valence rather than hostility—an "affective-discursive ecology" that softens ideological content for wider circulation.

## Algorithms, moderation, and the limits of platform governance

Several papers directly interrogate whether recommendation algorithms actively drive people toward extremism, and the answers are more mixed than popular narratives suggest. [[Brown2026-br]]'s audit of YouTube finds no evidence of algorithmically-driven radicalization pathways and only mild, user-driven echo chamber effects—though it does detect a platform-wide conservative tilt and strong "rabbit hole" dynamics where the current video heavily predicts the next recommendation. This tempers claims found elsewhere: [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows that even after Andrew Tate's official deplatforming, YouTube's recommendation engine outpaces its own moderation, sustaining an "ambient ideology" through a decentralized ecosystem of interviews, remixes, and memes—moderation targets discrete accounts while circulation itself proves nearly ungovernable. [[Karo2026-dn]]'s TikTok findings reinforce this infrastructural critique, showing that keyword-based moderation is trivially bypassed by vernacular evasion. Together these three papers push against a content-removal paradigm and toward viewing moderation failure as a structural, not merely policy, problem—a framing that resonates with [[Bailard2024-pj]]'s explicit call to move past "discrete hateful posts" toward analyzing discursive ecosystems and their temporal dynamics.

## Protest, coordination, and the online–offline nexus

A cluster of studies examines how online discourse translates into offline mobilization, and how protest movements themselves become vectors for mainstreaming. [[Bailard2024-pj]] offers the most granular causal evidence: Granger-causality analysis of Proud Boys Telegram channels shows diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) framing—rather than explicit calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, with a reciprocal four-week cycle linking nonviolent protest, online morale-building, and later violence. [[Rothut2026-or]] examines a parallel dynamic in the German Querdenken movement, introducing "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to describe how anti-elite, conspiratorial framing on Telegram—paired with connective infrastructures like outlinking and network embedding—gradually routed a heterogeneous protest public toward far-right and conspiracist milieus, with prominent far-right figures (not the movement itself) occupying the network's brokerage core. Both papers thus complicate simple radicalization narratives by foregrounding meso-level group dynamics and framing processes over individual conversion.

This coordination lens extends to explicitly authoritarian contexts. [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] finds that Twitter functioned as dual-use infrastructure during China's COVID-19 protests, simultaneously enabling protest coordination and state-aligned counter-narratives, detectable through computational coordination analysis. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] documents the inverse configuration in Russia and Belarus, where pro-government Telegram channels use forwarding networks to coordinate top-down amplification of regime narratives. Read alongside the Western protest cases, these papers suggest that the same computational signatures of coordinated activity—and the same platform affordances (forwarding, connective outlinking)—can serve grassroots mobilization, far-right mainstreaming, or state-aligned repression, depending on political context.

## Narrating radicalization: epistemics, affect, and individual pathways

Where the previous cluster operates at the network and group level, two papers by Marwick and colleagues push toward the individual and discursive level, directly challenging exposure-based models. [[Marwick2025-vx]] shows through qualitative analysis of "redpilling" narratives that most Far-Right adherents describe gradual socialization rather than sudden conversion, with disinformation functioning as "evidence" that lets adherents style themselves as rational, scientific evaluators—a self-presentation the authors argue is central to legitimizing extremist belief. [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends this epistemic argument to "ConspiracyTok," finding that young, often non-White and female creators construct a "generous epistemology" and "populist knowledge production" that can simultaneously voice marginalized standpoint critique and circulate antisemitic or transphobic disinformation, using conspiracy as a boundary object connecting otherwise disparate communities. Both papers directly corroborate [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s review finding that single exposure rarely radicalizes and that prior attitudes, identity, and community socialization are decisive—while also demonstrating empirically the demographic and ideological heterogeneity the review notes as an emerging trend beyond classic left–right categories.

## Transnational hybridization and the commercialization of extremism

Two studies trace how far-right actors scale beyond national and platform boundaries by fusing political and commercial logics. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents a Swedish "alternative influence network" on YouTube during the pandemic, showing how guest appearances, hyperlinking, and in-video mentions bind influencers, alternative media, and organized groups into a cohesive but ideologically diverse ecosystem that hybridizes microcelebrity branding, monetization, and propaganda, while treating deplatforming as fuel for persecution narratives. [[Groebner2026-pc]] scales this argument up to a transnational register, finding sustained commenter overlap across four English-language hyperpartisan news channels (US, UK, Canada, Australia) on YouTube, with US content occupying a structurally central position and transphobia functioning as a shared transnational connector. Together these papers extend the affordance and network arguments of [[Kakavand2026-kt]] and the ambient-circulation logic of [[Rieder2026-pp]] into explicitly commercial and transnational registers, suggesting that professionalization and audience-building strategies, not just ideological content, drive far-right platform success.

## New frontiers: generative AI and the boundaries of exposure

Two papers gesture toward emerging directions for the field. [[Beacken2026-zb]] cautions against technologically deterministic assumptions about generative AI's role in propaganda, showing across six democratically weakened states that GenAI adoption for political messaging varies with local political, economic, and social structures rather than following a uniform trajectory—a corrective in the same spirit as [[Brown2026-br]]'s algorithmic-audit skepticism toward totalizing claims about platform effects. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] closes the loop conceptually, proposing "networked exposure" to capture how ostensibly neutral content (news articles, Wikipedia entries) becomes weaponized once circulated within extremist-aligned networks—a framing that dovetails with [[Rothut2026-or]]'s content/connective mechanism distinction and with [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s account of "ambient" ideological circulation, and that points toward future work attentive not just to what extremist actors post, but to how ordinary content is recontextualized as it moves through radicalized networks.
