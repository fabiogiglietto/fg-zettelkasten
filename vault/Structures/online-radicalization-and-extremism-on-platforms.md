# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## The Shape of the Field: From Causes to Catalysts

A sensible place to begin is with [[Rothut2026-wt]], a 25-year scoping review that reframes the entire topic. Rather than asking whether "the internet causes radicalization," it argues the question is poorly formed: online and offline domains are no longer separable, and the internet is better understood as a catalyst operating across macro (societal/discursive), meso (group), and micro (individual) levels. This framing usefully organizes the rest of the corpus, which largely populates the macro and meso levels the review finds comparatively well-studied, while confirming its diagnosis that causal, longitudinal evidence remains scarce — a gap several papers here directly attempt to close.

## Affordances, Not Ideology, as the Unit of Analysis

A recurring move across these papers is to treat platform affordances — rather than ideological content per se — as the primary explanatory variable. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] makes this explicit: tracing the same German far-right actors across five platforms, it shows that connectivity, replicability, and scalability are not fixed platform properties but relational outcomes of design and practice, producing a broadcast arena on Twitter/X, fragmented echo chambers on Instagram, and party-hierarchical structures on Facebook. This affordance-first lens recurs in [[Karo2026-dn]], where jihadist supporters exploit TikTok's recommendation system and keyword-based moderation through audio camouflage, emoji codes, and bait-and-switch editing — tactics the author stresses are not ideology-specific but structural adversarial responses to platform architecture, also observed among anti-vaccine and far-right communities. [[Rieder2026-pp]] extends this logic to deplatforming's limits: Andrew Tate's content persists and even grows post-ban because YouTube's recommendation system structurally outpaces its own moderation, diffusing his influence across thousands of small channels rather than concentrating it in bannable hubs. [[Nangle2026-yo]] adds the affective dimension to this affordance story, showing how Instagram's memetic, humor-driven "ecology" softens far-right ideology through algorithmic proximity rather than confrontation.

Against this, [[Brown2026-br]] offers an important corrective, empirically disentangling algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube and finding no evidence of algorithmic "radicalization pathways," only content rabbit holes and a mild platform-wide conservative tilt. Read alongside [[Kakavand2026-kt]] and [[Karo2026-dn]], this suggests the mechanism of concern may be less an algorithm actively pushing users toward extremes than an ecosystem of affordances that extremist actors learn to instrumentalize — a distinction with real stakes for governance.

## Mainstreaming as Process: Protest, Pandemic, and the Alt-Influence Economy

A second cluster examines mainstreaming as a gradual, infrastructural process rather than sudden conversion. [[Rothut2026-or]] coins "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to describe how Germany's Querdenken movement, via shared anti-elite framing and Telegram's network structure, became entangled with far-right and conspiracist communities without members necessarily holding explicit far-right views — content and connective mechanisms operating somewhat independently of belief. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents a parallel Swedish case, showing how pandemic-era alternative influence networks fuse activist messaging with influencer microcelebrity and monetization, using YouTube as a "sanitized" gateway funneling audiences toward alt-tech platforms when moderated. [[Groebner2026-pc]] scales this transnationally, finding sustained commenter overlap across far-right hyperpartisan news channels in four countries, with US content occupying a centralizing role — evidence that mainstreaming operates not just locally but as an integrated transnational information space. [[Bailard2024-pj]] complicates the mainstreaming narrative by demonstrating a concrete behavioral mechanism connecting online discourse to offline violence: diagnostic and motivational framing (not explicit calls to action) predict Proud Boys' violent mobilization, with a reciprocal cycle linking protest and online solidarity messaging. Together these papers suggest mainstreaming and mobilization are not opposed processes but may feed each other through shared affective and discursive infrastructures — a point [[Marwick2025-vx]] grounds at the individual level, reframing "redpilling" as gradual socialization through accumulated pseudo-evidence rather than sudden conversion, with anti-feminism and Islamophobia operating as socially palatable gateway beliefs.

## State and Quasi-State Actors: Coordination as Infrastructure

A distinct thread addresses coordinated inauthentic behavior as a governance and labor problem rather than a content problem. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] reconstructs the Internet Research Agency's organizational structure from leaked CVs, finding it operated as an ordinary media-labor firm integrated into Russia's PR economy rather than a specialized disinformation unit — a finding that recasts troll farms as a production studies object. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] extend coordination detection to Telegram amplification of Russian/Belarusian state narratives and multilingual Twitter activity around China's COVID protests, respectively, showing social media's "dual-use" capacity for both protest mobilization and repression. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] pushes this further by identifying a subtler mode of digital authoritarianism in Serbia — CIB that mimics authentic grassroots support rather than operating as detectable bot farms — arguing existing detection frameworks are inadequate to such mimicry. [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers a complementary case from the opposite direction: Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-audit accounts produced disproportionate network collapse in the right-leaning cluster, demonstrating that targeted removal of hub accounts can function as an effective "network intervention" — a finding in some tension with [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s account of moderation's limits, suggesting effectiveness may hinge on whether a community's authority structure is centralized (as with AER) or diffuse (as with the Tate-space).

## Frontiers: Generative AI and Underexplored Geographies

The most recent papers push the topic toward generative AI and into geographies beyond the Western, English-language core that [[Rothut2026-wt]] identifies as the field's default focus. [[Crichton2026-kn]] and [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] both examine AI-generated imagery in far-right propaganda, with the former making the striking argument that commercial image models don't neutralize hateful content so much as sanitize its explicit markers while preserving legible ideological cues for in-groups — a structural bias in training data and architecture, not an accident. [[Beacken2026-zb]] widens the comparative lens to six democratically weakened countries, arguing against technologically deterministic narratives: GenAI propaganda adoption is contingent on local political-economic structure, not inevitable uniform diffusion. Meanwhile [[Cabbuag2024-me]] and [[De_Leon2025-qn]] gesture toward underexamined dimensions — Filipino TikTok humor cultures normalizing incivility, and the concept of "networked exposure," whereby ostensibly neutral content (news, Wikipedia) becomes ideologically weaponized through circulation in extremist networks, extending the volume's throughline that it is context, infrastructure, and circulation — not content alone — that determines radicalizing potential.
