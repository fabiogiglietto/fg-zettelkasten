# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Setting the Terrain

[[Rothut2026-wt]] functions as the natural entry point for this collection, offering a 25-year scoping review that maps how "online radicalization" has been conceptualized, operationalized, and studied. Its central argument—that the internet is best understood as a *catalyst* rather than a root cause of radicalization, and that strictly separating online from offline domains is no longer tenable—provides the conceptual scaffolding against which the more granular, platform-specific studies in this set can be read. Its multi-level framework (macro/meso/micro) and its call for more longitudinal, causally-grounded work also anticipates several methodological moves made elsewhere in the corpus, from Granger-causal modeling of Telegram discourse to algorithmic audits of YouTube.

## Platform Affordances as Vectors: Algorithms, Recommendations, and Evasion

A cluster of papers examines how specific platform architectures are actively exploited by extremist actors. [[Karo2026-dn]] provides perhaps the most granular account, cataloguing five distinct communicative strategies—audio camouflage, meme infiltration, blurred intent, emoji codes, and bait-and-switch—that jihadist supporters use to embed propaganda within TikTok's vernacular culture and evade keyword- and image-based moderation. This notion of "everyday extremism," where ideological content is smuggled inside mundane platform genres rather than presented as spectacle, resonates strongly with [[Nangle2026-yo]], which shows far-right actors on Instagram using positive affect, humor, and memetic Reels to "soften" extremist worldviews within an "affective-discursive ecology" shaped by algorithmic proximity. Both papers reject a content-moderation paradigm focused on discrete hateful utterances in favor of attention to vernacular form and platform logic—a critique also voiced explicitly in [[Bailard2024-pj]].

[[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Askanius2026-de]] extend this platform-affordance lens to YouTube, but shift the emphasis toward recommendation-driven circulation and network structure. Rieder's concept of the "Tate-space" demonstrates that a decentralized ecosystem of interviews, remixes, and YouTube-native formats can sustain—and even amplify—a deplatformed figure's ideological influence, because the recommendation system outpaces moderation's ability to remove content. Askanius's mapping of the Swedish "alternative influence network" similarly shows YouTube functioning as a comparatively sanitized gateway that funnels audiences toward more extreme alt-tech spaces, while far-right actors hybridize activist and influencer practices (microcelebrity branding, monetization, coded evasion tactics) to sustain visibility. Notably, [[Brown2026-br]] complicates this algorithmic-amplification narrative: its audit design, which disentangles supply-side algorithmic effects from demand-side user choice, finds no evidence that YouTube's recommender independently drives users along ideologically escalating "radicalization pathways," even as it confirms strong content "rabbit holes" and a platform-wide conservative tilt. Read together, these papers suggest the mechanism of concern may lie less in algorithmically engineered escalation than in the sustained, low-friction *availability* and *normalization* of extremist-adjacent content within otherwise ordinary recommendation flows.

## Mainstreaming and the Blurring of Fringe and Mainstream

Several papers converge on the idea that extremist ideas spread less through direct conversion than through gradual, boundary-shifting *mainstreaming*. [[Rothut2026-or]] coins "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to describe how the German Querdenken movement's anti-elite, conspiratorial Telegram discourse structurally embedded ordinary pandemic sceptics within transnational far-right and QAnon-adjacent networks, even though explicit far-right ideology remained a minority of content. This dynamic of shared grievance as a bridge—rather than explicit ideological commitment—echoes across [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s affective softening on Instagram, [[Askanius2026-de]]'s hybridization of Swedish far-right influencers with wellness and anti-vaccine communities, and [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s "ambient ideology," where Tate's misogynistic and conspiratorial worldview circulates as an atmosphere rather than an explicit doctrine. Collectively these studies push back against a spectacle-centric or singular-actor view of extremist content, arguing that normalization operates through everyday aesthetics, humor, commercial branding, and cross-milieu networking.

## Micro-Level Pathways: Identity, Affect, and the Making of Believers

A second thread turns from platform structure to audience meaning-making. [[Marwick2025-vx]] directly challenges the "exposure equals belief" model implicit in much radicalization research, showing through redpilling narratives on Reddit, Gab, and Discord that adoption of far-right beliefs is typically a *processual* socialization—sustained by disinformation reframed as "evidence" and by a self-image of rational, scientific skepticism. This theme of self-styled epistemic rebellion recurs in [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s study of ConspiracyTok, where young, disproportionately non-white and female creators construct "populist knowledge production" through visual evidence and intimacy, complicating stereotypes of who conspiracy believers and spreaders are. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] extends this identity-centered account across six platforms, showing that "Great Reset" communities cohere less around shared ideology than around symbolic boundary work: reclaiming the stigmatized "conspiracy theorist" label, converting personal hardship into collective grievance, and constructing a "mainstream other." Together these three papers argue that affect, identity, and relational solidarity—not platform algorithms or content exposure alone—are the primary engines sustaining conspiratorial and extremist in-groups, a claim that stands somewhat in tension with the more platform-deterministic accounts above. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]], although focused on influencer-driven brand misinformation rather than ideological extremism per se, adds a complementary mechanism: it shows how source credibility and parasocial bonds can legitimize misinformation and convert follower reactions into homogeneous collective antagonism, a dynamic structurally similar to the community-enmeshment processes described in the explicitly ideological cases.

## From Online Discourse to Offline Action

Where most papers in this set treat online radicalization as a discursive or infrastructural phenomenon, [[Bailard2024-pj]] closes the loop empirically by linking Proud Boys Telegram messaging to real-world violence. Using Granger causality on frame-classified messages, it finds that diagnostic (grievance) framing predicts subsequent offline violence, while motivational (pride/solidarity) framing exists in a reciprocal, roughly four-week cycle with offline protest activity. Notably, explicit calls-to-action (prognostic frames) are *not* predictive—an important corrective to intuitive assumptions about which online content most directly drives mobilization. This finding dovetails with [[Rothut2026-or]]'s account of protest movements as mainstreaming conduits, suggesting a shared underlying pattern: offline collective action and online discursive ecosystems co-evolve, with affective and identity-based framing—rather than explicit incitement—doing much of the mobilizing work.

## Moderation, Deplatforming, and Adversarial/State-Linked Networks

A final cluster addresses governance responses and their limits, alongside more instrumentalized forms of information manipulation. [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers a rare in-situ analysis of deplatforming's network effects, showing that Twitter's targeted suspension of a small number of central accounts in the Arizona Election Review community collapsed its hub/authority structure and shifted discourse from mobilization to abstract defiance—evidence that "network interventions" can outperform simple visibility-reduction approaches. Yet this success is complicated by [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Karo2026-dn]], both of which show moderation structurally outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation and adversarial creativity: Tate's ecosystem persists through diffuse reposting after channel removal, while jihadist supporters adapt in real time to keyword and image filters through platform-vernacular camouflage. Finally, [[Kim2026-br]] broadens the frame from grassroots and influencer-driven extremism to state-linked information operations, using explainable machine learning to detect two decades of suspected North Korea-linked troll activity in South Korean news comments. Its finding that morally condemning (rather than praising) rhetoric achieves disproportionate engagement-based visibility, and that such rhetoric targets political leaders across the ideological spectrum, suggests that polarization-amplification—rather than persuasion toward a specific ideology—can be a deliberate strategic goal, situating extremist and disinformation dynamics within a broader ecology of adversarial online influence that spans state and non-state actors alike.
