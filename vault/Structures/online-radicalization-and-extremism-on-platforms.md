# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping the field: from cause to catalyst

The natural entry point into this cluster is [[Rothut2026-wt]], a scoping review of 382 publications spanning 25 years of internet-radicalization research. It offers the conceptual scaffolding the rest of the corpus implicitly works within: radicalization as an incremental, multi-factor process rather than a single event; the internet as catalyst rather than root cause; and the insistence that online/offline is no longer a tenable analytic boundary. Its call to move beyond content-centric snapshots toward longitudinal, multi-level (macro/meso/micro) analysis is precisely the gap that several other papers in this set try to fill empirically. Its taxonomy—macro discursive shifts, meso group dynamics, micro individual pathways—provides a useful lens for organizing the rest of the collection, which clusters heavily around meso-level group/platform ecosystems and macro-level mainstreaming, with comparatively thinner coverage of individual-level micro processes (though [[Marwick2025-vx]] partially fills this gap).

## From exposure to socialization: rethinking the mechanism of belief change

A crucial corrective to naive "exposure equals radicalization" models comes from [[Marwick2025-vx]], whose qualitative analysis of far-right "redpilling" narratives shows that belief adoption is typically a slow, socially embedded process of accumulating pseudo-evidence and community validation, not a single transformative encounter with extremist content. This reframes disinformation not as an incidental trigger but as constitutive material in an ongoing self-presentation of the believer as a rational, scientific evaluator—a finding that resonates with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s emphasis on multi-factor, recipient-dependent processes and pushes against journalistic "rabbit hole" narratives. This same skepticism about strong causal exposure effects is corroborated at the platform-infrastructure level by [[Brown2026-br]], whose algorithmic audit of YouTube finds no evidence of an independent recommender-driven radicalization pathway, only user-choice-driven mild echo chambers and strong (ideologically neutral) content rabbit holes, alongside a platform-wide moderate-conservative tilt affecting all users. Together these two papers complicate the popular "algorithm radicalizes" thesis, suggesting instead that platform structure shapes *what* is available and *how* it circulates, while socialization processes shape *how* it is metabolized into belief.

## Platform affordances as differentiators of extremist practice

A second major thread treats platform affordances not as background but as the analytic object itself. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] offers the most systematic version of this argument, comparing German far-right networks across five platforms and showing that ostensibly similar connective features (retweets, forwards, shares, mentions) produce structurally distinct networks—broadcast-like scalability on Twitter/X, fragmented echo chambers on Instagram, hierarchical party-driven networks on Facebook, inward-facing fringe density on Telegram. This affordance-relational framework dovetails with [[Karo2026-dn]]'s account of jihadist supporters' exploitation of TikTok's algorithmic and moderation architecture through audio camouflage, meme infiltration, emoji codes, and bait-and-switch tactics—strategies the author frames as "everyday extremism" that collapses spectacle into vernacular participation, and that are explicitly argued to be ideology-agnostic, appearing similarly in far-right and anti-vaccine communities. [[Nangle2026-yo]] extends this logic to Instagram, showing how positive affect, humour, and memetic softening—rather than overt hostility—normalize far-right worldviews within an "affective-discursive ecology" shaped by algorithmic proximity. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] provides a non-Western complement, showing how TikTok's affordances amplify a culturally specific register of "legal-but-harmful" humor (Filipino "dogshows") that blurs entertainment and incivility, underscoring that platform-vernacular normalization is not confined to explicitly ideological far-right or jihadist contexts.

## Networked ecosystems, hybridization, and mainstreaming

A third thread scales up from single actors to entire ecosystems. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents how Swedish far-right YouTubers built a cohesive "alternative influence network" during Covid-19 through guest appearances, hyperlinking, and in-video mentions, fusing influencer self-branding and monetization with political propaganda while using coded language and "phantom videos" to survive moderation—demonstrating hybridization at the level of actors, practices, and content. [[Groebner2026-pc]] pushes this outward transnationally, showing sustained commenter overlap among four English-language far-right hyperpartisan channels (Sky News Australia, Fox News, Rebel News, GB News), with US content occupying the structural center and shared themes (transphobia, anti-immigration) acting as transnational connective tissue. [[Rieder2026-pp]] extends the ecosystem argument to a deplatformed figure, arguing that Andrew Tate's YouTube "ambient ideology" persists and even intensifies post-removal through a decentralized network of interviews, remixes, and memeified formats that recommendation-driven circulation outpaces moderation efforts to contain—directly illustrating [[Karo2026-dn]]'s and [[Kakavand2026-kt]]'s point that removal-based governance is structurally overmatched by platform circulation logics.

This ecosystem lens converges with explicit theories of mainstreaming. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to explain how the German Querdenken movement's anti-elite, conspiratorial framing on Telegram structurally embedded ordinary protest sympathizers within far-right and international conspiracist networks, even though explicit far-right ideology remained rare in raw content terms. [[Bailard2024-pj]] complements this with a rare longitudinal, causal-inference approach to a single organized movement (Proud Boys on Telegram), showing that diagnostic grievance framing and motivational solidarity messaging—not explicit calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, and that a reciprocal online-offline mobilization cycle operates on a roughly four-week rhythm. Both papers push the field, as [[Rothut2026-wt]] recommends, toward treating extremist communication as embedded in dynamic discursive ecosystems rather than discrete moderatable posts.

## State-driven and networked exposure: extending the frame

Two further pieces extend the topic beyond organic/movement-driven extremism. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] shows that authoritarian state actors exploit Telegram's forwarding affordances for coordinated amplification of regime-aligned narratives in Russia and Belarus, a useful reminder that the same platform mechanics enabling grassroots far-right networking (per [[Kakavand2026-kt]]) also serve top-down propaganda. Finally, [[De_Leon2025-qn]] offers a conceptual intervention—"networked exposure"—arguing that even ostensibly neutral content (news articles, Wikipedia pages) becomes politically weaponized once circulated within extremist-aligned networks, a claim that formalizes what several empirical papers here observe in practice: that mainstreaming and radicalization operate as much through circulatory context (hyperlinks, forwards, algorithmic proximity) as through the ideological content of any single artifact.

## Synthesis

Read together, these fourteen papers trace an arc from broad conceptual stock-taking ([[Rothut2026-wt]]) through fine-grained affordance-sensitive platform studies ([[Kakavand2026-kt]], [[Karo2026-dn]], [[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Cabbuag2024-me]]), to ecosystem- and network-level accounts of mainstreaming and hybridization ([[Askanius2026-de]], [[Rothut2026-or]], [[Groebner2026-pc]], [[Rieder2026-pp]], [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]]), and finally to causal/mechanistic scrutiny of how online discourse translates into offline action or belief ([[Bailard2024-pj]], [[Marwick2025-vx]], [[Brown2026-br]]), with [[De_Leon2025-qn]] offering a unifying conceptual vocabulary for the circulatory dynamics common to nearly all of them. The collective thrust is a shift away from moderation-as-content-removal and exposure-as-cause models toward understanding extremism as an emergent property of platform architecture, vernacular creativity, and socially embedded meaning-making—one that requires infrastructural, longitudinal, and networked analytic tools rather than snapshot content audits.
