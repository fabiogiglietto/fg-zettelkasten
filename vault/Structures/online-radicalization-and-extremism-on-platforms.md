# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## The internet as catalyst, not cause

The three papers converge on a foundational claim that reshapes how we should think about online radicalization: the internet is not a discrete "cause" of radicalization but a socio-technical environment that accelerates, textures, and normalizes extremist trajectories. [[Rothut2026-wt]] argues explicitly, on the basis of 25 years and 382 publications, that the online/offline distinction is no longer analytically tenable — radicalization happens in *hybrid* ecologies where information, contact, and infrastructural affordances interact with prior dispositions. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Karo2026-dn]] operationalize this hybrid view empirically, showing that on Instagram and TikTok respectively, extremist content does not sit apart from mainstream digital culture but is stitched into its everyday textures — memes, Reels, trending sounds, and platform vernaculars.

## From spectacle to vernacular: the aesthetic turn

A striking convergence across the two empirical studies is a shift away from the classical image of extremist communication as *spectacle* (beheading videos, manifestos, overt hostility) toward what might be called an aesthetic of normalization. [[Nangle2026-yo]] describes far-right Instagram as an "affective-discursive ecology" where positive affect, humour, and memetic softening do the ideological work that overt hostility once did. [[Karo2026-dn]] identifies a functionally parallel logic for jihadist TikTok, coining "everyday extremism" to describe propaganda embedded in Roblox memes, Inside Out 2 clips, and pitched-shifted nasheeds. Both suggest the mainstreaming of extremism now proceeds through emotional resonance and cultural fluency rather than shock. This dovetails with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s observation that recent scholarship since 2022 has increasingly attended to phenomenologically diffuse forms — misogynist, conspiracy, anti-system — that resist traditional ideological taxonomies.

## Platform affordances and algorithmic proximity

The two case studies also share an infrastructural argument: platform logics are constitutive of extremist mobilization, not merely its passive conduit. [[Nangle2026-yo]] foregrounds "algorithmic proximity" — the way Instagram's recommendation architecture places far-right adjacent content next to mainstream lifestyle content, enabling drift and mobilization. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents how TikTok's affordances are actively gamed through five adversarial strategies (audio camouflage, meme infiltration, blurred intent, emoji codes, bait-and-switch), while noting that trivial textual mutations like "1S1 S" defeat keyword filters. Crucially, [[Karo2026-dn]] observes that these evasion tactics are not ideology-specific, appearing across far-right, anti-vaccine, and jihadist communities — pointing to structural rather than ideological vulnerabilities. This resonates with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s macro-level synthesis, which identifies algorithmic curation and mainstreaming as dominant themes in the literature, though the review also flags that Instagram, TikTok, and alternative platforms remain understudied relative to Twitter/X, YouTube, and Facebook.

## Methodological and evidentiary gaps

Read together, the empirical papers begin to address gaps the review identifies. [[Rothut2026-wt]] notes the scarcity of longitudinal and causal work, the field's reliance on proxies, and its Western/mainstream-platform bias. The two case studies partly redress the platform imbalance — Instagram Reels and TikTok are precisely the visual, algorithmically-driven environments the review flags as underexamined — and both deploy computational or ethnographic approaches that reflect the "empirical turn" [[Rothut2026-wt]] dates to 2018. Neither, however, is longitudinal or causal; both offer rich descriptive typologies of communicative strategy, leaving open questions about *effects* on recipients. Here [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s micro-level synthesis is a needed corrective: single exposures rarely radicalize, and outcomes depend on prior attitudes, identification, and protective factors like media literacy.

## Implications for governance

The papers point toward a shared, if only partially articulated, policy stance. [[Karo2026-dn]] is most explicit, arguing that content-removal paradigms miss the point when evasion is adversarially creative and culturally embedded; the answer lies in *infrastructural* critique and coordinated public-private governance. [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s emphasis on affective softening implies that moderation focused on overt hostility will systematically miss the normalizing register in which contemporary far-right content circulates. [[Rothut2026-wt]] frames this as a call for multi-faceted, multi-level responses that reject monocausal explanations. The through-line: as extremism becomes vernacular, affective, and algorithmically proximate, both research and governance must move from content to infrastructure, and from ideology-specific detection to structural analysis of how platforms produce the conditions for mainstreaming.
