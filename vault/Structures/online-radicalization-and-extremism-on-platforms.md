# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping the Field

[[Rothut2026-wt]] provides the natural entry point into this cluster: a scoping review of 382 publications spanning 25 years, it argues that the internet functions as a catalyst rather than a root cause of radicalization, and that macro-, meso-, and micro-level dynamics must be read together rather than in isolation. Its call to abandon the online/offline binary, and its observation that the field has undergone an "empirical turn" since 2018, is more or less enacted by the twelve empirical studies filed alongside it—each of which supplies granular evidence for one or more of the mechanisms the review only sketches in aggregate.

## Platform Affordances as Exploited Infrastructure

A recurring line of inquiry treats platforms not as neutral pipes but as contested infrastructures that extremist actors learn to instrumentalize. [[Karo2026-dn]] offers the sharpest case: jihadist supporters on TikTok exploit the platform's recommendation system and vernacular culture through five distinct evasion strategies (audio camouflage, meme infiltration, blurred intent, emoji codes, bait-and-switch), producing what the authors call "everyday extremism"—propaganda that hides in plain sight by mimicking ordinary platform genres rather than performing spectacle. [[Rieder2026-pp]] documents a structurally similar dynamic on YouTube: even after Andrew Tate's deplatforming, a decentralized "Tate-space" of interviews, remixes, and native formats sustains and even amplifies his visibility, because the recommendation system outpaces moderation. [[Askanius2026-de]] extends this to a networked, multi-actor scale, showing how Swedish far-right YouTubers weave a cohesive "alternative influence network" through guest appearances, hyperlinking, and coded evasion tactics, fusing influencer-style monetization with propaganda. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] shifts the register to state-aligned rather than fringe actors, showing that Telegram's forwarding architecture is similarly exploited for coordinated amplification by pro-government channels in Russia and Belarus—a reminder that affordance-exploitation is not the exclusive province of non-state extremists. Across these cases, the platform-specific mechanism differs (TikTok's algorithmic vernacular, YouTube's recommendation lag, Telegram's forwarding networks), but the shared finding is that moderation regimes built around discrete content violations are structurally outmatched by circulation dynamics.

## Mainstreaming and the Blurring of Boundaries

A second thread concerns how radical content becomes normalized without necessarily converting audiences outright. [[Nangle2026-yo]] frames far-right Instagram as an "affective-discursive ecology" in which humor and positive affect—rather than hostility—do the ideological work of softening extremist worldviews, aided by algorithmic proximity to mainstream content. [[Rothut2026-or]] develops a parallel but distinct concept, "protest-facilitated mainstreaming," showing through the German Querdenken case how a heterogeneous protest movement became structurally embedded in far-right and conspiracist Telegram networks via shared anti-elite framing rather than explicit ideological convergence. [[Askanius2026-de]] again complements this by showing how the pandemic catalyzed ideological diversification and alliance-building between far-right actors, wellness communities, and anti-vaccine movements. [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s notion of "ambient ideology" belongs in this company too: Tate's worldview circulates through motivational and lifestyle content that requires no explicit endorsement to normalize. Together these papers suggest mainstreaming operates less through direct persuasion than through affective resonance, structural bridging, and the gradual erosion of discursive boundaries—a finding that sits uneasily next to more individualist "exposure" models of radicalization.

## Micro-Level Meaning-Making: Identity, Epistemology, and "Redpilling"

A third set of papers relocates the explanatory burden from platforms and algorithms to the interpretive work users do. [[Marwick2025-vx]] directly challenges the popular "exposure equals belief" model, showing through redpilling narratives on Reddit, Gab, and Discord that extremist belief adoption is typically a slow, socially embedded process in which disinformation is marshaled as "evidence" to construct a self-image of rational, scientific superiority. [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends this epistemological lens to ConspiracyTok, arguing that conspiracy content functions as "populist knowledge production" within a "generous epistemology" that welcomes divergent truths—simultaneously empowering marginalized standpoints and circulating antisemitic or transphobic disinformation, and notably complicating the stereotype of the middle-aged white male conspiracist. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes furthest in this direction, arguing—against techno-centric echo-chamber explanations—that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity (reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor) that is remarkably consistent across six different platforms, suggesting identity formation rather than platform architecture or content per se is the primary driver of cohesion. Read together with [[Marwick2025-vx]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]], this suggests a converging critique: platform-centric and algorithm-centric accounts of radicalization risk overlooking the interpretive, identity-building labor performed by users themselves.

## Contesting the Algorithm: Does Recommendation Really Radicalize?

This body of work sits in productive tension with [[Brown2026-br]], which directly interrogates the "rabbit hole" thesis using an experimental audit design that disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube. Its findings are deflationary: echo chambers are mild and mostly user-driven, rabbit holes (path-dependent recommendation) are real, but there is no evidence of a distinctly *radicalization* pathway pushing users toward escalating extremity matched to partisanship—though the algorithm does exhibit a platform-wide conservative tilt. This complicates the more qualitative, platform-exploitation findings of [[Karo2026-dn]], [[Rieder2026-pp]], and [[Askanius2026-de]]: those studies show extremist actors successfully gaming recommendation systems for visibility, but [[Brown2026-br]] suggests the algorithm itself does not independently manufacture ideological escalation. The synthesis implied across this topic is that algorithms enable circulation and amplification of already-produced extremist content (a supply-side, actor-driven phenomenon) more than they autonomously manufacture radicalized audiences (a demand-side effect)—a distinction [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s "catalyst not cause" framing anticipates.

## From Online Discourse to Offline Violence

Where most of this corpus asks how ideology spreads, [[Bailard2024-pj]] closes the loop by asking whether it *predicts action*. Its Granger-causality analysis of Proud Boys Telegram discourse finds that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity/pride) framing—but not explicit calls-to-action—predict subsequent offline violence, and identifies a reciprocal cycle whereby nonviolent protest boosts motivational messaging, which in turn precedes violent escalation. This is among the few studies in the set offering causal, longitudinal evidence linking online discursive ecosystems to offline mobilization, directly addressing the gap [[Rothut2026-wt]] identifies as most acute in the field.

## Beyond the Far-Right/Jihadist Binary: Comparative and Authoritarian Contexts

Finally, two papers push the topic's geographic and political scope outward. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] reframes "extremist" coordination as a tool of authoritarian regimes rather than only fringe or insurgent movements. [[Beacken2026-zb]] broadens the aperture further still, examining generative-AI-enabled propaganda across six democratically weakened states (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey) and arguing against technologically deterministic accounts—propaganda adoption is shaped by local political, economic, and social structures rather than following a uniform AI-driven trajectory. Both studies function as a corrective to the overwhelmingly Western, far-right/jihadist framing of the rest of the corpus, suggesting that the mechanisms identified here (affordance exploitation, coordinated amplification, algorithmic mainstreaming) are not ideology-specific but recur wherever political actors—state or non-state—seek to weaponize platform infrastructures.

## Synthesis

Read as a whole, this literature traces an arc from infrastructural exploitation (how extremists game specific platforms) through discursive normalization (how radical ideas become mainstream without requiring conversion) to interpretive agency (how users construct rational, affectively satisfying identities around radical belief), while remaining genuinely contested on the question of algorithmic causation itself. The throughline connecting [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s review to the empirical studies is a shared skepticism toward monocausal, exposure-based models of radicalization, in favor of accounts that are processual, multimodal, and irreducibly socio-technical—treating online and offline, algorithm and audience, ideology and affect as mutually constitutive rather than separable variables.
