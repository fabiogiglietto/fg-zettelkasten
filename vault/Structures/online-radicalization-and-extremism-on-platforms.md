# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## The Shape of the Field: From Cause to Ecology

The most fundamental move across this literature is a rejection of the "internet-as-cause" and "exposure-equals-belief" models that dominated early radicalization research. [[Rothut2026-wt]], the field-defining scoping review of 25 years of scholarship, crystallizes this shift: the internet is best understood as a *catalyst* operating within hybrid socio-technical environments, not a discrete causal mechanism, and the online/offline binary is no longer analytically tenable. This reframing licenses the rest of the corpus to ask not "does the internet radicalize?" but "through what platform-specific mechanisms does extremist meaning circulate, normalize, and occasionally trigger action?" [[Marwick2025-vx]] delivers the micro-level correlate of this argument, showing empirically that "redpilling" is rarely a sudden conversion but a *processual* socialization built from cumulative disinformation reframed as rational "evidence"—directly undercutting single-exposure models and echoing Rothut et al.'s call for multi-factor, incremental accounts.

## Affordances and Algorithms: Platform-Specificity as Analytic Lens

A cluster of papers insists that extremist communication cannot be studied platform-agnostically. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] makes this the explicit thesis: the same German far-right actors produce structurally distinct networks on Twitter, Telegram, YouTube, Instagram, and Facebook because affordances like connectivity, replicability, and scalability are relational outcomes of design meeting practice, not fixed properties—Twitter as broadcast arena, Telegram as fringe-dominated echo structure, Facebook as hierarchical party apparatus. This affordance-sensitivity is deepened by studies of algorithmic exploitation on specific platforms. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents how jihadist "everyday extremism" evades TikTok moderation via audio camouflage, meme infiltration, and emoji codes, arguing these adversarial tactics are ideology-agnostic and expose infrastructural rather than merely content-level vulnerabilities. [[Rieder2026-pp]] extends this infrastructural critique to YouTube, showing that Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology" persists post-deplatforming because recommendation-driven circulation structurally outpaces moderation—removal is a symbolic gesture against a logistics problem. [[Nangle2026-yo]] adds the affective dimension: far-right Instagram Reels normalize ideology not through hostility but through humor, positive affect, and "algorithmic proximity," constituting an "affective-discursive ecology" where platform logic and emotion are mutually constitutive.

Yet not all algorithmic claims survive scrutiny. [[Brown2026-br]] provides a crucial methodological corrective, disentangling supply-side (algorithmic) from demand-side (user-choice) effects on YouTube and finding no evidence of algorithmic "radicalization pathways"—only mild, user-driven echo chambers and strong content rabbit holes, alongside a platform-wide conservative tilt affecting all users equally. This paper functions as a check on the more totalizing algorithmic-blame narratives elsewhere in the set, suggesting that infrastructural critique (as in [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]]) must be paired with rigorous causal identification.

## Networks, Hybridization, and Transnational Scale

Several papers scale up from single actors to entire ecosystems. [[Askanius2026-de]] traces the Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube, showing how pandemic-era instability fused activist propaganda with influencer commercial logics—monetization, microcelebrity branding, coded evasion—producing a "hybridization" of extremism across actors, practices, and content. [[Groebner2026-pc]] pushes this outward transnationally, using massive comment-overlap data across four Anglophone hyperpartisan YouTube channels to demonstrate a genuinely integrated transnational far-right audience, with US content occupying a gravitational center. Both papers converge on YouTube as a "gateway" or connective infrastructure that launders extremist content through mainstream-adjacent genres (news, lifestyle, wellness). [[De_Leon2025-qn]] offers a conceptual complement here: its notion of "networked exposure" explains how even ostensibly neutral content (a Wikipedia article, a news story) becomes weaponized once circulated within these ideologically charged networks—theoretically underwriting what Askanius and Groebner observe empirically.

## Mainstreaming as Process

A parallel thread examines how protest and movement dynamics mainstream extremism. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming," showing via the German Querdenken case how anti-elite, conspiratorial framing (rather than explicit ideology) served as the connective tissue drawing moderate protesters into far-right and conspiracist Telegram networks—with prominent far-right figures, not the movement itself, occupying the network's structural center. This dovetails with [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s and [[Askanius2026-de]]'s findings that mainstreaming works through affective and infrastructural means rather than overt persuasion, and it complements [[Marwick2025-vx]]'s claim that anti-feminism, transphobia, and anti-immigration attitudes function as socially acceptable "gateway beliefs."

## From Discourse to Violence

Finally, [[Bailard2024-pj]] closes the loop between online discourse and offline consequence, demonstrating via Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) framing—not explicit calls-to-action—predict subsequent violent mobilization, with a reciprocal four-week cycle linking nonviolent protest, online morale-building, and violence. This finding pressures the content-moderation paradigm critiqued throughout the corpus ([[Karo2026-dn]], [[Rieder2026-pp]]): if violence is predicted by emotional and identity-affirming framing rather than discrete calls-to-action, then platform governance focused on removing explicit incitement will systematically miss the actual mechanisms of mobilization—reinforcing Rothut et al.'s call for theoretically grounded, longitudinal, ecosystem-level analysis over episodic content policing.
