# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping the field: from radicalization theory to platform specifics

[[Rothut2026-wt]] provides the scaffolding for this entire topic, synthesizing 382 studies across 25 years to argue that the internet functions as a *catalyst* rather than a root cause of radicalization, and that the online/offline distinction is no longer analytically defensible. Its call for multi-level (macro/meso/micro) and Lasswellian (communicator/content/medium/recipient) analysis maps neatly onto the papers filed here, which collectively populate every cell of that matrix: platform-specific content strategies (Karo2026-dn, Nangle2026-yo, Bosch2024-hj), meso-level group and network dynamics (Askanius2026-de, Rothut2026-or), and the discursive/affective work of recipients constructing identity (Grusauskaite2026-po, Marwick2025-vx). The review's observation that empirical, computational work has surged since 2018 while causal and longitudinal evidence remains scarce is a useful diagnostic lens for reading the rest of the cluster: most studies here are rich descriptive or correlational accounts of *how* extremist communication operates, with only a few (Bailard2024-pj, Simeone2025-vo, Brown2026-br) attempting to establish directional or causal claims about online-offline linkage or algorithmic effects.

## Platform affordances as tactical terrain

A dominant thread is that extremist and fringe actors do not simply post content but actively engineer it to exploit the specific grammar of each platform. [[Karo2026-dn]] offers the sharpest articulation of this, cataloguing five concrete evasion tactics—audio camouflage, meme infiltration, blurred intent, emoji codes, bait-and-switch—through which jihadist supporters embed propaganda in TikTok's vernacular, reframing extremist content as "everyday" rather than spectacular. This resonates with [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s attention to sound as an under-examined, platform-specific propaganda vector in Ukraine-war disinformation, and with [[Cabbuag2024-me]]'s account of "dogshows," where TikTok's affordances normalize legal-but-harmful humor among Filipino Gen Z influencers. Together these studies argue that TikTok's algorithmic culture (short-form, sound-driven, trend-based) produces distinctive radicalization dynamics that text-centric moderation frameworks—built for platforms like Facebook or Twitter—cannot adequately police.

A parallel argument is made for image-first platforms: [[Nangle2026-yo]] shows how far-right Instagram content relies on positive affect, humour, and memetic softening rather than overt hostility, conceptualizing the result as an "affective-discursive ecology" in which platform logics, ideology, and emotion co-produce mainstreaming. [[Gardam2025-er]] complements this methodologically, arguing that visual-first analytic frameworks are necessary to capture how climate-denial conspiracy narratives operate on Instagram, where text-centric discourse analysis systematically misses the primary communicative channel. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] extends the cross-platform comparative logic explicitly, showing how the Sweden Democrats tailored securitising visual rhetoric differently across Facebook, X, and TikTok—text-laden argument on the legacy platforms, memetic, decontextualized "wall-building" imagery on TikTok aimed at younger voters—demonstrating that platform choice and format are themselves strategic, ideological decisions, not incidental packaging.

## Mainstreaming, hybridization, and the blurring of extreme/mainstream boundaries

Several papers converge on "mainstreaming" as a meso-level process distinct from individual radicalization. [[Rothut2026-or]] coins "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to describe how the Querdenken movement's anti-elite, conspiratorial framing on Telegram became structurally entangled with far-right and international conspiracist networks, even though only a small minority of posts contained explicit far-right ideology—suggesting that shared antagonism toward elites, rather than ideological commitment, is the connective tissue. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents an analogous but more commercially inflected process among Swedish far-right YouTubers, where the pandemic catalyzed hybridization across activists, alt-media, and wellness/conspiracy communities, with influencer techniques (microcelebrity branding, monetization, coded evasion of moderation) fusing political propaganda with platform-native marketing logics. [[Rieder2026-pp]] pushes this argument furthest with the concept of "ambient ideology": even after Andrew Tate's deplatforming, a decentralized "Tate-space" of interviews, remixes, and memefied content sustains his worldview's circulation without requiring explicit endorsement, exposing the limits of moderation systems that target discrete accounts rather than diffuse ecosystems. Read together, these three papers suggest mainstreaming operates less through direct ideological conversion than through structural embedding—networked proximity, shared enemies, and affective/commercial incentives that lower the threshold for encountering extreme content.

## From discourse to action: the online/offline nexus

Two papers directly interrogate the causal link between online discourse and offline mobilization that much of the broader literature assumes but rarely tests. [[Bailard2024-pj]] uses Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data to show that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (solidarity) framing—not explicit calls-to-action—predict subsequent offline violence, and identifies a reciprocal "online messaging–offline action cycle" in which nonviolent protest itself feeds back into online motivational framing. This finding usefully complicates content-moderation paradigms that focus on discrete hateful posts, echoing [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s critique of treating online and offline as separable domains. [[Rothut2026-or]]'s Querdenken case operates in a similar register at the meso level, tracing how protest infrastructure itself becomes a conduit for extremist connection. Both studies model radicalization/mobilization as a temporally extended, ecosystem-level phenomenon rather than a single exposure event—a claim that dovetails with [[Marwick2025-vx]]'s critique, discussed below, of the popular "exposure equals belief" model of online radicalization.

## Identity, affect, and community as radicalizing mechanisms

A distinct cluster foregrounds identity-work and affective solidarity over algorithmic or content-based explanations. [[Marwick2025-vx]] directly challenges the dominant "exposure equals belief" account of redpilling, showing through narrative analysis of Reddit, Gab, and Discord communities that most adherents describe a gradual, socially embedded process of "processual redpilling," in which disinformation functions as "evidence" marshaled to construct a self-image of rational, scientific evaluation. [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends this identity-centered lens to ConspiracyTok, showing that conspiracy content creators—disproportionately young, non-White, and/or female—use platform affordances (greenscreen evidence-collages, AAVE, conspiritual aesthetics) to build intimacy and legitimacy through a "generous epistemology" that can simultaneously empower marginalized standpoints and circulate antisemitic or transphobic disinformation. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] generalizes this insight across six platforms in the "Great Reset" case, arguing that conspiracy cultures cohere through symbolic boundary work—reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label, constructing a "mainstream other," transforming personal hardship into collective grievance—that remains remarkably consistent regardless of platform affordances, explicitly pushing back against techno-centric echo-chamber and algorithmic-radicalization explanations. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]], while centered on brand misinformation rather than political extremism, contributes a structurally similar insight: toxicity and antagonism are source-amplified (driven by influencer credibility and parasocial bonds) rather than purely content-driven, reinforcing the broader claim across this cluster that *who* is speaking and *how community is felt* matter as much as *what* is said.

## Coordination, state actors, and moderation's limits

A smaller set of papers shifts attention to coordinated and state-aligned activity and to the effectiveness of platform interventions. [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] and [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] both use computational coordination-detection methods—on Twitter around China's COVID-19 protests and on Telegram among pro-Kremlin/Belarusian channels, respectively—to show how state and pro-regime actors weaponize the same connective infrastructures that enable protest mobilization, framing platforms as sites of "dual-use" contestation between repression and resistance. On the moderation side, [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers a rare causally-oriented account showing that Twitter's targeted deplatforming of a handful of central accounts collapsed the hub/authority structure of the Arizona Election Review network and shifted discourse from active mobilization to abstract grievance—evidence that network-level interventions can be more effective than content removal alone. This sits in productive tension with [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Karo2026-dn]], both of which show moderation being structurally outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation and adversarial evasion, suggesting that the efficacy of deplatforming may depend heavily on network centralization versus the diffuse, vernacular embedding characteristic of TikTok and YouTube ecosystems.

## The algorithmic question: contested and unsettled

Running beneath much of this literature is the assumption that recommendation algorithms actively drive users toward extremism—an assumption [[Brown2026-br]] directly challenges. Using an audit design that disentangles algorithmic supply-side effects from user-choice demand-side effects, the study finds only mild, user-driven echo-chamber effects, strong "rabbit hole" dynamics (recommendations heavily anchored to the currently watched video), but no evidence of a radicalization pathway that escalates users toward ideological extremity—though it does find a platform-wide algorithmic tilt toward moderately conservative content for everyone. This is a notable methodological counterweight to the largely qualitative and network-analytic claims elsewhere in the cluster (e.g., Rieder2026-pp's "ambient ideology," Nangle2026-yo's "algorithmic proximity," Askanius2026-de's "gateway platform" framing) that treat algorithmic amplification as a taken-for-granted mechanism of radicalization. Read against [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s call for more rigorous causal work, Brown2026-br exemplifies the kind of adjudicating, supply-versus-demand methodology the field needs more of—even as its null result for "radicalization pathways" sits uneasily beside the rich qualitative evidence, elsewhere in this topic, that platform architectures do shape extremist visibility, vernacular, and community formation in consequential ways.
