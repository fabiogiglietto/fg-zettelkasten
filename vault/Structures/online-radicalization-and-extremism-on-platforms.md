# Online Radicalization and Extremism Across Platforms

## Mapping the Field: Definitions, Scope, and Gaps

The natural entry point into this cluster is [[Rothut2026-wt]], a scoping review of 382 publications spanning 25 years of research on internet-facilitated radicalization. It proposes treating the internet as a catalyst rather than a root cause, insists that "online" and "offline" radicalization can no longer be meaningfully separated, and organizes the field along macro/meso/micro levels crossed with Lasswell's communicator–content–medium–recipient scheme. Crucially, it flags persistent limitations that the other papers in this set can be read as attempts to redress: a scarcity of longitudinal and causal designs, an empirical corpus skewed toward a handful of platforms (Twitter/X, YouTube, Facebook, Telegram), and underexamined phenomena like Incel/manosphere and conspiracy-driven radicalization that resist a simple left-right axis. Nearly every other paper here can be located somewhere on this map—as a macro-level content study, a meso-level network study, or a rare attempt at the elusive micro-level, longitudinal, or causal analysis the review calls for.

## Platform Affordances as Differentiators

A recurring argument is that extremist and illiberal actors do not transplant a single strategy across platforms but adapt to platform-specific affordances. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] makes this the explicit object of study, comparing German far-right networks across five platforms and showing that even structurally similar connectors (retweets, forwards, shares) yield distinct topologies—Twitter's sprawling low-reciprocity broadcast arena, Telegram's clustered fringe ecology, Instagram's tightly-knit but fragmented echo chambers, Facebook's hierarchical party-driven structure. This affordance-sensitivity recurs throughout the corpus: [[Karo2026-dn]] catalogs how jihadist supporters exploit TikTok's specific recommendation and moderation architecture (audio, meme, emoji, and visual-obfuscation tactics), while [[Bosch2024-hj]] isolates sound specifically as an underexamined, TikTok-native propaganda channel for Ukraine-war disinformation. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] extends this affordance lens to a non-Western case, showing how TikTok's Filipino Gen Z "dogshow" culture normalizes legal-but-harmful humor as incivility. Together these studies argue against any platform-agnostic theory of radicalization, insisting instead on granular, infrastructure-aware analysis.

## Mainstreaming Through Affect, Humor, and the Everyday

A second thread concerns how extremist content becomes normalized not through shocking spectacle but through affectively pleasant, humorous, or aspirational packaging. [[Nangle2026-yo]] frames far-right Instagram as an "affective-discursive ecology" in which positive affect and memetic humor "soften" ideology for mainstream consumption, while [[Karo2026-dn]]'s notion of "everyday extremism" makes a structurally similar claim for jihadist TikTok content embedded in mundane platform vernacular. [[Rieder2026-pp]] pushes this furthest with the concept of "ambient ideology": Andrew Tate's post-deplatforming YouTube presence persists not through coordinated reposting but through a decentralized ecosystem of interviews, remixes, and memeified formats that normalize his worldview without requiring explicit endorsement—demonstrating that recommendation-driven circulation structurally outpaces moderation. [[Rothut2026-or]] generalizes this dynamic at the level of social movements: analyzing Querdenken's Telegram communication, it proposes "protest-facilitated mainstreaming," in which shared anti-elite framing—rather than explicit ideological commitment—bridges moderate protest publics to entrenched far-right and conspiracist networks, with prominent far-right brokers, not protest organizers, occupying the network's structural center.

## Algorithmic Exploitation and Adversarial Moderation Evasion

Several papers focus squarely on the cat-and-mouse dynamics between extremist actors and platform governance. [[Karo2026-dn]] offers the most granular typology (audio camouflage, meme infiltration, blurred intent, emoji codes, bait-and-switch), arguing these tactics are not ideology-specific but reflect general adversarial creativity against platform moderation infrastructure—a point echoed in [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s finding that YouTube's own recommendation system undermines its removal efforts, and in [[Askanius2026-de]]'s account of Swedish far-right YouTubers using coded language, on-screen URLs, and "phantom videos" to survive deplatforming while reframing moderation itself as evidence of persecution. These studies collectively push back against content-moderation paradigms centered on discrete removable posts, arguing (most explicitly in [[Bailard2024-pj]] and [[Karo2026-dn]]) for infrastructural and ecosystem-level interventions instead.

## From Online Discourse to Offline Action

Where much of the literature stops at describing online normalization, [[Bailard2024-pj]] directly tests the online–offline link, using Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data to show that diagnostic (grievance) and motivational (pride/solidarity) framing—but not explicit prognostic calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, with a reciprocal four-week cycle linking nonviolent protest, motivational messaging, and violent escalation. This finding complicates simpler models of online radicalization as individual belief change; it suggests instead a collective, emotionally-mediated mobilization pathway. [[Marwick2025-vx]] offers a complementary micro-level corrective, showing through "redpilling" narratives that the popular "exposure equals belief" model misrepresents actual pathways into Far-Right belief, which are typically gradual socialization processes in which disinformation is marshaled as "evidence" to sustain a self-image of rational, scientific inquiry—reinforcing [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s call to treat radicalization as incremental and multi-factor rather than a single triggering exposure.

## Networked and Organizational Scale: From Influencer Ecosystems to Party Capture

A further set of papers scales up from individual platforms to transnational and institutional networks. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents a Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube that fuses activist and influencer logics—guest appearances, hyperlinking, monetization—into a cohesive, professionalized ecosystem that also serves as a gateway to more extreme alt-tech platforms. [[Groebner2026-pc]] extends this transnationally, showing sustained commenter overlap among four English-language far-right hyperpartisan news channels across Australia, the US, Canada, and the UK, with US-centric content occupying the network's structural core. [[Bennett2025-xs]] provides the most macro-institutional frame, arguing that "digital surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Proud Boys, Stop the Steal) have reorganized the Republican Party itself around illiberal networks, synthesizing technocentric disinformation research with institutionalist democratic-backsliding theory. Read together, these papers trace an escalating scale of consequence: from platform-level influencer ecosystems, to transnational audience integration, to outright capture of mainstream party institutions—directly extending [[Bailard2024-pj]]'s online-to-offline concern into electoral and constitutional stakes.

## Multimodality Beyond Text

Cutting across these thematic clusters is a shared methodological turn toward multimodal analysis. [[Bosch2024-hj]] isolates sound, Bastos2025-ol isolates the visual self-presentation of troll-farm profile images (finding identifiable, attributable patterns across gender, race, and camera-angle features), and [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Karo2026-dn]] both stress memetic, audiovisual composition over textual content alone. This multimodal turn reflects platforms' own affordances (short video, image, sound) and signals a methodological frontier the field is only beginning to systematize, in line with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s observation that computational and mixed-methods approaches are increasingly displacing purely qualitative or textual analysis.

## The Blurred Edges of Extremism: Coordinated and State-Linked Activity

Finally, a cluster of papers on coordinated inauthentic and state-linked activity sits at the boundary of "extremism" proper, illustrating how the affordances exploited by ideological movements are equally exploited by state and quasi-state actors. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] documents coordinated forwarding among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus; [[Kim2026-br]] develops an explainable ML framework to detect two decades of suspected state-linked "condemning" rhetoric in Korean news comments, showing that morally condemning (rather than praising) content is disproportionately amplified by engagement-based ranking; and Bastos2025-ol probes visual signatures of troll farms across a massive cross-platform takedown dataset. These studies extend the volume's central concerns—platform affordance, algorithmic amplification, coordination—into adjacent authoritarian and geopolitical contexts, suggesting that the infrastructural vulnerabilities exploited by far-right and jihadist actors are general features of networked platforms rather than idiosyncrasies of any single ideological movement.
