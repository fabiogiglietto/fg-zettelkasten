# Platform Affordances & Audience Engagement

## The affective layer: reactions as engagement infrastructure

The most literal expression of this topic's concern with platform mechanics is the Facebook "Reactions" button itself. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] synthesises 64 studies of this feature and establishes the baseline finding that structures much of what follows: Reactions are paralinguistic affordances that carry emotional meaning at near-zero cognitive cost, and negative valence—especially "Angry"—reliably out-mobilises positive valence in political contexts. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] operationalises this claim empirically across three Scandinavian elections, showing that populist parties' anger-inducing posts convert into shares far more efficiently than mainstream parties' gratitude-inducing "Love" posts, effectively demonstrating the review's central hypothesis with country-comparative granularity and identifying an important boundary condition (Sweden's more balanced emotional economy). [[Xue2025-bp]] extends the emotion-engagement nexus into a genre that should, normatively, resist it: fact-checking. That even corrective content is emotionally inflected—and that this inflection shapes both engagement and audience sentiment toward the fact-checked target—suggests the reaction economy is not confined to overtly partisan content but colonises adjacent genres built on claims to objectivity. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] supplies a useful counterpoint from a decade earlier: its account of "Liking" as a low-cost relational signal within networked intimacy shows how the same lightweight affordance later systematised into Reactions originally served interpersonal bonding rather than mobilisation, framing the shift from sociality to affect-as-metric as a longer platform trajectory rather than a single design decision.

## Facebook as active curator: algorithmic governance and journalistic quality signals

A second cluster interrogates whether Facebook's News Feed is a neutral conduit or an interventionist gatekeeper, and the arc here moves from cautious null results toward increasingly assertive claims of algorithmic agency. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] is the foundational, comparatively modest finding: individual click behaviour, not the ranking algorithm, is the primary suppressor of cross-cutting exposure. [[Allcott2025-jb]] and the broader Meta-collaborative research tradition it belongs to similarly report null effects—removing political ads for six weeks moved no attitudes—reinforcing a picture of a fairly inert platform vis-à-vis political persuasion. Against this backdrop, [[McNally2025-dn]] and especially [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] push back forcefully. McNally's decade-long time-series analysis shows that Facebook's News Feed algorithm treats hard news categorically differently from soft content, with detectable ~20-day lagged effects traceable to specific ranking updates—directly rebutting the "black box" framing that underwrites null-effect studies. Giglietto goes further, showing that the very relationship between sharing and viewing—the mechanism Bakshy and Allcott take largely for granted—is itself volatile and governed: partisan-audience content is systematically penalised in reach while journalistically high-quality content is rewarded, and both effects intensify sharply around known "break the glass" interventions (the 2020 election, COVID). Read together, these two papers argue that the null results from platform-cooperative studies may reflect stable governance regimes at the moment of measurement rather than the absence of governance altogether—an important methodological caution for the whole Meta-collaboration research programme. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] extend this governance question outward and downward: Rossi's cross-national replication of Guess et al. finds that even as untrustworthy URL *shares* rise in Germany and Italy during election years, *views* of untrustworthy content remain comparatively stable—consistent with Giglietto's claim of an active dampening mechanism—while Ducci shows that gatekeeping now operates one step upstream of Facebook entirely, in Google News' aggregation choices, which then interact with Facebook's engagement logics (celebrity and malpractice stories draw comments; technical health stories draw shares) to shape the public health agenda.

## Beyond Facebook: algorithmic feeds reshaping the wider platform ecology

A parallel body of work generalises the governance question to platforms whose architecture differs sharply from Facebook's friend-graph model, and in doing so clarifies what is platform-specific versus general about algorithmic curation. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] both audit X/Twitter's post-Musk "For You" feed and, like Giglietto2026, find that algorithmic effects are neither neutral nor stable: Gauthier's field experiment shows the algorithm causally shifts users rightward by reshaping *who they follow*—a persistence mechanism that helps reconcile Twitter/X findings with the earlier Meta null results—while Efstratiou shows that apparent partisan bias substantially dissolves once one controls for "agitating" content and proximity to Elon Musk himself, relocating political bias in visibility to platform-owner-mediated incentive structures rather than ideology per se. [[Brady2026-ln]] takes this diagnostic work a step further into intervention: its Bluesky field experiment demonstrates that engagement-based ranking amplifies moralised, emotional, and toxic content and distorts *perceived social norms* around political discourse, but that a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can suppress this without sacrificing platform enjoyment—offering the collection's clearest evidence that governance effects documented descriptively elsewhere (McNally, Giglietto, Gauthier) are, in principle, engineerable. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] and [[Boyd2026-op]] supply the macro-theoretical scaffolding for why this matters: Gerbaudo's distinction between "networked publics" (friend-graph, people-centric, explicit) and "clustered publics" (interest-graph, item-centric, implicit) explains why TikTok-style platforms produce qualitatively different visibility dynamics than Facebook's Reactions-and-shares economy, while boyd's provocation that "social media" has become "parasocial media" names the same underlying shift from reciprocal exchange to algorithmically curated, professionally produced consumption that Facebook-focused papers document empirically (declining posting, feed dominance of processed content) without necessarily theorising.

## Platform vernaculars: how actors adapt content to affordances

A third, more actor-centred cluster asks how political, commercial, and cultural actors strategically read and exploit platform affordances rather than merely being shaped by them. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] offers the clearest demonstration: the Sweden Democrats deliberately shift from argumentative text on Facebook and X to memetic, wall-building visuals on TikTok, prioritising a smaller follower base there precisely because TikTok's affordances (For You distribution, remixable templates, hashtag virality) reach younger audiences more effectively than raw follower counts would predict—directly instantiating Gerbaudo's "clustered publics" thesis in a campaign setting. [[Lukito2026-il]] documents a parallel logic among right-leaning US outlets curating distinct story packages for Twitter versus Truth Social, and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] shows that ideologically opposed Canadian TikTok users nonetheless deploy the platform's affordances (sounds, hashtags, humour) in symmetrical ways, suggesting a shared vernacular grammar beneath divergent political content. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]] extend this to more insidious content: far-right Instagram Reels and "ConspiracyTok" videos alike exploit positive affect, humour, and identity-legible aesthetics to soften extremist or fringe claims into algorithmically favoured, mainstream-adjacent content, echoing Anwar's finding that positive-valence framing can itself be strategically weaponised, not merely a "safe" register. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] and [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] show the underside of engagement optimisation: Philippine TikTok "dogshows" normalise legal-but-harmful humour as an affordance-driven incivility practice, while influencer-sourced misinformation generates qualitatively distinct, more homogeneous toxicity than identical claims from ordinary users—because influencer credibility and parasocial bonds convert audience hostility into "brand-legitimated" collective antagonism, a mechanism that complicates any simple valence account of engagement (cf. Kalsnes, Anwar) by foregrounding *source* as an independent driver. [[Tang2026-gu]] and [[Volpe2026-um]] round out this cluster by showing how creators internalise these affordance pressures into labour practice: Chinese "AI slop" producers engineer virality as a manageable production process, while Italian micro-influencers frame algorithmically necessary, low-cost content niches (second-hand fashion) as ethical care work—both cases illustrating that content strategy is inseparable from platform-imposed production constraints.

## Audience epistemics, literacy, and the limits of cross-platform generalisation

A final set of papers steps back to ask what audiences actually do with, understand about, and move between these affordance regimes, and collectively cautions against treating any single platform's dynamics as representative. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s review of 169 studies finds "algorithm literacy" itself conceptually fragmented, while [[Wedel2026-ix]] shows young audiences construct bespoke, platform-specific notions of "informativeness" that only partially map onto journalistic quality signals like those Giglietto and McNally treat as objectively measurable (NewsGuard scores, hard-news categorisation)—implying a gap between what platforms reward as "quality" and what audiences experientially value. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends the quality-signal question to search and AI Overviews, finding uneven algorithmic guardrails against conspiratorial queries that mirror the inconsistent governance documented for feeds. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] offers a more optimistic counter-note: a scalable Instagram "prebunking" ad campaign durably improved users' ability to detect emotional manipulation, suggesting affordances (ad units, poll stickers) can be repurposed for literacy interventions rather than only exploited for engagement. [[Graham2026-fb]] and [[Dehghan2026-sy]] complicate binary assumptions about platform content and community: Reddit's "botting" phenomenon dissolves the human/bot distinction into a spectrum of culturally negotiated authenticity, while Reddit's political subreddits show that even discursively resonant communities maintain minimal cross-ideological overlap, arguing that polarisation is "sedimented" through an entanglement of moderation, design, and discourse rather than reducible to any single affordance. [[Mosleh2024-op]], [[Zhu2026-tn]], and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] press the comparative point further, showing that engagement with partisan and low-quality news, and the structural position of alternative media, vary meaningfully across seven platforms and six national media systems—directly warning against generalising from Facebook- or Twitter-centric findings (the bulk of this topic's literature) to the platform ecology as a whole. [[Wang2026-ub]] supplies a migration case study reinforcing the same caution: even highly motivated academic users largely failed to sustain a move from Twitter to Mastodon, with retention hinging on affordances like discoverability and topic-specific servers rather than network loyalty alone. [[Smith2025-kc]]'s "attention broker" mechanism, whereby influential accounts' amplification causally accelerates follower tie-formation, and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s earlier account of narrow, TV-anchored second-screen participation on Italian political Twitter, both remind us that affordance effects on visibility are historically contingent and mechanism-specific rather than fixed platform properties—closing the loop back to this topic's central question of how mechanics, far from being neutral scaffolding, actively co-produce what audiences see, feel, and believe they are seeing.
