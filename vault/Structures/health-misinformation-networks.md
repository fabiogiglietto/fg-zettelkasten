# Health Misinformation & Coordinated Networks

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster traces problematic health information — chiefly COVID-19 vaccine discourse — as it moves through three analytically distinct but empirically entangled layers: the psychological conditions under which individuals form and update misbeliefs, the structural properties of the online information ecosystem that make some moments and some populations more vulnerable than others, and the coordinated behavioral infrastructures that produce and amplify that content at scale. Read together, the papers argue that "misinformation" cannot be treated as an undifferentiated stream: its causes, carriers, and consequences differ by media type, national political culture, and platform architecture, and effective mitigation requires matching the right diagnostic tool to the right layer of the problem.

## Who is exposed, and why

Two papers anchor the individual-level story using web-tracking data linked to survey measures. [[Adam2026-tz]] shows that conspiracy belief formation during the pandemic's outbreak was never simply a matter of exposure; it was a "marriage" of media content and preexisting predispositions. Populists and the politically mistrustful did not merely consume more conspiracy content — they selectively sought out alternative media that reinforced their views and avoided mainstream debunking, and when debunking did reach them it sometimes backfired into counterarguing rather than correction. Notably, even mainstream quality media circulated more conspiracy-supporting than conspiracy-opposing content, complicating any simple alternative-media-versus-mainstream-media morality tale.

[[Lyons2026-ca]] extends this logic from political predispositions to demographic vulnerability, and from conspiracy belief to concrete health behavior. Using passive web and YouTube tracking, it finds that low-credibility health content exposure is rare in aggregate but extremely concentrated — the top 10% of users account for over three-quarters of all exposure — and that this concentration falls disproportionately on adults 60 and older, independent of their overall health-browsing volume. Crucially, this exposure is not driven by search engines or social media referral but by habitual cross-domain consumption: users move from one low-credibility site to another, and low-credibility health consumption correlates with dubious political news consumption, poor headline discernment, and conspiracist worldviews. Together, [[Adam2026-tz]] and [[Lyons2026-ca]] suggest that vulnerability to health misinformation is less about incidental algorithmic exposure than about durable orientations — political distrust, discernment deficits, age-linked media habits — that shape which corners of the information environment a person habitually inhabits.

## Detecting and modeling systemic vulnerability

Where the previous two papers ask *who* is exposed, [[Scalco2026-bd]] asks *when and where the ecosystem itself becomes vulnerable*. It operationalizes "information voids" as measurable imbalances between public demand (Wikipedia views, Google Trends) and credible supply (Facebook, Twitter, GDELT), showing that such voids cluster around major vaccine-related events (EMA authorizations, the AstraZeneca suspension) and are structurally associated with reduced circulation of high-credibility content and a corresponding rise in low-credibility content. This reframes misinformation prevalence not as a fixed property of platforms or audiences but as an emergent, time-varying property of supply-demand mismatch — a diagnostic layer that could, in principle, explain the very moments in which the individual-level dynamics described by [[Adam2026-tz]] and [[Lyons2026-ca]] are most likely to bite.

[[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes the systemic question further, from correlational description to causal, real-world consequence. By embedding geolocated antivaccine tweet exposure into a compartmental SIRVA epidemic model fit to county-level US data, it estimates a lower-bound causal chain from antivaccine content to vaccine refusal to excess cases and deaths. This paper is the volume's clearest bridge between online discourse and offline public-health harm, giving empirical teeth to the concern — implicit throughout the other papers — that circulation dynamics are not merely a communication problem but a mortality problem.

## Coordination as an organizing behavioral signature

The remaining three papers shift the lens from exposure and epidemiological consequence to the *production side*: how problematic health content is deliberately organized and amplified by coordinated actor networks, primarily via Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) on Facebook. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] establishes the methodological backbone, documenting how an Italian covid-skeptic coordinated network emerged from preexisting political clusters and adapted tactics (link laundering, first-comment link placement, exploitation of religious groups) to evade platform enforcement — and demonstrating that CLSB is a reliable, content-agnostic signal fact-checkers can use to get ahead of the infodemic rather than chase it.

[[Marino2023-9137f448]] applies this coordinated-actor lens to the influencer layer, showing that an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of alternative influencers generates disproportionately high, share-driven engagement, and — importantly — that legacy media and mainstream journalists remain central nodes in circulating this content through remediation, recycling expert soundbites from TV and print into shareable, conspiracy-adjacent packages. This complicates any clean separation between "mainstream" and "alternative" media, echoing the finding in [[Adam2026-tz]] that mainstream sources are themselves conduits, not just correctives.

[[Song2025-yh]] closes the arc by comparing pro- and anti-vaccine coordinated communities across the UK and US, showing that CLSB itself is ideologically agnostic — it can scale institutional public-health messaging (NHS, CDC) just as readily as it can scale unverifiable anti-vaccine claims — and that its content and framing are shaped by national political culture (safety-focused skepticism in the UK versus freedom-focused resistance in the US). This is a useful corrective to any assumption that coordination itself is inherently nefarious; the diagnostic question, as in [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]], is not whether actors coordinate but what they coordinate to say and how credible that content is.

## Synthesis

Across the seven papers, a layered model of the health-misinformation problem emerges: predispositions and habitual consumption patterns determine who is vulnerable ([[Adam2026-tz]], [[Lyons2026-ca]]); systemic supply-demand imbalances determine when and where that vulnerability is most exploitable ([[Scalco2026-bd]]); coordinated actor networks determine how problematic content is manufactured and pushed through the mainstream/alternative media hybrid ([[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]], [[Marino2023-9137f448]], [[Song2025-yh]]); and causal epidemiological modeling shows what is ultimately at stake when these mechanisms succeed ([[Bollenbacher2026-vz]]). The throughline is methodological as much as thematic: each paper insists that health misinformation must be measured behaviorally and longitudinally — via tracking, network analysis, or epidemic modeling — rather than assumed from static content audits, and that interventions (fact-checking, platform moderation, media literacy) must be targeted to the specific layer, actor, and national context in which circulation actually occurs.
