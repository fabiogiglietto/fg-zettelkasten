# Health Misinformation Networks

## From Belief Formation to Network Mapping

The papers in this collection trace an arc from *why* people come to hold health misinformation beliefs, through *how* problematic content is organized and amplified at scale, to *whether* any of it actually changes real-world outcomes. [[Adam2026-tz]] anchors the belief-formation end of this arc, showing with panel-plus-web-tracking data that COVID-19 conspiracy belief is not simply "exposure causes belief" but a joint product of predispositions (populism, political mistrust) and differentiated media diets: alternative media produce contagion, mainstream media produce mitigation, but populists selectively engage with the former and avoid (or counterargue against) the latter. This individual-level, psychologically grounded account sets up a question the rest of the cluster answers from the supply side: where does the conspiracy-supporting content circulating in those diets actually come from, and who is organizing its spread?

## Mapping Coordinated Networks

A strong methodological throughline is the use of coordination signals—rather than content classification alone—to surface "nefarious" actors. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] is the clearest instance: building on the CooRnet Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) framework, it documents the emergence of an Italian covid-skeptic coordinated network, the tactics used to dodge platform moderation (link laundering, first-comment links, religious-group infiltration), and demonstrates that a content-agnostic coordination detector can outperform routine fact-checking in surfacing problematic material—directly operationalizing the "fact-checking acceleration" theme. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this corpus by zooming into the *content* circulated by these coordinated Italian accounts, identifying an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of alternative influencers whose posts achieve outsized share-driven engagement, and showing that legacy media and mainstream journalists remain the central relay nodes—evidence that hybrid remediation, not purely fringe actors, powers amplification. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] finds a structurally similar pattern on Twitter/X and in US news: a coordinated retweet network that is overwhelmingly contrarian, amplifying a narrow set of credentialed anti-consensus experts, with news coverage typically *following* rather than leading superspreader activity—reinforcing across platforms and countries the finding that coordination campaigns target real, credentialed dissenting voices rather than inventing fictitious ones. [[Song2025-yh]] complicates the "coordination = malicious" assumption directly, comparing pro- and anti-vaccine CLSB on Facebook in the UK and US: coordination itself is ideologically neutral, deployed as effectively by public-health institutions as by anti-vaccine communities, with national political culture (safety skepticism in the UK vs. individual-freedom framing in the US) shaping narrative content more than the coordination mechanism itself.

## Detecting Voids and Quantifying Exposure

Complementing network-mapping approaches, [[Scalco2026-bd]] introduces a demand-supply framework for detecting "information voids"—periods where reliable content fails to meet public information demand—and shows empirically that these voids correlate with reduced credible content share and increased misinformation prevalence around key vaccine events, offering a macro-level early-warning signal that could feed into the kind of rapid-response fact-checking infrastructure built in [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]]. At the individual-exposure level, [[Lyons2026-ca]] shifts attention from coordinated producers to consumers, finding that exposure to low-credibility health content is rare in aggregate but highly concentrated among older adults with poorer discernment and more conspiracist worldviews, and—crucially—driven by habitual cross-domain browsing (other low-credibility sites) rather than social media or search referrals. This finding tempers claims about social-platform-driven contagion and dovetails with Ducci2022-10cb5d70's gatekeeping study of Google News Italia, which shows that "emotional" or "popular" health topics (celebrity, malpractice) generate disproportionate engagement relative to their prevalence, even when aggregators prioritize medical-scientific content—suggesting that engagement-driven circulation dynamics, not just coordinated malice, help concentrate attention on sensational health narratives.

## From Networks to Measured Harm

The final analytic move in this cluster is causal: having mapped who spreads what, several papers ask how much it matters. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] develops a compartmental epidemic model (SIRVA) fit to county-level data linking geolocated antivaccine tweets to reduced vaccination rates, cases, and deaths, offering a rare causally-framed bridge from online speech to offline epidemic outcomes, albeit for a modest estimated toll. [[Allen2024-av]] delivers a more deflationary verdict at Facebook scale: flagged misinformation is more persuasive per view but reaches so few people that unflagged, "vaccine-skeptical" content from mainstream outlets drove roughly 46 times more aggregate harm—reframing the infodemic narrative away from fringe coordinated networks and toward the vastly larger, legitimized media ecosystem. Read alongside [[Marino2023-9137f448]] and Efstratiou2026-ij, this suggests a consistent pattern across the cluster: the most consequential vectors for health misinformation are not isolated bot farms but mainstream and quasi-mainstream media acting as amplifiers and legitimizers of fringe claims.

## Politicization and the Limits of "Misinformation"

Finally, [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] serves as a useful counterpoint to the rest of the cluster by examining a case where the "problematic" content is not misinformation per se but politically polarized *selection* among legitimate scientists (Great Barrington Declaration vs. John Snow Memorandum signatories). Its finding—that UK newspapers sort scientists by political alignment while the scientists themselves remain mainstream and interconnected only within ideological camps—cautions against collapsing all contested health communication into the misinformation/coordination frame used elsewhere in this collection, and highlights that media polarization in science coverage can operate through selective legitimate sourcing rather than through fabricated content, coordinated inauthenticity, or algorithmic amplification.
