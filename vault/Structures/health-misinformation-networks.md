# Health Misinformation & Problematic Content Networks

## The Italian testbed: gatekeepers, coordinated networks, and alternative influence

The core empirical program under this topic begins in Italy and traces a single pipeline: from the algorithmic gatekeeping of news aggregation, through coordinated amplification on Facebook, to the alternative-influencer ecosystems that exploit both. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] establishes the upstream layer, showing that Google News Italia's selection of health stories already skews the public agenda toward "emotional" or "popular" topics (celebrity health scandals, malpractice) that generate disproportionate Facebook engagement relative to their prevalence—evidence that platform gatekeeping, not just user behavior, shapes what health content is available to become misinformation in the first place. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] picks up the story on the demand/circulation side: the MINE-FACTS project operationalizes Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) via the CooRnet methodology as a content-agnostic signal for surfacing problematic COVID-19 content faster than manual fact-checking, documenting how an Italian covid-skeptic coordinated network emerged from pre-existing political clusters and continually adapted its tactics (link laundering, first-comment link placement, religious-group infiltration) to evade enforcement. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] then zooms into the *content* of that same coordinated network, showing that an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of alternative influencers—despite low post volume—achieves outsized, shares-driven engagement, and crucially that legacy journalists and right-leaning newspapers remain the central nodes recirculating covid-skeptic voices. Read together, these three papers form a coherent argument: gatekeepers curate a supply of health content (Ducci2022), coordinated networks exploit and amplify it (Giglietto2022), and alternative influence networks convert that amplification into durable, high-engagement counter-narratives that are laundered back through mainstream media remediation (Marino2023). Marino's finding that "hybridity" operates through remediation of TV/newspaper expert statements rather than pure fringe production directly echoes and extends Ducci's gatekeeping argument, closing a loop between algorithmic curation and coordinated inauthentic amplification.

## Coordination as a portable, ideologically agnostic mechanism

A second cluster generalizes the CLSB/coordination lens beyond Italy and beyond Facebook, testing whether the mechanisms found in the MINE-FACTS work travel across platforms and political contexts. [[Song2025-yh]] applies CLSB analysis to UK and US Facebook vaccine discourse and finds that coordination is not intrinsically malicious—pro-vaccine communities coordinate just as densely as anti-vaccine ones, with national political culture (safety skepticism in the UK vs. individual-freedom framing in the US) determining content rather than the coordination mechanism itself, a useful counterpoint to Giglietto2022's more one-sidedly "nefarious" framing. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] shifts the platform to Twitter and the object to scientific-paper circulation, identifying an almost entirely contrarian coordinated network (96.4%) that amplifies a narrow set of credentialed anti-consensus experts—functionally the Twitter-scientific analogue of Marino's IDW cluster—while showing that mainstream news coverage typically *follows* rather than precedes superspreader activity, reinforcing the pipeline logic of legacy-media remediation seen in the Italian work. [[Kansaon2025-id]] pushes coordination analysis into the encrypted, closed ecosystem of WhatsApp in Brazil, demonstrating that the same "identical-message-in-short-window" coordination signature used by CooRnet-style tools can be adapted to closed platforms, and—unlike the largely discursive Italian and UK/US cases—ties coordinated misinformation directly to offline mobilization (doxxing of Supreme Court justices, the January 8 protests). Across this trio, coordination emerges as a portable behavioral signature rather than a platform-specific artifact, but its consequences range from discourse framing (Song2025) to expert delegitimization (Efstratiou2026) to real-world political violence (Kansaon2025).

## From network structure to individual exposure, belief, and behavior

A third line of inquiry asks what coordinated and gatekept content actually does to audiences, moving from network-level description toward causal or quasi-causal claims about belief and behavior. [[Adam2026-tz]] combines panel survey and web-tracking data to show that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of media exposure and predispositions: alternative media exposure produces contagion effects while mainstream media exposure produces mitigation, but populists selectively engage with the former and avoid (or backfire-react to) the latter—a psychological mechanism that helps explain *why* the alternative-influence and coordinated networks documented in Marino2023 and Efstratiou2026-ij find a receptive audience. [[Lyons2026-ca]] narrows the exposure question to low-credibility *health* content specifically, using passive web/YouTube tracking to show that such exposure is rare in aggregate but heavily concentrated among older, more conspiracist, cross-domain consumers—and, notably, that exposure pathways run through other low-credibility sites rather than search or social media referral, a finding that complicates platform-gatekeeper-centric accounts like Ducci2022's. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] takes the furthest step toward causal inference, embedding geolocated antivaccine tweet exposure into a compartmental SIRVA epidemic model to estimate that antivaccine Twitter content caused roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and measurable excess cases and deaths—translating the discourse-level dynamics traced by Efstratiou2026-ij and Song2025-yh into quantified public-health harm.

## Measuring the ecosystem and its polarized supply

A methodological subthread asks how to detect and quantify the structural conditions that make coordinated misinformation and alternative influence effective in the first place. [[Scalco2026-bd]] formalizes "information voids"—anomalous mismatches between demand (Wikipedia views, Google Trends) and supply (Facebook, Twitter, GDELT news)—showing across six European countries that voids around major vaccine events correlate with reduced high-credibility content and increased misinformation prevalence, giving a quantitative, mechanistic complement to the largely qualitative gatekeeping account in Ducci2022-10cb5d70. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] examines a related but distinct form of imbalance: not fringe misinformation but partisan skew in how UK newspapers cite *legitimate* scientists (Great Barrington vs. John Snow Memorandum signatories), showing that even mainstream science coverage bifurcates along political lines and that the two scientific camps form separate co-authorship and Twitter-sharing bubbles. This paper is a useful boundary case for the topic: it shows that the "alternative vs. mainstream" polarization documented throughout the coordinated-network literature (Marino2023, Efstratiou2026) can occur even without anyone crossing into misinformation, underscoring that politicized gatekeeping of legitimate expertise is a parallel, not identical, phenomenon to the coordinated amplification of problematic content.

## The affective residue: toxicity as a downstream signature

Finally, [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] examines what happens after misinformation is consumed, showing that influencer-sourced brand-related misinformation generates more frequent and more homogeneous toxic audience reactions than identical content from regular users, driven by credibility-as-legitimation and parasocial "community enmeshment" mechanisms. This finding dovetails with the AIN/IDW pattern identified in Marino2023-9137f448—where influencer status converts into disproportionate share-driven engagement—by showing that the same credibility-and-parasociality mechanism that amplifies reach also curdles into toxic, echo-chamber-like hostility, offering a plausible affective mechanism underlying the selective engagement and backfire effects modeled by Adam2026-tz. Taken together, the twelve papers trace a full arc: gatekeepers and voids shape what health information is available (Ducci2022, Scalco2026), coordinated networks and alternative influencers exploit and amplify it across platforms and countries (Giglietto2022, Marino2023, Efstratiou2026, Song2025, Kansaon2025), politicized framing polarizes even legitimate expertise (Ghezzi2023), and the resulting exposure translates into belief change, toxic discourse, and measurable public-health harm (Adam2026, Lyons2026, Di-Domenico2026, Bollenbacher2026).
