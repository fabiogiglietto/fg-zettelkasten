# Health Misinformation Networks

## Structure Note: Health Misinformation Networks

The papers filed here trace a single inquiry across three registers: who is exposed to problematic health content and why, how that content is coordinated and amplified by networked actors and gatekeepers, and what measurable real-world consequences follow. Read together, they move from individual-level susceptibility toward increasingly structural, network- and platform-level accounts of the "infodemic," before closing the loop with causal estimates of downstream harm. While the topic brief gestures toward ethnic/religious cleavages in India and Nigeria, the actual corpus is heavily anchored in Italy (Facebook coordination and gatekeeping), with comparative extensions to the US, UK, and German-speaking Europe — a reminder that the mechanisms studied (coordinated amplification, gatekeeping, demand–supply imbalance) are argued to generalize beyond any single national or confessional context, even as religious/political community infrastructure repeatedly surfaces as a vector.

### Who is exposed, and why it matters

[[Adam2026-tz]] and [[Lyons2026-ca]] anchor the demand side of the ecosystem. Adam et al. combine panel survey and web-tracking data in Germany/Switzerland to show that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of media diet and predisposition: populists and the politically mistrustful selectively engage with conspiracy-supporting alternative media and avoid mainstream debunking, or else experience backfire when they do encounter it. Crucially, even mainstream outlets carried substantial conspiracy-supporting content, decoupling source quality from content valence. Lyons et al., working with US web-tracking and YouTube histories, complicate the picture further: exposure to low-credibility health content is rare in aggregate but extremely concentrated (top 10% of users account for 77% of exposure), skews toward older adults with poorer discernment and stronger conspiracist worldviews, and travels through habitual cross-domain referral networks (other low-credibility or shopping sites) rather than through search or social media referrals — directly challenging assumptions, common in the coordination literature below, that social platforms are the primary exposure vector. Together these two papers establish that the "who" of exposure is not simply "everyone," but a structured minority whose media habits already predispose them toward problematic content, with older users emerging as a health-specific risk group even where general political-misinformation literatures might not predict it.

### Coordinated amplification and the network architecture of problematic content

The Italian-centered cluster — [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]], [[Marino2023-9137f448]], and comparatively [[Song2025-yh]] and [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] — shifts from individual susceptibility to the supply-side architecture of coordination. Giglietto et al.'s MINE-FACTS project uses Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) and the CooRnet methodology to map an Italian covid-skeptic network that emerged from pre-existing political clusters (League, Five Star Movement) and evolved evasive tactics — link laundering through reputable outlets, first-comment link placement, and infiltration of Catholic community groups — that let it evade platform enforcement while a content-agnostic behavioral signal (CLSB, comments-to-shares ratio) still outperforms routine fact-checking at surfacing problematic content. Marino et al. extends this same Italian dataset "upward" into the influencer layer, showing that an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of micro-celebrities generates disproportionate share-driven engagement, and — echoing Giglietto's link-laundering finding — that legacy journalists and right-leaning newspapers remain the central nodes recirculating and thereby laundering covid-skeptic claims via remediation of TV and press statements. Song et al. broadens the comparative lens to UK/US Facebook, showing CLSB is not inherently malicious: pro-vaccine coalitions coordinate just as densely as anti-vaccine ones, but with starkly different credibility profiles (only ~10% of anti-vaccine CLSB URLs verified true vs. ~40-45% for pro-vaccine), and with content divergently inflected by national political culture (UK anti-vax discourse emphasizes safety, US emphasizes individual freedom). Efstratiou et al., focused on Twitter/X, identifies a structurally similar phenomenon at the level of scientific discourse: a small, almost entirely contrarian coordinated network disproportionately amplifies credentialed anti-consensus experts, and — in a finding that bridges this cluster with the gatekeeping cluster below — shows that news coverage of scientific papers tends to *follow* superspreader activity, with high-trust outlets aligning with conformist superspreaders and low-trust outlets with contrarian ones. Across these four papers, a consistent claim emerges: coordination is a general-purpose behavioral technology, agnostic to political valence, whose reach depends on symbiotic relationships with legacy/mainstream media rather than existing in a purely alternative-media silo.

### Gatekeepers and the measurable ecosystem

[[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] and [[Scalco2026-bd]] pull back to the aggregate structure of the information environment rather than the actors within it. Ducci et al. examine Google News Italia as an algorithmic gatekeeper shaping the health agenda: medical-scientific and healthcare-system stories dominate aggregation, yet celebrity and malpractice items generate the most Facebook engagement, illustrating a gap between what gatekeepers surface and what publics amplify — a dynamic that dovetails with Marino's finding that emotionally charged, shareable content (rather than technical accuracy) drives circulation. Scalco et al. formalizes the demand side of this gap by operationalizing "information voids" as anomalous imbalances between information supply (Facebook, Twitter, GDELT) and demand (Wikipedia, Google Trends) across six European countries; these voids, triggered by events like vaccine authorizations or safety suspensions, are shown to correlate with reduced circulation of high-credibility content and increased misinformation share — offering a mechanistic, ecosystem-level complement to the network-level coordination studies, and suggesting that gatekeeping failures and coordination successes are two faces of the same vacuum-filling dynamic.

### From exposure to consequence

Finally, [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] closes the arc by asking what all this amplification actually does. Using a novel SIRVA epidemic model fit to US county-level vaccination, case, and geolocated antivaccine-tweet data, the authors estimate a causal, if modest, effect: antivaccine Twitter content caused roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and at least 545 additional cases and 8 deaths. This is a deliberately conservative lower bound, but it is the only paper in the set that attempts to convert the correlational and structural findings of the other eight — populist media diets, coordinated amplification, gatekeeping gaps, information voids — into a quantified public-health toll, thereby validating the collective premise that health misinformation networks are not merely a discursive curiosity but a measurable epidemiological input.
