# LLMs in Computational Content Analysis

## The methodological turn: from classifiers to collaborators

The papers filed here trace an arc from using LLMs as faster annotators to treating them as epistemic partners whose outputs require their own validation logic. At one end sit straightforward applications: [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to classify collective action frames in extremist Telegram discourse, and [[Meher2025-qb]] fine-tunes Llama 3.1 with QLoRA to classify conflict events, both showing that domain-adapted open models can rival or beat older supervised pipelines, including on rare categories, while running on consumer hardware. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] extends this logic to unsupervised clustering, showing that commercial embeddings (text-embedding-3-large) outperform language-specific BERT models (UmBERTo) for Italian political news—suggesting that multilingual LLM representations may be supplanting bespoke national-language tools. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] pushes further into measurement theory, showing that simply asking instruction-tuned LLMs to place texts on ideological dimensions and averaging responses reproduces established scaling benchmarks across languages, reframing scaling-by-prompting as a viable alternative to Wordscores/Wordfish.

But a second cluster insists that classification accuracy is not the same as validity, and that LLM integration demands new protocols of trust. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] is the pivotal methodological statement here: describing an "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline (classification → embedding → cluster labeling) for Italian political Facebook content, it argues that each stage needs task-specific validation, that crowdworkers are no longer adequate benchmarks because LLMs often outperform them, and that expert coders are now the necessary gold standard. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] radicalizes this stance, reconceiving LLMs not as classifiers to be validated against ground truth but as "analytical collaborators" whose disagreements with human coders expose ambiguities and historically contingent assumptions baked into theoretical codebooks (e.g., what counts as "moral" framing in a privacy-leak era). Both papers converge on a claim that recurs across the topic: agreement metrics alone cannot capture what LLMs contribute, because disagreement itself can be diagnostic.

## Human–machine agreement, bias, and the limits of annotation

A substantial strand interrogates whether LLM judgments track human judgment reliably enough to replace it. [[Brown2025-jk]] offers the most systematic answer: across four contentious annotation tasks, demographic bias in LLM-human agreement is small and dataset-specific rather than model-specific, while item difficulty (label entropy, i.e., how much humans themselves disagree) is by far the dominant predictor of LLM-human agreement—a finding that reframes "bias" debates around the harder problem of irreducible interpretive disagreement. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Lee2026-je]] push into comparative territory: Larsson validates GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment coding against human raters in a non-English (Norwegian) populist-communication context, finding it serviceable for longitudinal content analysis, while Lee shows LLMs can infer users' *political alignment* even from nonpolitical text, exploiting culturally politicized lexical cues—raising the stakes of "agreement" from content classification to covert trait inference and its attendant privacy risks.

Several papers surface specific failure modes that complicate blanket trust in LLM annotation. [[Paci2025-ag]] finds that even strong models fall well short of human/expert interpretation of implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political speech, with performance sensitive to distractor design and little benefit from few-shot prompting—suggesting pragmatic competence, not just topic classification, is a genuine LLM weak point for discourse analysis. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows that political fact-checking fails dramatically on internal LLM knowledge or even web search alone, improving only with curated retrieval—implying that "LLM-as-fact-checker" pipelines are really "LLM-as-reader-of-curated-evidence" pipelines, with citation patterns additionally skewing toward left-leaning (if reliable) sources. Together these works caution that apparent fluency in classification tasks can mask brittle or systematically skewed underlying competence.

## Validity, bias, and epistemic critique

A more skeptical bloc interrogates whether the entire enterprise of LLM-based content analysis rests on sound epistemic and political foundations. [[Balluff2026-if]] is the most direct intervention, cataloguing reproducibility, environmental, infrastructural, and bias costs of unreflective LLM adoption in communication research and arguing for a "least resource-intensive adequate method" norm—an implicit rebuttal to papers in this collection that treat bigger/commercial models as default best practice. [[Waight2026-ts]] substantiates a related worry empirically: state-coordinated media is memorized by commercial LLMs and measurably shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence when prompted in languages tied to low-media-freedom states, showing that training-data politics, not just prompt design, shape "neutral" model outputs used in downstream coding. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] approaches the problem from epistemology rather than measurement, arguing that generative AI outputs are neither evidence-based, interpretative, nor rule-based facts in the traditional fact-checking sense, but constitute an "emergent" category requiring new evaluative criteria (accuracy, verifiability, contextual relevance, consistency)—a conceptual scaffold that complements the practical validation struggles documented by [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]].

## Beyond text: multimodal and relational extensions

Several papers extend LLM-based content analysis beyond single-text classification into multimodal and relational territory. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] shows GPT-4o outperforming specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person-counting in political Instagram imagery, while [[Arora2025-tx]] argues framing itself is multimodal and uses LLM-style extraction across text, image, and their interaction to capture editorial choices invisible to text-only frame coding. [[Waight2025-al]] and [[Elfes2026-jb]] both push from classification toward relational/narrative measurement: Waight uses LLM-distilled claims-and-subjects comparison to measure cross-lingual "narrative similarity" far more faithfully than text-reuse or topic-modeling baselines (revealing narrative diffusion invisible to lexical methods), while Elfes operationalizes Greimas' actantial model via an LLM to measure "narrative polarisation," finding that YouTube comments paradoxically converge in surface narrative even as deeper motifs preserve partisan difference. [[Fan2025-ut]] addresses a more infrastructural problem underlying all embedding-based applications in this group (clustering, similarity, retrieval): pretrained embeddings encode confounds like source and language, and linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove this contamination cheaply—directly relevant to the clustering pipelines in [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]].

## Applications in substantive political communication research

A final set of papers demonstrates the payoff of validated LLM pipelines for substantive questions, implicitly testing the methodological claims above against real political dynamics. [[Marino2026-he]] applies GPT-4o classification at scale (12M+ posts) to track affective engagement in pro-Bolsonaro Facebook networks, finding hyperpartisan communities are temporally volatile rather than stable echo chambers. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] uses LLMs not to classify but to generate persuasive political content experimentally, identifying information density as the key persuasive mechanism and exposing a persuasion-accuracy trade-off that echoes the fact-checking fragility found in [[DeVerna2025-dl]]. [[Allen2025-ot]] situates a related platform-independent LLM-based intervention (detecting antidemocratic/partisan-animosity content in real feeds) within the broader methodological crisis of platform API closure, arguing such LLM-powered tools are becoming indispensable as direct platform collaboration wanes. Finally, [[Tan2024-vl]] offers the field's organizing survey, mapping generation, assessment, and downstream use of LLM annotations as a distinct subfield—providing the taxonomy against which this entire topic's methodological debates (validation protocols, bias diagnostics, multimodal extensions, epistemic critique) can be located.
