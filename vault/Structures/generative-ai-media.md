# Generative AI & Media Manipulation

## Reframing the media environment after generative AI

A cluster of theoretical interventions argues that the analytic vocabulary built for the "social media era" is no longer adequate once generative AI enters the picture. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] makes the most direct claim: the social-graph paradigm is dissolving under three simultaneous pressures—algorithmic recommendation replacing follower networks, user retreat into closed communities, and generative AI's capacity to produce content without human authors—yielding three "post-social" formations (algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private spheres, AI-mediated communication). [[Helmond2026-ll]] arrives at a kindred diagnosis from platform-studies history, arguing that "platformization," her own decade-old coinage, has been conceptually hollowed out by overuse and must be redefined around AI-driven transcoding; Meta's retirement of the Like button exemplifies a shift from harvesting social-graph signals to AI inference, while Google's space-based compute ambitions signal platform logics migrating into wholly new sectors. [[Tornberg2026-im]] generalizes this trajectory into a periodization—from "platform society" to "AI society"—tracing ruptures across economic (network effects giving way to cognitive lock-in), epistemic (prediction giving way to generative "research-as-a-service"), and political (visibility struggles giving way to alignment politics) registers. Together these three papers converge on a shared empirical observation—declining organic posting, rising chatbot use, synthetic content absorbed into engagement-optimized feeds—while differing in emphasis: Törnberg's papers favor structural/political-economy diagnosis, whereas Helmond insists on situated, uneven institutional analysis against technologically deterministic narratives. This theoretical layer supplies the conceptual scaffolding for the more empirical work below: if platforms are no longer primarily spaces of human-to-human circulation, then claims about "coordinated campaigns" and "disinformation" must be rethought in terms of algorithmic amplification and synthetic throughput rather than purely social contagion.

## The persuasive and coordinating power of generative systems

A second thread asks, empirically, what generative AI *can do* once unleashed on persuasion and coordination tasks. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] provides the most striking capability result: frontier conversational AI reliably out-persuades even world-champion debaters and professional canvassers, with the advantage traced to information throughput (fact density) rather than rapport—an effect that survives into consequential behavior like real-money charitable donations. [[Waight2026-ts]] demonstrates a subtler, institutional channel of influence: state-coordinated media embedded in training corpora is memorized and reproduced by commercial LLMs, producing systematically more pro-regime outputs in the languages of lower-media-freedom countries—a form of "laundering" state narratives into ostensibly neutral AI answers. [[Orlando2025-ul]] and [[Schroeder2026-im]] extend the concern to multi-agent coordination: Orlando's generative agent-based simulations show that LLM agents spontaneously reproduce the network density, narrative convergence, and hashtag-adoption patterns of real information operations, with mere "teammate awareness" nearly matching explicit collusion; Schroeder's policy forum generalizes this into a governance warning about "malicious AI swarms" capable of manufacturing consensus, poisoning training data ("LLM grooming"), and eroding institutional trust, arguing that voluntary platform compliance and purely detection-based defenses cannot keep pace. Read together, these papers move the debate from content-level deception (is this image fake?) to system-level capability (can AI out-argue, out-coordinate, and structurally bias discourse at scale?)—a shift that sets up the more applied studies of actual campaigns.

## Visual manipulation, slop, and coordinated campaigns in the wild

Several papers document how these generic capabilities manifest in concrete manipulation and promotional ecosystems. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] traces an industrial-scale case: coordinated Facebook gambling networks exploiting Meta's organic/paid regulatory grey zone, where ChatGPT's 2022 launch coincides with an exponential, structurally-detected jump in post volume and increasingly hyper-real, AI-generated persuasive imagery layered onto existing drivers like FOMO and manufactured trust. [[Crichton2026-kn]] and [[Topinka2026-hb]] both examine far-right visual culture, but from different angles: Crichton's "re-generative" pipeline shows commercial image models sanitizing explicit hateful markers while displacing rather than erasing ideological content—evidence, they argue, of a structural (not accidental) affinity between far-right aesthetics and generative architectures; Topinka theorizes this affinity at a more abstract level via "slopaganda"—AI content that is obviously fake yet affectively resonant, working through ambient attunement and RLHF-trained "rhetorical sensing" rather than through deception, thereby intensifying existing ideological formations rather than disrupting them. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] situates this empirically within the 2024 European Parliament elections, documenting far-right adoption of AI-generated multimedia on Instagram and X, though with thinner reported findings than its companion pieces. [[Tang2026-gu]] and [[Galip2026-ix]] broaden the lens from political manipulation to cultural production per se: Tang's account of Chinese "AI slop" creators shows virality being industrialized into a manageable, benchmarked production process via matrix-account systems, while Galip, drawing on Ngai's theory of the gimmick, offers the most reflexive reading—arguing that "slop" and "brainrot" work simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," exposing contradictions of cultural labor under AI capitalism while leaving open whether such frictive forms hold any resistant potential. [[Beacken2026-zb]] provides the comparative corrective to any universalizing narrative here, showing across six democratically weakened countries (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey) that GenAI propaganda adoption is structurally mediated rather than uniform—an explicit counterpoint to technologically deterministic accounts found elsewhere in this literature.

## Deepfakes in the wild: audience reception, fraud, and regulation

A parallel empirical strand interrogates how actual audiences respond to synthetic and manipulated media, often complicating assumptions of straightforward deception. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds that visual disinformation does not uniformly outperform text—AI-generated images gain little traction over textual claims, and even decontextualized real video only out-persuades text when abundant authentic footage exists to lend plausibility (as with the Ukraine war but not MH370)—suggesting that fears about generative AI's unprecedented persuasive power may be overstated relative to older, low-tech decontextualization tactics. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s large Kenyan survey experiment pushes this further into "collective sensemaking": a deepfake's political impact is not fixed at production but negotiated through surrounding audience commentary, with credulous comments amplifying harm and incredulous comments partially restoring it, moderated heavily by partisanship. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] shifts from political persuasion to financial harm, mapping the "scam ecosystem" of deepfake-enabled fraud across platforms, telecoms, and financial institutions, and arguing—in a notable policy pivot—that liability should shift from individual victims to gatekeeper intermediaries, since transparency/labeling measures alone prove insufficient once scams migrate into encrypted channels. Read against Hameleers' and Wack's reception-focused findings, Schiffrin's brief reframes the deepfake problem as one of infrastructural enablement rather than individual credulity, echoing Schroeder's and Ferrara's calls for systemic rather than artifact-level intervention.

## Generative AI and the crisis of evaluation: facts, trust, and knowledge infrastructures

The topic closes on a more reflexive problem: how scholarly and journalistic evaluation itself is destabilized by generative AI. [[Emilio2026-ik]] offers the broadest synthesis, arguing that the real danger is not isolated deepfakes but "synthetic realities"—coherent, interactive, personalized fabrications across a content/identity/interaction/institutions stack—culminating in a paradox whereby societies may rationally discount all digital evidence, raising the "cost of truth" itself and demanding a shift toward measuring "epistemic security" rather than artifact authenticity. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] approaches the same rupture from epistemology and fact-checking practice, showing that evidence-based, interpretative, and institutional fact categories all fail to capture GenAI outputs, and proposing "emergent facts" as a new category requiring relational, indicator-based assessment (accuracy, verifiability, contextual relevance, consistency) rather than binary truth judgments. [[Mattis2026-gu]] tests the practical consequences of this epistemic unease for journalism, finding that *all* task-specific AI disclosures reduce perceived news trustworthiness, with fact-checking disclosures penalized most heavily—a finding that complicates simple transparency mandates as a solution to the trust problems Emilio and Dierickx describe theoretically. Finally, [[Triedman2025-uy]] grounds these abstract worries in a concrete case of AI reshaping a trusted knowledge infrastructure: Grokipedia is shown to be heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically more reliant on low-quality and blacklisted sources—especially on controversial and political topics—illustrating empirically how an AI-mediated "encyclopedia" can inherit the legitimacy of crowdsourced knowledge while quietly eroding its sourcing standards. Across this section, the throughline is that generative AI does not merely add new false content to evaluate, but destabilizes the very categories and institutional protocols—fact-checking, source reliability, disclosure norms—through which societies have historically adjudicated truth.
