# Information Disorder & Disinformation Narratives

## The Shape of the Field: From "Fake News" to Information Disorder

The corpus begins, chronologically and conceptually, with a reckoning over vocabulary. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] opens with a now-foundational critique: "fake news" is an invention of a liar, a creator-centric category that cannot capture how falsehood actually *circulates* in a hybrid news system. This diagnosis anchors the entire topic. [[Freelon2020-yp]] formalizes the terminological turn toward "disinformation" (intent + harm + deception) as the field's organizing concept, while [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] demonstrate empirically what Giglietto predicted: "fake news" has become a floating rhetorical weapon—used by Newsmax and Occupy Democrats alike, and by Italian politicians via "Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification"—to delegitimize opponents rather than describe falsity. [[Bennett2018-ys]] supplies the macro-theoretical frame uniting these threads: a systemic "disinformation order" rooted in institutional distrust, not isolated hoaxes. [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]] provide the structural substrate—the hybrid media system—within which all these dynamics of propagation, legitimacy contest, and gatekeeping collapse unfold.

## Measuring the Problem: Prevalence, Reach, and the "Overstated" Debate

A parallel empirical tradition asks, simply, how much misinformation actually circulates and who encounters it. The foundational 2016-election studies—[[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Vosoughi2018-at]], [[Allen2020-nj]]—converge on a now-classic finding: exposure is heavily concentrated among a narrow, ideologically extreme, often older subpopulation, and fake news is a vanishingly small share of the total media diet, even as it diffuses farther and faster than truth once shared. [[Allcott2019-gn]] and [[Guess2019-ym]] extend this into post-2016 platform interventions, showing Facebook's relative decline even as Twitter's problem persisted. This "it's smaller than you think" current runs directly into revisionist pushback: [[Yang2023-cg]] argues that link-based measurement methodologically erases *image*-based misinformation, which may be the platform's largest vector; [[Goel2025-iq]] shows that mainstream, "true" articles are routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives, meaning domain-blocklist approaches systematically undercount the phenomenon; and [[Nenno2025-xa]], [[Hourigan2026-oc]] push the empirical gaze beyond WEIRD contexts and beyond political "hot-button" topics into everyday, cross-national misinformation encounters. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Vincent_undated-re]] extend this measurement project to Europe and TikTok respectively, part of an ongoing platform-monitoring infrastructure that includes [[Luhring2025-od]]'s meticulous audit of the NewsGuard database underlying much of this research—a methodological corrective essential to the PROMPT project's monitoring ambitions.

## From Claims to Narratives: The Interpretive Turn

A decisive shift across the corpus moves from treating disinformation as a set of discrete false claims toward treating it as *narrative*. [[Marwick2026-qd]] is the programmatic statement: disinformation succeeds as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic story, with characters, plots, and catchphrases that evade domain-level detection entirely. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the theoretical apparatus, arguing via Ricoeur and narratology that stories can be referentially and ethically "suspicious" without being straightforwardly false—undermining the true/false binary that structures most fact-checking. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes this empirically in the U.S. hybrid media ecosystem, finding that cumulative cross-outlet exposure to a narrative—not any single claim—drives belief. The CSCW tradition of [[Starbird2019-qv]], [[Starbird2025-jj]], and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develops parallel machinery: disinformation as *collaborative work* and *collective sensemaking*, where "evidence" (often true) is fused with partisan "frames" through quote-tweet and comment structures, and where "deep stories" (per Hochschild) are performed jointly by elites, influencers, and audiences rather than authored by any single source. [[Marwick2025-vx]] extends the narrative/socialization lens to Far-Right "redpilling," reframing radicalization as gradual story-immersion rather than sudden exposure. Methodologically, [[Waight2025-al]] offers the computational payoff of this turn: an LLM-based pipeline for measuring *narrative* similarity (shared claims about shared events) rather than lexical or topical similarity, demonstrated on the Russian "biolabs" narrative's diffusion into mainstream U.S. outlets—a direct technical answer to the narrative-not-fact-check thesis advanced by [[Marwick2026-qd]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]], the latter arguing explicitly that Valencia flood conspiracy theories require "narrative monitoring and argument-checking" beyond claim-level fact-checking.

## The War in Ukraine as Narrative Battleground

The Ukraine conflict recurs as the corpus's central live case for cross-national narrative monitoring. [[Sadler2025-vu]]'s close reading of NATO-blame narratives, [[Waight2025-al]]'s biolabs tracing, [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s analysis of TikTok's sonic propaganda techniques, [[Graham2025-gp]]'s account of #IStandWithPutin exploiting the "infrastructure of truth," [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s post-truth-space mapping of Moldovan Facebook discourse, [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s dual-audience (domestic/foreign) propaganda model, and [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]]'s coordination analysis of pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus together sketch a multi-platform, multi-national disinformation apparatus. These studies collectively illustrate PROMPT's core proposition: war narratives are not contained within single platforms or languages but require comparative, cross-border monitoring infrastructure—precisely what [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s vera.ai-affiliated methodology and [[Jurg2025-ur]]'s YouTube moderation audit are built to supply.

## Coordination, Infrastructure, and the Industrialization of Influence

A substantial cluster treats disinformation as organized labor and infrastructure rather than spontaneous belief-sharing. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT reconstruction of the Internet Research Agency reframes the "troll factory" as an ordinary media-labor-market actor, integrated with legitimate PR and journalism careers—a corrective to romanticized accounts of covert propaganda. [[Keller2019-nk]]'s South Korean NIS case offers rare ground-truth evidence that coordination signatures, not bot-like individual behavior, best identify astroturfing, while showing that even well-resourced campaigns have limited discourse impact—a sobering counterpoint to alarmist assumptions. [[Gaw2025-ru]] extends this to influence operations as "political brokerage" in the Philippines, while [[Kansaon2025-id]], [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]], [[Zhao2025-ny]], and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] develop detection methodologies for coordinated inauthentic behavior across WhatsApp, Bluesky, hashtag ecosystems, and Facebook groups respectively—the latter proposing "Deceptive Information Operations" as a category that subsumes state propaganda, commercial scams, and moderation evasion within one structural logic. [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Knupfer2025-vt]] trace how hyperpartisan media (Sky News Australia, the CRT episode) function as "digital surrogates" bridging elite political actors and networked audiences, extending Benkler, Faris, and Roberts's asymmetric propaganda thesis ([[Benkler2018-lw]]) into new national and issue contexts. [[Marino2023-9137f448]]'s Alternative Influence Networks study and [[Arceneaux2026-xk]]'s bot-agenda-building analysis similarly trace how fringe and automated voices are laundered through legacy-media remediation and campaign messaging.

## Partisan Asymmetry and Its Contestation

Running throughout is the unresolved question of partisan asymmetry. [[Benkler2018-lw]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Yang2023-cg]], and [[Tornberg2025-ir]] each find right-leaning and especially radical-right-populist sources disproportionately implicated in low-factuality sharing—with [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary analysis isolating the *interaction* of populism and right-wing ideology, not either alone, as causal. [[Renault2025-uh]] extends this into the post-fact-checking era, showing Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under X's crowdsourced Community Notes—directly rebutting claims that professional fact-checkers alone produce this asymmetry. Against this, [[Osmundsen2021-et]] locates the psychological driver not in ignorance or ideology per se but in partisan animus (out-party hatred), while [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" shows citizens of all stripes perceive their own side as disproportionately targeted—a perceptual bias independent of actual asymmetry. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] and [[Karlsson2026-hd]] connect these dynamics to electoral consequences: far-right performance shapes disinformation concern, and mere *media narratives* about interference (regardless of actual effects) can itself produce asymmetric polarization.

## Platforms, Governance, and the Fact-Checking Apparatus

The corpus's final major strand concerns institutional response. [[Humprecht2020-gd]] offers the comparative resilience framework—structural, macro-level, cross-national—that gives the whole topic its comparative ambition, distinguishing high-resilience Northern Europe from polarized Southern Europe and an exceptional United States. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes platform moderation as a business-model-constrained practice (behavioral rather than truth-based intervention), a thesis sharply tested by [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]]'s cross-national study of what actually happens to debunked content after Meta's 2025 retreat from fact-checking, and by [[Holt2026-zq]]'s finding that user "false news" reports reflect political disagreement more than objective falsity—raising doubts about Community Notes as a replacement. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects Meta's self-exculpatory discursive framing, while [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines fact-checkers' own rhetorical defense of platform partnerships, and [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's inconsistent "authoritative source" labeling during EU elections. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Mahl2026-hc]]—the latter a 47-expert Delphi study—bookend this governance conversation with calls for coordinated, multi-level, anticipatory intervention, explicitly rejecting any "silver bullet," individual-level fix. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] and [[Xue2025-bp]] turn to the automation of fact-checking itself, finding LLMs unreliable without curated retrieval and fact-checks themselves surprisingly emotional. [[Emilio2026-ik]] and [[Wack2026-bt]] close the loop on the emerging generative-AI threat: not isolated deepfakes but "synthetic realities" and socially negotiated interpretation of AI content—the latter grounded in a large Kenyan experiment that insists Global South contexts, not just WEIRD ones, must anchor this research.

## National and Electoral Case Studies: Brazil, the Philippines, the UK

Brazil functions as a recurring laboratory for testing these theories outside the U.S./Europe axis: [[Rodarte2026-dk]], [[Rossini2026-jn]], [[Rossini2026-mj]], [[Ventura2025-sw]], [[Kansaon2025-id]], and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] together trace how electoral misinformation, WhatsApp coordination, and parliamentary narrative contests erode institutional trust and political tolerance in a weak-partisanship, Global South democracy—directly answering [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s editorial call to de-center WEIRD assumptions. [[Balluff2026-bv]], [[Bouchafra2026-ts]], and [[Tai2026-qk]] extend the elite-strategy lens to Austrian media capture, Swedish far-right visual securitization on TikTok, and U.S. elected officials' institutional checks on misinformation sharing, respectively—each reinforcing the volume's central thesis that disinformation is best understood as a strategic, elite-driven, narratively-structured practice embedded in specific national media systems, rather than a generic technological pathology of "fake news" writ large.
