# Information Disorder & Disinformation Narratives

## The Shape of the Field: From "Fake News" to Narrative Ecosystems

The corpus traces a conceptual migration away from treating disinformation as discrete false statements toward understanding it as narrative, ecosystemic, and structurally produced. The foundational move is made early and explicitly by [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], which argues that creator-intent-based typologies of "fake news" cannot capture how falsehood actually circulates in a hybrid news system, proposing instead a micro-meso-macro model centred on propagators rather than injectors. This reorientation from *content* to *circulation* becomes the connective tissue of the whole topic. [[Marwick2026-qd]] pushes furthest in this direction, reconceiving disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *stories* rather than fact-claims — a move echoed and formalised by [[Sadler2025-vu]], who imports narratology and hermeneutic philosophy to argue that the true/false binary cannot capture referentially and ethically problematic storytelling. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] operationalise this narrative turn empirically, showing via evidence-frame analysis and "deep storytelling" of U.S. election rumors that misinformation lives in the interaction between (often accurate) evidence and partisan frames, not in falsity per se. [[Marwick2025-vx]] extends the same logic to radicalization, showing that "redpilling" is processual community socialization built from accumulated disinformation-as-evidence rather than sudden conversion. This narrative paradigm is precisely what animates the researcher's own applied work: [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]] operationalise narrative monitoring directly, the latter proposing "argument-checking" as a complement to fact-checking during the Valencia DANA floods, and [[Waight2025-al]] supplies a methodological bridge, using LLMs to measure narrative similarity across languages in the Ukraine "biolabs" case — the computational infrastructure PROMPT-style narrative-tracking work depends on. [[Goel2025-iq]] complicates the picture further by showing that misleading narratives are frequently built from *true* mainstream content, undermining source-list approaches to measurement.

## Conceptual Housekeeping: Definitions, Complexity, and Weaponized Labels

A second cluster interrogates the vocabulary of the field itself. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that dominant analogies — infodemic, information warfare, information pollution — each illuminate only one level of a multilevel complex system and can produce backfiring interventions when applied alone. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] extends epistemological reflection to generative AI, proposing "emergent facts" as a fourth category beyond evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based facts. Meanwhile [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] show empirically that "fake news" itself has become a rhetorical weapon — deployed by hyperpartisan outlets and political actors to delegitimize opponents rather than to describe misinformation — a dynamic also visible in [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s early case study of counter-hashtag discourse after Charlie Hebdo. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of radicalization research and [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s review of depolarization scholarship both diagnose the same disciplinary malady from adjacent fields: fragmented, under-theorized, and disconnected from evidence-based recommendations, calling for integrative frameworks much as [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study calls for coordinated, multi-level (macro/meso/micro) responses rather than siloed individual-level fixes. [[Emilio2026-ik]] pushes the definitional frontier forward into "synthetic realities," arguing GenAI's risk lies not in isolated deepfakes but coherent fabricated information environments that produce a rational discounting of all digital evidence.

## Platforms, Coordination, and the Infrastructure of Falsehood

A substantial cluster examines *where* and *how* disinformation is produced and amplified at scale. Historical and organizational work — [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] on the St Petersburg IRA's labor-market structure, [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] on the persistence and event-appropriation strategies of manipulation campaigns — is complemented by contemporary detection studies: [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] on real-time bot detection on Bluesky, [[Zhao2025-ny]] on hashtag hijacking, [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]]'s "Zombie Internet" framework for Deceptive Information Operations that unifies state propaganda, gambling networks, and poorly-moderated adult-content groups under shared structural logics, and [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s "post-truth spaces" concept for mapping influence in Moldovan Facebook discourse around the Ukraine war. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] and [[Bosch2024-hj]] extend this to specific vectors — coordinated contrarian science networks and TikTok's sonic propaganda techniques respectively — while measurement-oriented pieces ([[Vincent_undated-re]], [[Mosleh2024-op]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], [[Scalco2026-bd]]) track platform-level prevalence, cross-platform divergence, and the "information voids" that create fertile ground for misinformation to fill demand unmet by credible supply. [[Luhring2025-od]]'s methodological audit of NewsGuard is essential connective infrastructure for much of this measurement work, warning that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications can badly distort findings.

## Effects, Elites, and Political Consequences

Downstream consequences form a third strand. Several papers examine misinformation's role in eroding democratic norms: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use Brazilian panel data to show belief in electoral misinformation predicts rising political intolerance and declining trust in election results, with messaging apps (rather than social media) as the key indirect pathway — a finding sharpened by [[Ventura2025-sw]]'s WhatsApp deactivation experiment. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a "hostile misinformation effect" — a partisan perceptual bias parallel to the hostile media effect — across three European elections. At the elite level, [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation-sharing, but their conjunction (radical-right populism specifically) does; [[Tai2026-qk]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] extend this elite-level lens to institutional checks on politicians and far-right electoral performance's effect on public disinformation concern. [[Wack2026-bt]] shows deepfake impact in Kenya is not fixed at production but "socially negotiated" through audience comments, extending collective-sensemaking theory into the Global South and generative-AI era. [[Nenno2025-xa]] and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] both push against WEIRD-centric bias, the former showing news-value patterns in flagged misinformation diverge meaningfully outside Western contexts.

## Interventions, Fact-Checking, and the Platform-Research Nexus

The final and most self-reflexive cluster concerns countermeasures and the political economy of studying them. Prebunking work ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], [[Spampatti2026-kx]]) shows scalable, real-world inoculation effects while urging misinformation research to borrow climate psychology's lessons on systems thinking and behavioural (not just cognitive) outcomes. Fact-checking itself is scrutinized as an institution under strain: [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines fact-checkers' rhetorical defense of platform funding, [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] documents the fallout from Meta's abandonment of third-party fact-checking, [[Holt2026-zq]] shows user-flagged "false news" mostly reflects political disagreement rather than falsity, and [[Renault2025-uh]] finds persistent partisan asymmetries even under crowdsourced Community Notes. [[Xue2025-bp]] and [[Hourigan2026-oc]] examine fact-checking and misinformation perception from the ground up — emotionality as fact-checking strategy, and everyday non-political misinformation encounters respectively. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] tests LLMs as fact-checking tools, finding curated retrieval, not model scale, is decisive. Undergirding all of this is a sober metascientific reckoning with the field's dependence on platforms: [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] document pervasive, often undisclosed industry influence on misinformation research itself, echoed by [[Heiss2026-qv]], while [[Pierri2025-hm]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] trace the fragile, contested emergence of the EU Digital Services Act's Article 40 as a regulatory alternative to platform-controlled "data philanthropy" — the institutional terrain on which the researcher's own EU-facing narrative-tracking work is built. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] closes the loop by showing how Meta's own public discourse frames "problematic content" to externalize blame and pre-empt exactly this kind of regulatory scrutiny, while [[Fletcher2026-lv]] and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] provide the broader trust-in-news and science-communication backdrop against which all these narrative and platform dynamics play out.
