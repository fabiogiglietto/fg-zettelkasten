# Information Disorder & Fact-Checking Support

## The disinformation ontology problem: from claims to narratives

A recurring theoretical move across this literature is the rejection of "fake news" as a stable, definable category of false content, in favor of processual and narrative accounts of how falsehood circulates. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] provides an early and influential statement: drawing on second-order cybernetics and Chadwick's hybrid media system, it argues that the intent of a content's *creator* is analytically far less important than the judgments and choices of successive *propagators*, and proposes a micro/meso/macro model of composite propagation cascades rather than a single "disinformation vs. misinformation" typology. This anti-creator-centrism reappears, radicalized, in [[Marwick2026-qd]], which argues that successful disinformation is best understood as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative*—stories with characters, catchphrases, and moral arcs that satisfy desire and produce catharsis, and that therefore evade domain-level detection or platform-bound moderation entirely. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes the narrative turn further still into hermeneutic and ethical territory, arguing that disinformation studies has used "narrative" loosely and that a Ricoeur-inflected, narratological toolkit is needed to assess content that is referentially and ethically troubling without being straightforwardly *false*—a direct challenge to the field's true/false binary.

Several empirical papers operationalize this narrative turn. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] shows survey evidence that cumulative, cross-outlet exposure to a recurring disinformation narrative (not a single false claim) predicts belief. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]], working from CSCW and sensemaking traditions, demonstrate empirically how election-fraud "deep stories" are co-produced by influencers, elites, and audiences through quote-tweet framing dynamics—evidence and frame interacting, often on largely accurate raw material, to yield misleading conclusions. [[Goel2025-iq]] operationalizes this insight at scale, showing that mainstream news articles are disproportionately co-shared with fake news specifically because they contain narrative structures usable for misleading ends—a direct empirical rebuttal to domain-list measurement approaches. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written from inside a fact-checking organization (Maldita.es), translates the narrative critique into practice: proposing "argument-checking" and "narrative monitoring" as necessary complements to claim-level fact-checking during crises, since corrected claims regress while narratives persist and mutate. [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Yoo2026-ev]] complete this arc by turning the analytic lens on the term "fake news" itself, showing—in Italian and U.S. contexts respectively—that accusations of fake news function as rhetorical weapons of delegitimization rather than descriptions of informational quality, reshaping Hallin-style spheres of consensus and deviance.

## Political elites, parties, and the strategic uses of misinformation

A second cluster treats misinformation not as a bottom-up contagion but as an elite-driven, strategic phenomenon embedded in institutional and ideological contests over epistemic authority. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents how Brazilian parliamentarians pursued distinct "epistemic authority" strategies (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, proximity brokerage) during a health crisis, effectively contesting journalistic gatekeeping. [[Tornberg2025-ir]], with a 26-country, 32-million-tweet dataset, shows that neither populism nor right-left ideology alone predicts elite misinformation sharing—only their conjunction, radical-right populism, does—reframing misinformation as a symptom of institutional-legitimacy crisis rather than generic platform pathology. [[Tai2026-qk]] complements this by asking which institutional and ideological positions function as *checks* constraining elite sharing. [[Balluff2026-bv]] extends the elite-capture logic into "journalism for sale," showing quasi-experimentally how covert government-advertising arrangements (the Austrian Inseratenaffäre) reshape coverage patterns even absent visible bribery, while [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a structural, cross-national visibility bias favoring the Radical Right in European election coverage—undercutting radical-right claims of media marginalization. Downstream consequences for democratic norms are traced by the Brazil-focused pair [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]], which show belief in electoral misinformation predicting rising political intolerance and eroded trust in results respectively, positioning mainstream news use as a resilience factor even among supporters of an illiberal incumbent. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a novel bias mechanism—the "hostile misinformation effect," a misinformation-domain analogue of the hostile media effect—showing partisans perceive their own side as disproportionately targeted, with consequences for trust in media and elections. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] similarly connects far-right electoral performance to shifts in public disinformation concern.

## Platforms, moderation, and the political economy of information governance

A substantial thread interrogates platforms' own role in defining, moderating, and profiting from "misinformation." [[Donovan2025-ws]] offers the most sweeping account, arguing that "misinformation-at-scale" is a structural feature of engagement-driven business models, tracing platforms' oscillation between behavioral moderation and retreat. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] complements this with discourse analysis of Meta's own Newsroom communications, showing how the company frames problematic content as originating from outside actors while erasing its own platform affordances (e.g., Facebook Groups) from the story. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] turn to fact-checkers themselves as they navigate this shifting terrain: the former documents what actually happens to content after verification (rarely removal) in the wake of Zuckerberg's anti-fact-checking pivot, while the latter analyzes the "apologia" rhetoric fact-checkers use to defend their financial dependence on the very platforms they scrutinize. [[Holt2026-zq]] and [[Renault2025-uh]] empirically stress-test the Community Notes/crowd-reporting alternative to professional fact-checking that Meta and X have embraced: the former shows user reports track political disagreement rather than falsity, the latter shows persistent partisan asymmetry in flagged content even under crowdsourced rather than professional adjudication—jointly complicating claims that crowd-based systems are less biased. Zooming out, [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Pierri2025-hm]] address the meta-level problem of studying platforms at all: the former catalogs mechanisms of industry capture of research (echoing tobacco/fossil-fuel agnotology), the latter assesses the DSA's Article 40 researcher-access provisions as a partial, contested remedy.

## Measurement, methodology, and infrastructure for fact-checking support

Several papers are explicitly infrastructural, building or auditing tools that support journalists and fact-checkers—the PROMPT/MINE-FACTS lineage most directly represented by [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]], which documents a content-agnostic Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior detection tool tested with Italian fact-checkers, showing markedly higher hit rates for problematic content than routine manual fact-checking. [[Rogers2026-cy]], from the EU's vera.ai project, develops the adjacent concept of "post-truth spaces"—mappable clusters of hard-to-verify or disinformation-linked accounts—as an actionable lead-generation tool for verification specialists, demonstrated on Moldovan Facebook discourse around the Ukraine war. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" (Golebiewski & boyd) as a quantifiable demand-supply imbalance, showing empirically that voids correlate with reduced high-credibility content and increased misinformation share. Measurement itself comes under scrutiny in [[Luhring2025-od]], an audit of the NewsGuard database widely used in source-based misinformation research, cautioning that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can badly distort findings even where continuous scores are stable. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s Facebook-newsfeed methodology to Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares in election years but stable exposure—demonstrating the value and limits of cross-national platform-data comparisons. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] and [[Dierickx2026-tw]] turn to the newest frontier, LLM-assisted fact-checking: the former shows curated retrieval dramatically outperforms raw LLM knowledge or web search (with a left-leaning citation skew), while the latter argues generative AI outputs require an entirely new epistemic category, "emergent facts," beyond evidence-based, interpretative, or rule-based notions of factuality inherited from pre-AI fact-checking. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s comparative audit of Grokipedia against Wikipedia offers a concrete, large-scale case of exactly this problem: an AI-generated reference source that is derivative yet systematically skews toward low-quality and blacklisted citations on controversial topics.

## Emotion, virality, and the psychology/sociology of belief

A final set of papers addresses the affective and cognitive machinery underlying misinformation's spread and correction. [[Xue2025-bp]] finds fact-checking content itself is highly emotional despite its objectivity norms, complicating the genre's self-presentation. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that the *source* of misinformation (influencer vs. ordinary user) shapes the toxicity of audience response as much as content does. [[Nenno2025-xa]] extends news-values theory cross-nationally (24 countries) to user-flagged Facebook misinformation, finding conflict is the most universal driver but with meaningful WEIRD/non-WEIRD divergence—one of several papers, alongside [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue introduction, explicitly pushing the field past its WEIRD-centric defaults. [[Adam2026-tz]] uses panel-plus-web-tracking data to show both contagion (alternative media) and mitigation (mainstream media) effects on COVID conspiracy belief, mediated by populist selective exposure and avoidance—directly complementing Efstspampatti-style calls (see [[Spampatti2026-kx]]) to borrow climate psychology's lessons on motivated, not just cognitive, drivers of misinformation belief, and echoing [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi-based expert consensus that individual-level interventions like fact-checking are rated *least* important relative to platform and journalism-ecosystem reforms. [[Marwick2025-vx]] documents "processual redpilling" into far-right belief as gradual socialization rather than single-exposure conversion, while [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] examine journalism's own inadvertent role in conspiracy formation and politicized science-source selection. On the harder end of causal inference, [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] estimates a lower-bound causal effect of antivaccine tweets on vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths via a novel epidemic-compartmental model—one of the few papers in this set to trace a rigorous line from online speech to offline harm. Finally, several papers address emergent technical vectors: [[Choi2026-bz]], [[Hameleers2026-mc]], and [[Wack2026-bt]] all interrogate deepfakes and AI-generated visual/audio disinformation, converging on a shared finding that modality and technological sophistication matter less than context, social framing, and partisan motivated reasoning—echoed at civilizational scale in [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s account of "synthetic realities" and the corrosive "epistemic tax" of ubiquitous GenAI, and grounded empirically in platform-specific case studies such as [[Bosch2024-hj]] (TikTok's sonic propaganda around Ukraine), [[Graham2025-gp]] (#IStandWithPutin's exploitation of platform trust infrastructure), and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] (real-time coordinated-bot detection on Bluesky)—together mapping how the fact-checking and information-disorder research agenda is being forced to keep pace with each new platform and generative technology.
