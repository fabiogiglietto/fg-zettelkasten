# Health Misinformation

## Health Misinformation

The papers filed here trace a shared arc: from documenting *who* encounters problematic health content, to mapping *how* it circulates and is coordinated, to asking whether any of this actually moves the needle on health behavior and outcomes. Read together, they push against a simplistic "misinformation causes harm" narrative, replacing it with a more conditional, structurally-situated account of exposure, amplification, and impact.

### Who is exposed, and how concentrated is the problem?

[[Lyons2026-ca]] opens the strand with a sobering corrective to infodemic alarmism: passive web-tracking data show that exposure to low-credibility health content is rare in the aggregate, but heavily concentrated among older adults, poor discerners, and conspiracist-minded users — and crucially, this exposure travels through habitual cross-domain browsing (other low-credibility sites, YouTube) rather than through search or social media referrals. This finding reframes the problem: rather than asking "how much bad content exists," the more useful question is "who keeps seeking it out, and through what media diet." It also complicates any simple platform-blame story, since social platforms are not the primary vector here — a theme that resurfaces, in inverted form, when the Facebook-centered papers below find Facebook *is* a major vector for coordinated vaccine content.

### Mapping coordinated networks and problematic actors

Several papers, largely from the MINE-FACTS Italian fact-checking collaboration, develop methods for detecting coordinated spreaders of problematic health content at scale. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] documents the construction of a content-agnostic tool built on Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) to help Italian fact-checkers surface problematic COVID-19 content faster than manual review allows, tracing how a covid-skeptic coordinated network grew out of pre-existing political clusters and adapted tactics (link laundering, first-comment link hiding, religious-group infiltration) to evade platform enforcement. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this dataset to examine Alternative Influence Networks, showing that an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of influencers generates outsized, share-driven engagement, and — importantly — that legacy media and mainstream journalists remain central nodes in circulating this content via remediation of TV and newspaper statements. Together these two papers establish that coordinated inauthentic behavior is not a fringe phenomenon operating outside the mainstream media system, but is parasitic on it.

[[Song2025-yh]] broadens the CLSB lens cross-nationally and ideologically, comparing pro- and anti-vaccine coordinated communities on Facebook in the UK and US. Its key move is to show that CLSB itself is not inherently nefarious — pro-vaccine institutional actors (NHS, CDC) coordinate just as much as anti-vaccine ones, but circulate markedly more verifiable content — and that the content and framing of anti-vaccine discourse is shaped by national political culture (safety concerns in the UK vs. individual-freedom rhetoric in the US). This nuance pairs with [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]], which shifts attention from Facebook coordination to newspaper citation practices, showing that UK outlets selectively amplify scientists aligned with their political orientation (Great Barrington vs. John Snow Memorandum signatories) even though both groups are mainstream researchers rather than fringe figures. Read alongside Song, Ghezzi suggests that polarization in health discourse operates at multiple institutional layers — not just via coordinated grassroots/platform manipulation, but through ordinary editorial selection among legitimate experts.

[[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] adds a complementary, less adversarial layer: even without any coordination or disinformation intent, algorithmic gatekeepers like Google News Italia structure the health agenda in ways that reward emotionally charged, "popular" content (celebrity health news, healthcare malpractice) over technical scientific topics when it reaches Facebook audiences. This paper is a useful bridge, showing that platform dynamics amplify sensational health content through ordinary aggregation and engagement logics, independent of any bad-faith coordination — a baseline against which the deliberately coordinated networks in Giglietto, Marino, and Song can be read as an intensification rather than a wholly separate phenomenon.

### Does it matter? Causal impact and the limits of exposure

The strand closes by confronting the hardest question: does exposure to this material actually change vaccination behavior and downstream health outcomes? [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] and [[Allen2024-av]] offer complementary but differently calibrated answers. Bollenbacher develops a compartmental epidemic model (SIRVA) fit to county-level Twitter exposure, cases, and vaccinations, estimating that antivaccine tweets caused roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and a measurable, if modest, uptick in cases and deaths — a causally-framed, lower-bound estimate linking online speech directly to epidemic burden. Allen, working at Facebook scale with a combination of survey experiments and machine-learned persuasion scores, arrives at a more unsettling reframing: flagged misinformation does persuade when seen, but its reach is so limited that its aggregate societal impact is dwarfed — by a factor of 46 — by factually accurate but "vaccine-skeptical" content from mainstream outlets that nobody is fact-checking or flagging. This directly complicates the fact-checking apparatus built by Giglietto and colleagues: veracity-focused interventions, however effective at the individual level, may be targeting only a sliver of the content actually responsible for eroding vaccine confidence.

### Synthesis

Across these eight papers, a consistent tension emerges between *visibility* (what gets flagged, coordinated, or studied as "misinformation") and *consequence* (what actually shapes beliefs and behavior). Lyons and Allen both suggest that the conventionally demonized content — low-credibility sites, fact-check-flagged posts — is a smaller piece of the puzzle than its salience in misinformation research implies; the real action lies in habitual consumption patterns (Lyons), mainstream media framing (Ghezzi, Marino, Ducci), and accurate-but-misleading content (Allen). Meanwhile, the CLSB-based papers (Giglietto, Marino, Song) demonstrate that coordination is a genuinely useful signal for triage even if it's ideologically agnostic, and Bollenbacher shows that, under the right causal model, even a platform like Twitter's antivaccine corpus leaves a detectable epidemiological fingerprint. Taken as a set, this body of work in the MINE programme argues for a more structurally and institutionally grounded understanding of health misinformation — one attentive to who is exposed, which networks and gatekeepers amplify what, and, crucially, whether the usual suspects (flagged falsehoods) are even the right target for intervention.
