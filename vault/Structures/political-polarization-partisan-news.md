# Political Polarization and Partisan News Ecosystems

## The contested empirical core: does polarization exist, and how would we know?

The corpus opens onto a foundational methodological anxiety: much of what is called "polarization" or "echo chamber" research rests on inconsistent definitions and incompatible operationalizations. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 studies shows that disagreement about whether echo chambers exist is substantially an artifact of measurement choices, regional and platform biases, rather than a stable empirical fact. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] extends this critique to the policy side, showing that scholarly recommendations for *depolarization* are rarely tied to the very evidence the same studies produce, and instead smuggle in unexamined normative commitments — chiefly a Habermasian deliberative ideal. This meta-level skepticism is echoed by [[Munger2025-cz]] and [[Allen2025-ot]], who use the Meta2020 partnership and its successors to argue that single-shot, platform-dependent experiments suffer from low "temporal validity" and inconsistent conceptualization of core terms like "echo chamber," making cumulative knowledge difficult. Against this backdrop, [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the touchstone empirical result: Facebook's algorithm modestly narrows cross-cutting exposure, but individual clicking behavior narrows it more — a finding that several papers in this collection either extend, complicate, or attempt to overturn with newer platforms and methods.

## Platforms as active curators, not neutral conduits

A major thread treats recommender systems and feed algorithms not as passive mirrors of user preference but as causal, sometimes owner-manipulable, forces. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X shows that switching to the algorithmic "For You" feed causally shifts attitudes rightward and — crucially — that this effect persists asymmetrically because the algorithm reshapes *who users follow*, not just what they see, reconciling the puzzling null results of the 2020 Meta studies. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complements this by showing that apparent right-leaning algorithmic bias on post-Musk Twitter is largely explained by rewards for "agitating" content and proximity to Elon Musk himself, who became disproportionately central to the network — a finding that reframes ideological amplification as an artifact of platform-owner-centric incentive structures. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] pushes further into the machinery, showing that X's "Who to Follow" embeddings inadvertently encode a near-perfect linear representation of Left-Right ideology, raising GDPR-relevant concerns about inadvertent political profiling. [[Brown2026-br]] offers a methodological corrective to YouTube radicalization panics, disentangling algorithmic from user-driven effects and finding strong "rabbit hole" dynamics but no independent echo-chamber or radicalization pathway — though a platform-wide conservative tilt persists regardless of user ideology. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment demonstrates that engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup, moralized, emotional content and distorts perceived social norms (not directly attitudes), and that a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment — a rare intervention-oriented result. Finally, [[Ventura2026-yc]] shows that even platform *bans* have durable partisan consequences: Brazil's suspension of X produced a "sorting ratchet" in which conservative users disproportionately evaded the ban, permanently shifting the platform's ideological composition. Together these papers argue that platform governance decisions — feed design, ranking, bans, moderation architecture — are themselves independent variables in polarization dynamics, not simply background conditions, a claim [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] makes forcefully by showing that Facebook's viewership-shares relationship fluctuates in lockstep with known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures.

## Italian and European election ecosystems: parallelism, insularity, and visibility

A cluster of papers grounds these general dynamics in specific European, and especially Italian, electoral contexts, extending Hallin and Mancini's comparative media systems framework into the social media era. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] is a central node here: analyzing the 2018 Italian election, it shows that populist-aligned news sources (M5S, Lega) are more "insular" on Twitter, and that insularity inversely predicts Facebook's comments-to-shares ratio — with M5S uniquely able to mobilize amplification for favorable coverage and contestation for unfavorable coverage, evidencing a strategic "cyber party" logic. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s earlier study of the 2013 Italian election's talk-show/Twitter hybridity shows that second-screen "hybrid media system" participation was narrow and largely reactive to broadcast logic rather than agenda-shaping — an important baseline against which later, more platform-native dynamics can be measured. [[Balluff2026-bv]] extends the media-capture angle to Austria, using computational content analysis to detect the "Inseratenaffäre" advertising-for-coverage scandal's signature: increased visibility for Kurz and, notably, more negative coverage of his rivals rather than simple favorability inflation. At the European scale, [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate media visibility for Radical Right parties across the 2024 European Parliament elections — a structural pattern independent of outlet orientation or popularity — while [[Gattermann2025-yx]] shows that far-right electoral performance itself shapes voters' disinformation concerns, and [[Karlsson2026-hd]] demonstrates that mere media *narratives* about election interference (regardless of truth or efficacy) can asymmetrically inflame affective polarization depending on which party is portrayed as beneficiary. [[Zhu2026-tn]] rounds out the comparative picture, showing that alternative-mainstream audience overlap is shaped less by fixed "alternativeness" than by media-system type and the institutionalized access of populist parties — producing isolated alternative enclaves (Sweden) versus blurred boundaries (Austria, France). [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s three-country panel study (Germany, Netherlands, Poland) around the 2024 EP elections adds a "hostile misinformation effect": partisans systematically believe their own side is more targeted by misinformation than their rivals, an effect amplified by interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation.

## Elite strategy and the political economy of misinformation

Several papers reject person-level or platform-level accounts of misinformation in favor of elite-strategic ones. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country, 32-million-tweet analysis of parliamentarians finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing — only their *interaction*, radical-right populism, does, tying the phenomenon to a legitimacy crisis of liberal institutions rather than generic platform pathology. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] and [[Bennett2025-xs]] develop complementary institutionalist frameworks: the former's "logic of connective faction" shows how the sudden CRT panic reveals ideologically extreme Republicans building measurable ties to hyper-partisan digital surrogates, rewarded with disproportionate engagement and attention; the latter extends this into a full "communication-as-organization" account of how digital surrogate organizations (QAnon, Stop the Steal networks) pull the GOP toward illiberalism, arguing that technocentric, individual-cognition-focused disinformation research has displaced institutional analysis. [[Tai2026-qk]] narrows this elite lens to institutional and ideological "checks" on officials' own misinformation sharing. Community-based moderation gets a skeptical audit: [[Renault2025-uh]] finds Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under X's crowdsourced Community Notes, undermining claims that bridging-based systems eliminate partisan asymmetry in sanctioning, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] argues this is not incidental but structural — bridging algorithms are designed in a way that systematically undermoderates precisely the most polarizing (and least cross-partisan-agreeable) content, with acute risks during elections.

## Rethinking what "misinformation" and "polarization" even are

A parallel conceptual current insists that treating misinformation as discrete false claims, or polarization as simple attitudinal distance, misses how meaning is collaboratively constructed. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] reframes "fake news" through second-order cybernetics, shifting analytic focus from creators' intent to chains of propagator judgment that produce emergent cascades — a stance echoed in [[Marwick2026-qd]]'s account of disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative* (the Pee Tape, Parkland "crisis actor," Lottery Manure cases) that resists fact-checking precisely because it operates through emotion and genre rather than propositional claims. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop this narratological turn empirically for U.S. election-integrity rumors, showing how "evidence" and "frame" interact through quote-tweet structures and how 2022 rumors relied on audiences' prior internalization of the 2020 "deep story" of fraud — meaning that misinformation cannot be assessed at the level of the single post. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes a structurally similar insight using Greimas' actantial model and an LLM pipeline on Israeli-Palestinian YouTube discourse, finding that videos are sharply polarized in *narrative* terms while comments surprisingly converge on the surface even as deeper "narrative motifs" preserve partisan difference — suggesting narrative polarization is partially orthogonal to audience ideology. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows a related dynamic in the weaponization of the term "fake news" itself by Newsmax and Occupy Democrats, both of which use it not descriptively but to redraw Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance around their opponents. Methodologically adjacent, [[Sarmiento2025-as]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] push framing analysis toward unsupervised and multi-modal detection, arguing that fixed frame taxonomies and text-only methods miss much of what makes coverage partisan.

## Measuring ideology and inference: from ideal points to LLMs

A cross-cutting methodological thread concerns how to *measure* partisan alignment and audience composition at scale. [[Green2025-ap]]'s "curation bubbles" concept is pivotal here: domain-level partisan audience scores systematically mask story-level heterogeneity, especially for moderate-scoring outlets, meaning conventional source-level polarization measures understate the networked, story-unbundling reality of how content circulates — a critique Gaisbauer2025-px (Gaisbauer2025-by) formalizes into a call for multi-level "political cartography" combining story, outlet, and content dimensions. [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent ideology scaling from users and outlets to individual YouTube videos via Reddit cross-posting, while [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows LLMs can be prompted-and-averaged to reliably position political texts across languages and genres, and [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical text by exploiting subtly politicized cultural cues — raising the privacy and micro-targeting stakes this whole research program eventually confronts. Two papers introduce productive friction into the LLM-as-measurement-tool narrative: [[Paci2025-ag]] shows current LLMs still struggle badly to interpret implicit, manipulative content (implicature, presupposition) in Italian political speech, and [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that LLM persuasiveness on political misconceptions collapses by roughly a quarter once users are told the model is biased against their party — underscoring that perceived neutrality, not just capability, gates AI's political influence. [[Mosleh2024-op]] adds an important scope condition to all single-platform inferences, showing partisan and low-quality news engagement diverges markedly across seven platforms, cautioning against Twitter/Facebook-centric generalizations that much of this literature (including many papers above) still risks.

## Affect, emotion, and the demand side of engagement

Running beneath algorithmic and elite-strategic accounts is a body of work on the emotional economy of partisan content. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] both find, across Scandinavian contexts, that negative/angry content reliably drives shares and comments while positive content drives likes — a distinction [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review of Facebook Reactions research generalizes, showing sociopolitical content elicits broader, more negative emotional profiles than lifestyle content, with anger a consistent engagement driver for populist and far-right actors. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] shows a parallel dynamic in expert-sourcing: UK newspapers' selective citation of Great Barrington versus John Snow Memorandum signatories tracks outlet political orientation even among mainstream, non-fringe scientists, illustrating how polarization can colonize science communication without requiring "misinformation" in any strict sense. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s case study of #JeNeSuisPasCharlie shows dissenting counter-hashtags can carve out nuanced positions even amid mass solidarity movements, an early illustration of how insularity and contestation coexist on the same platform event.

## Toward diagnosis, intervention, and disciplinary reflexivity

The collection closes on questions of what, if anything, can or should be done. [[Esau2025-tf]] offers a normative-theoretical response, proposing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as antidotes to "destructive" (as opposed to healthy, agonistic) polarization, explicitly trying to bridge deliberation and polarization research where [[van-Eck2026-xg]] found only a loose, evidence-disconnected coupling. [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s 16-year study of political Reddit complicates any easy intervention story by showing that cross-ideological overlap is minimal even between discursively similar subreddits — polarization here is "sedimented" through an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse rather than attributable to any single lever. [[Oswald2025-km]] adds a further caution for both interveners and researchers: the visible "iceberg tip" of online political content is produced by a small hyperactive minority, distorting inferences about public opinion that both citizens and scholars draw from social media traces — a methodological warning that, together with [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]'s earlier essay on the "three consequences of big data" (blurred organizational boundaries, micro-targeting, strategic amplification), and [[Kristensen2025-ni]]'s open question about alternative platforms' role in discursive polarization, frames the entire research program's central unresolved task: understanding polarization not as a fixed empirical object but as an unstable, platform-, method-, and context-contingent phenomenon that keeps outrunning the tools built to study it.
