# LLMs & Computational Text Analysis for Political Discourse

## The validation core: LLMs-in-the-loop as a methodological problem

The anchor of this collection is a pair of companion papers built around the same empirical case — Italian political news shared on Facebook before the 2018 and 2022 elections. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] runs a narrow, well-specified benchmark: does OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large outperform the Italian-specific UmBERTo for unsupervised clustering of political news, using a Grimmer-and-King-style coherence metric adjudicated by a fine-tuned GPT-4o-mini? It does, consistently, across years and clustering algorithms. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] zooms out from this single comparison to the pipeline as a whole — classification, embedding, and cluster-labeling — and argues that fully LLM-integrated pipelines need bespoke, multi-stage validation protocols rather than a single evaluation borrowed from fine-tuned-transformer practice, because LLM outputs are general-purpose, variably granular, and often exceed the competence of crowdworkers who would traditionally serve as ground truth. Read together, these two papers model a validation logic — task-specific benchmarks nested inside a documented pipeline — that recurs, in different guises, across nearly every other paper in this set. [[Marino2026-he]] extends the same GPT-4o classification logic longitudinally to a Brazilian hyperpartisan Facebook network, showing that a validated content classifier can support not just static description but multi-year, event-sensitive time-series analysis of affective engagement.

[[Balluff2026-if]] supplies the critical counterweight to this validation-first optimism: it catalogues the reproducibility, environmental, and epistemic costs of routine LLM adoption in communication research — opaque corporate updates that silently break replication, guardrails that suppress politically sensitive content, and the availability of cheaper encoder-only alternatives for many text-analysis tasks — arguing for a trade-off mindset in which LLM use is justified rather than assumed. [[Tan2024-vl]] situates the same practice within the broader NLP literature on LLM-based annotation and synthesis, mapping generation, assessment, and downstream learning strategies as a coherent methodological subfield, and thereby giving the validation exercises here (Giglietto, Marino, Brown) a disciplinary home.

## Cross-national and cross-linguistic validation exercises

A cluster of papers explicitly tests whether LLM-based measurement generalizes across languages and national contexts — the collaborative-validation ambition named in the topic description. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows that simply asking instruction-tuned LLMs to place texts on ideological dimensions and averaging many responses recovers established benchmark positions across US, UK, and multilingual EU political texts. [[Waight2025-al]] tackles a harder estimand — narrative similarity, not just topical or lexical overlap — with an LLM pipeline that distills claims and subjects before pairwise comparison, validated against hand-coded gold standards and applied to trace the cross-language diffusion of Russian "biolabs" narratives from state media into low-quality US outlets, a phenomenon invisible to exact-text-reuse methods. [[Fan2025-ut]] addresses a related but distinct problem at the representation level: pretrained embeddings encode confounds like language and source that bias similarity measures, and linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove this signal with dramatic gains in cross-lingual retrieval (e.g., Recall@1 from 0.175 to 0.826) without degrading out-of-distribution performance — a general-purpose fix that complements the embedding-choice question posed by [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]]. [[Iris2026-pg]] operationalizes cross-national validation empirically rather than methodologically, using ChatGPT-4o entity extraction plus fuzzy matching and manual checks across five European countries to show a structural overrepresentation of Radical Right actors in 2024 European election coverage — a substantive finding made possible only by a validated multilingual extraction pipeline. [[Nenno2025-xa]] similarly scales computational news-values detection across twenty-four WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, cautioning that typologies built on Western media may not travel, a warning that resonates with Balluff's point about LLMs' Western-skewed training distributions.

## Interrogating what LLMs actually capture: bias, pragmatics, and inference

Several papers probe the internal validity of LLM annotation more skeptically. [[Brown2025-jk]] finds that demographic disagreement between LLM and human annotators on contentious labeling tasks is small and dataset-specific rather than systematically model-biased, with item difficulty (label entropy) dwarfing demographic effects — a reassuring but conditional result that qualifies fairness critiques of LLM annotation. [[Paci2025-ag]] complicates the picture from the other direction: on Italian political speech requiring pragmatic inference (implicature, presupposition), even the best model falls twenty points short of a human ceiling, showing that surface-level classification competence does not guarantee genuine interpretive competence — a caution directly relevant to any pipeline, like Marino2024's cluster-labeling stage, that asks LLMs to summarize or interpret political meaning rather than merely classify it. [[Lee2026-je]] flips the validity question into a risk: LLMs can infer users' political alignment not only from explicit political talk but from culturally coded, ostensibly apolitical vocabulary, outperforming supervised baselines and raising the same kind of privacy concern that Balluff raises about ethics review lagging LLM capability. [[Waight2026-ts]] pushes concerns about embedded bias to the geopolitical level, showing that state-coordinated media is memorized into training corpora and shifts commercial model outputs toward pro-regime framings depending on prompt language — a finding that should give pause to any classification pipeline relying on off-the-shelf multilingual models for politically sensitive content, especially in the low-media-freedom contexts flagged by Iris2026 and Nenno2025.

## Scaling classification and annotation across applied domains

The largest cluster of papers demonstrates validated LLM/embedding pipelines put to substantive use. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa (rather than prompting a generalist LLM) to classify collective-action frames in over 500,000 Proud Boys Telegram messages, coupling this classifier to Granger-causality models of online-offline mobilization — an example of supervised fine-tuning as an alternative validation path to the prompting approaches used elsewhere. [[Larsson2026-ro]] uses GPT-4 zero-shot classification with human validation to code a decade of Norwegian-language Facebook sentiment, explicitly framing the exercise as a methodological demonstration of LLM-assisted content analysis in a non-English language, paralleling Giglietto and Marino's Italian-language work. [[Kim2026-br]] builds an explainable hierarchical classifier with span-level rationales to detect two decades of suspected state-linked troll activity in Korean news comments, emphasizing auditability as a validation criterion in its own right. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes a Greimasian actantial model via an open-weight LLM (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B) to measure "narrative polarisation" in YouTube discourse on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, reporting LLM-human agreement comparable to human-human agreement — again treating inter-coder reliability as the validation benchmark. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] and [[Arminio2025-tw]] extend validation into the visual domain: GPT-4o substantially outperforms specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person-counting in German campaign imagery, while a VLLM-to-text-to-embedding pipeline captures connotative meaning in climate-change Instagram images that CNN-based clustering misses, trading a little denotative coherence for interpretability via TF-IDF keyword summaries. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] both push framing analysis away from fixed, predefined categories — toward multi-modal and fully unsupervised frame discovery, respectively — continuing the same logic of letting LLM/embedding representations surface structure that supervised, closed-vocabulary approaches would miss. [[Lai2024-to]] and [[Eady2025-vm]] show that ideology can be measured without any LLM annotation at all, via homophily in sharing behavior (Reddit-YouTube links, Twitter URL-sharing) — a useful reminder that LLM-based measurement should be judged against, and can be triangulated with, established behavioral latent-scaling methods.

## Fact-checking, epistemics, and the reliability ceiling

A subset of papers stress-tests LLMs specifically on veracity tasks central to political discourse. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] finds that standard and even reasoning-enabled LLMs fact-check PolitiFact claims poorly, that built-in web search yields only moderate gains, and that curated retrieval-augmented generation is the decisive lever, improving macro F1 by 233% on average — while search-enabled models systematically cite credible but left-leaning sources, a bias finding that echoes Waight2026's demonstration of geopolitical skew and Iris2026's evidence of a rightward *visibility* skew in mainstream coverage, together suggesting that ideological asymmetries can run in different directions depending on where in the pipeline they are measured. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] steps back to ask what "fact" even means for generative outputs, proposing "emergent facts" as a category that existing evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based fact typologies cannot accommodate — a conceptual companion to DeVerna's empirical demonstration that internal LLM knowledge is an unreliable substitute for curated evidence. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a large-scale case study of what happens when this problem is institutionalized at scale: Grokipedia is shown, via embedding-based similarity and citation analysis, to be a heavily derivative but selectively rewritten fork of Wikipedia that cites far more unreliable and blacklisted sources, especially on controversial and political topics — essentially an applied test of the fact-checking and bias concerns raised abstractly by Dierickx and empirically by DeVerna and Waight.

## Persuasion, misinformation interventions, and their double edge

A distinct thread validates LLMs not as classifiers but as interlocutors capable of shifting political beliefs, and interrogates the mechanisms and boundary conditions of that persuasion. [[Costello2024-bg]] shows that personalized, evidence-dense AI dialogue durably reduces conspiracy belief; [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] generalizes this into a systematic audit of persuasion "levers" across nineteen LLMs, identifying information density as the central mechanism and — crucially — a persuasion-accuracy trade-off that qualifies Costello's optimism (the same techniques that persuade also degrade factual reliability, connecting back to DeVerna's accuracy findings). [[Lin2025-xp]] replicates and extends these effects across four national elections, finding AI persuasion driven mainly by facts and evidence rather than manipulation, but also documenting a partisan asymmetry in claim accuracy that echoes DeVerna's and Waight's asymmetric-bias findings. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complicates the whole picture by showing that perceived political bias in the messenger — not the content itself — can suppress persuasive uptake, implying that the ideological skews documented elsewhere in this set (DeVerna, Waight, Iris2026) may themselves undermine the practical persuasive and corrective power that Costello and Lin document. [[Szabo2026-rd]] applies the same conversational-AI logic prophylactically, building an LLM chatbot for "conversational inoculation" against misinformation and finding it outperforms static reading/writing interventions once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled.

## Media reception, disclosure, and governance of LLM-mediated content

The final thread turns from producing/validating LLM analyses to studying how audiences and institutions respond to LLM-generated or LLM-labeled content, and how platform governance shapes what data is even available for validation. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] both run disclosure experiments on AI-generated or AI-assisted news, finding that quality perceptions are largely unaffected by undisclosed AI authorship but that disclosure itself depresses trust (with task-specific and topic-specific variation), a reception-side counterpart to the production-side validation work elsewhere. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] examines how news media themselves frame LLMs, finding mentalistic-agentic language is present but not dominant and varies by outlet rather than region — a meta-level study of the very "AI imaginaries" that condition audience reactions documented by Gilardi and Mattis. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] takes the widest lens, arguing LLM-enabled bridging, collective-dialogue, and moderation systems could reshape digital public squares for better or worse, functioning as a normative synthesis of concerns threaded through Waight2026, Iris2026, and the persuasion papers. [[Peters2026-mo]] and [[Allen2025-ot]] close the loop methodologically: as platforms restrict API access, both the political contestation over what counts as adequate "data quality" under the EU's Digital Services Act and the emergence of platform-independent, LLM-powered browser-extension experiments illustrate that the validation exercises catalogued throughout this note are not merely technical choices but are constrained, and increasingly enabled, by the shifting data-access regimes that platforms and regulators control. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]], reviewing the pre-LLM literature on Facebook Reactions as affective signals, serves as a reminder of the baseline computational-social-science toolkit that this entire body of work is now extending, validating, and in places supplanting with LLM- and embedding-based methods.
