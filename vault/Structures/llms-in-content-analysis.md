# LLMs & Computational Methods for Content Analysis

## Validating LLMs as classifiers and annotators

The methodological core of this collection asks a deceptively simple question: can large language models replace or augment human coders for political text classification? A cluster of papers answers empirically, task by task. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to classify collective-action frames across 500,000 Proud Boys messages, embedding the classifier within a Granger-causality design that links online framing to offline violence — a template for how validated classification can support substantive causal inference. [[Meher2025-qb]] extends this logic to conflict event coding, showing that QLoRA-based fine-tuning of Llama 3.1 dramatically outperforms zero-shot prompting on GTD attack-type classification, with gains concentrated in rare categories, while remaining tractable on consumer hardware. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Marino2026-he]] demonstrate GPT-4-based classification pipelines (validated against human coders) applied longitudinally to Facebook sentiment and affective-engagement dynamics, showing both the payoff — multi-year, multi-million-post analyses previously infeasible — and the need for careful validation when classifiers are deployed over evolving corpora. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] complicates the picture for a harder task, political fact-checking: general-purpose LLMs, even with reasoning or web search, perform poorly without curated retrieval, and search-enabled models exhibit a left-leaning citation skew, underscoring that "validation" must be task- and pipeline-specific rather than a property of the base model alone. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes a lighter-weight alternative to full classification — prompting-and-averaging to place texts on policy/ideological dimensions — validated across languages and benchmarks, while [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys this rapidly expanding methodological space of LLM-based annotation and synthesis more broadly.

## Embeddings, representations, and what they encode

A second strand treats embeddings themselves as objects of methodological scrutiny. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against the Italian BERT model UmBERTo for clustering political news, finding the LLM-derived embedding consistently more semantically coherent — a finding with direct implications for non-English computational social science. [[Fan2025-ut]] goes further methodologically, showing that pretrained embeddings encode spurious confounders (source, language) that bias similarity-based measures, and that linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove this contamination without degrading downstream performance, effectively proposing a default preprocessing step for any embedding-based text-as-data pipeline. [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent-ideology scaling — long used for legislators and media outlets — to video content via Reddit sharing patterns, illustrating how embedding/latent-variable techniques travel across units of analysis. Strikingly, [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] shows that such representational structure is not confined to research pipelines: X's own recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear ideology dimension in its user embeddings, which the authors show can be identified and surgically removed — a direct bridge between academic embedding methodology and platform accountability.

## Multimodal and qualitative extensions

Several papers push validation beyond text. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] finds that GPT-4o substantially outperforms specialized computer-vision pipelines (RetinaFace, Google Cloud Vision) for face recognition and person-counting in campaign imagery, while [[Arminio2025-tw]] shows that vision-language models produce connotatively richer, more interpretable image clusters than CNN-based pipelines by routing images through textual description before embedding — reframing "semantic" clustering around socially meaningful connotation rather than denotative object recognition. [[Arora2025-tx]] argues the same logic should extend to framing analysis itself, proposing multi-modal frame extraction that captures editorial choices invisible to text-only approaches. On the qualitative side, [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] both develop human-in-the-loop workflows — integrating topic modeling, LLM labeling, and iterative human revision — but draw different lessons: Ober emphasizes topic modeling's transparency as a check on LLM opacity, while Gomez-Zara reframes the LLM not as classifier but as an "analytical collaborator" whose divergences from human codes expose implicit theoretical assumptions in a framing codebook, making ambiguity itself a generative methodological resource. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] ties these threads together in a reflexive account of building a three-stage LLM-in-the-loop pipeline (classification, embedding, labeling) for Italian political Facebook data, arguing that fully LLM-integrated pipelines demand bespoke, multi-level validation protocols rather than reliance on crowdsourced benchmarks, since LLMs now often outperform low-skilled human raters.

## The limits of LLM competence and the problem of embedded bias

Validation work also surfaces where LLMs fail or introduce subtle distortions. [[Paci2025-ag]] shows that even strong multilingual models struggle to interpret implicatures and presuppositions in authentic Italian political speech, falling well short of expert human interpretation despite chain-of-thought prompting — a caution against assuming pragmatic competence from benchmark performance on artificial stimuli. [[Brown2025-jk]] offers a more reassuring but equally important finding: across four contentious annotation datasets, demographic bias in LLM labeling is real but small and dataset-specific, while item difficulty (human label entropy) is by far the dominant predictor of LLM-human disagreement — suggesting fairness audits should foreground task difficulty over model or prompt selection. More unsettling are two papers showing that LLMs are not passive instruments but carriers of political signal. [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates that LLMs can infer users' political alignment from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse via subtle cultural cues, outperforming supervised baselines — a capability with clear utility for text-as-data research but equally clear surveillance implications. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows the signal runs the other direction too: state-coordinated media is memorized in training corpora and measurably shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence in low-media-freedom languages, meaning the "neutral" instrument used for coding may itself be politically contaminated. [[Elfes2026-jb]] uses an LLM (DeepSeek-R1) to operationalize Greimas's actantial model for narrative polarization analysis, validating LLM-human agreement on narrative coding while using the method to reveal convergence/divergence dynamics between video content and comments on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict — a case where the LLM is simultaneously a validated coding tool and a probe into exactly the kind of politically-inflected narrative structure that Waight and Lee warn can distort such tools.

## Fact-checking, misinformation, and the epistemics of AI-generated content

A dense cluster examines LLMs' role in truth-adjacent tasks. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] (again) and [[Triedman2025-uy]] both document epistemic risks at platform scale: Triedman's audit of Grokipedia finds it heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet citing far more low-quality and blacklisted sources, especially on controversial and biographical topics — a case study in how generative rewriting can degrade sourcing even while appearing comprehensive. [[Miller2026-cp]] offers a contrasting, more optimistic design case: X's Community Notes AI Note Writer API shows AI-generated notes are additive rather than substitutive for human fact-checking, with human raters retaining final authority — an institutional answer to the governance risks Triedman identifies. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] steps back to ask what "a fact" even means for generative AI, proposing "emergent facts" as a category distinct from evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based facts used in traditional fact-checking epistemology. [[Nenno2025-xa]] applies computational news-values detection across 24 countries to perceived misinformation, finding mostly small but systematic differences between WEIRD and non-WEIRD contexts — a reminder that validated computational constructs (like news values) may not travel cleanly across cultural contexts. [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Costello2024-bg]] shift from detection to intervention, showing LLM-powered conversational inoculation and personalized dialogue can durably reduce misinformation and conspiracy belief, while [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that disclosed AI authorship of news boosts short-term engagement without changing quality perceptions or long-term willingness to read AI journalism — curiosity rather than endorsement.

## Persuasion, transparency, and the political stakes of conversational AI

A final, closely related strand treats LLMs not as coding tools but as political actors whose persuasive capacities must themselves be measured and governed. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically decomposes the "levers" of AI persuasion, identifying information density and post-training as more important than scale or personalization, and documenting a persuasion-accuracy trade-off. [[Lin2025-xp]] extends this to real elections across four countries, finding conversational persuasion effects exceeding traditional advertising, driven by facts rather than manipulation, though right-leaning advocacy produced systematically less accurate claims. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] both probe the boundary conditions of this persuasive power: perceived partisan bias in the model sharply attenuates persuasion, while disclosing persuasive *intent* (not mere AI identity) roughly halves it — together suggesting that transparency and perceived neutrality, not model capability, are the binding constraints on AI persuasion. [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows these effects generalize across structurally distinct issues (climate, vaccines, inequality) and are strongest precisely among skeptics, while [[Goldberg2026-eb]] situates all of this within a broader vision of AI-mediated "digital public squares," cataloguing bridging systems, collective dialogue tools, and AI-assisted moderation as technologies that could reshape deliberation for better or worse.

## Reflexive critique: validating the validators

The collection closes the loop with explicitly reflexive contributions. [[Balluff2026-if]] directly challenges the uncritical adoption documented throughout this literature, arguing that convenience often obscures reproducibility problems, corporate dependency, environmental costs, and bias — and that smaller, open, task-appropriate models frequently rival LLMs at far lower cost, a direct counterpoint to papers like [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] and [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] that document LLM superiority. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] turns the analytical lens on the discourse *about* LLMs themselves, finding that mentalistic-agentic framing in news coverage is real but neither dominant nor uniform, shaped more by newsroom practice than by techno-cultural geography. Read together, this topic traces an arc from narrow technical validation (can GPT-4o count faces, classify frames, cluster embeddings reliably?) toward an increasingly reflexive recognition that the instruments of computational content analysis are themselves political objects — carrying embedded biases, persuasive power, and epistemic assumptions that must be interrogated with the same rigor historically reserved for the texts they are used to analyze.
