# Platform Policy & Content Visibility

## The anchor case: Meta's political content reduction policy

The topic's centre of gravity is [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], which turns Meta's own DSA-mandated Content Library into an instrument for auditing the company's political content reduction policy in Italy. Its breakpoint-detection design shows the policy silently in effect roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout, a 72% collapse in reach for re-elected MPs, only partial recovery after the January 2025 reversal, and — most consequentially — that extremist accounts absorbed the per-post penalty by posting far more often, ending up with *more* total weekly reach than before. This single paper crystallizes the topic's stakes: algorithmic visibility policy is not neutral demotion but a redistributive mechanism whose winners and losers depend on how actors adapt. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends the same evidentiary logic to the US using Meta's URL Shares data, showing that partisan-audience content is systematically penalized in reach while professionally-sourced content is rewarded, and that these penalties/rewards fluctuate sharply around known governance moments (the 2020 "break the glass" period), which the authors read as proof that Facebook is an *active curator*, not a passive conduit — directly complicating the "neutral platform" self-narrative examined discursively in [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]]. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] complements this from the moderation-outcomes side: contra Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors, actual removal after verification is rare (~30%), and fact-checkers overwhelmingly prefer labeling and reduced reach over deletion — a preference now stress-tested by Meta's shift to Community Notes, which [[Holt2026-zq]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] both show has structural weaknesses: user reports track political disagreement more than falsity, and bridging-based systems undermoderate precisely the polarizing content most likely to need it.

## Causal evidence of algorithmic effects, and its limits

A second cluster asks, more generally, whether feed and recommendation algorithms cause the political effects long alleged of them. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] is the standout: a field experiment on X showing that switching users onto the algorithmic feed shifts attitudes rightward and that this effect persists even after the algorithm is switched off, because users are induced to *follow* new accounts — a mechanism that reconciles the algorithm's apparent null effects in Meta's 2020 studies with its evident political power. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complicates simple "right-wing bias" narratives on Musk-era Twitter by showing the effect is mediated by agitation and proximity to Musk rather than ideology per se, while [[Brown2026-br]] finds essentially the opposite pattern on YouTube — no independent echo-chamber or radicalization effect, but strong "rabbit hole" dynamics and a platform-wide conservative tilt applied uniformly. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] shows X's recommender inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear ideology signal in its embedding space, raising profiling concerns distinct from behavioral bias. [[Brady2026-ln]] pushes the causal-experiment tradition toward intervention, demonstrating that engagement-based ranking distorts perceived social norms around political hostility and that a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can partially repair this without harming enjoyment — a rare demonstration that visibility governance is *tractable* by design, not just diagnosable. Against this experimental confidence stand important null and cautionary results: [[Allcott2025-jb]] and [[Bakshy2015-rn]] (the Meta-partnered 2020 studies) found limited or modest algorithmic effects on political attitudes and exposure, and [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a sharp meta-scientific critique arguing that even this apex of platform-cooperative science lacks temporal and conceptual validity — platforms change faster than social science can generalize, which is precisely why [[Bechmann2026-dr]] calls for reframing platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure rather than chasing causal point-estimates.

## Knowing what platforms do: infrastructure, access, and its distortions

A recurring meta-theme is that visibility research is bottlenecked by who can see platform data and how. [[Pierri2025-hm]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bruns2026-pn]] together diagnose a research-access crisis: Article 40 of the DSA promises vetted access but delivers asymmetric, industry-dominated "clean rooms" (the Meta Content Library chief among them) that structurally favor well-resourced, code-fluent, Global-North quantitative researchers over qualitative, comparative, or Majority-World scholarship — echoing [[Tonneau2025-bv]]'s finding that moderation workforce itself is starkly skewed against Global South languages. [[McNally2025-dn]] pushes back on the "black box" framing directly, showing that Facebook's undisclosed News Feed changes leave detectable time-series fingerprints in news engagement, which argues for systemic algorithmic auditing under DSA Article 40(4) rather than one-off access grants. [[Jurg2025-ur]] performs exactly this kind of audit for YouTube's 2024 EU election moderation, finding inconsistent application of authority labels and vague removal communications — evidence that transparency mandates so far produce "observability" rather than accountability. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] show that independent, crowdsourced, and conceptual auditing predates the DSA and remains necessary precisely because platform self-reporting (critiqued discursively in [[Hurcombe2025-cs]]) cannot be trusted at face value.

## Visibility of extremism, incivility, and the far right

Several papers examine what algorithmic visibility does to fringe and extremist actors specifically, converging on the finding that moderation regimes struggle against adaptive, vernacular content strategies. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist "everyday extremism" on TikTok exploiting keyword-evasion and platform vernacular; [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows Andrew Tate's YouTube visibility *increasing* post-deplatforming via a decentralized "Tate-space" that outpaces removal; [[Nangle2026-yo]] describes far-right Instagram content using humor and "algorithmic proximity" to mainstream extremist ideas; and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] finds Filipino Gen Z TikTok culture normalizing incivility through "dogshows." Read alongside [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]'s finding that extremists gained aggregate reach under Meta's political-content policy, these studies suggest a structural pattern: blunt, category-based visibility interventions (demotion, deplatforming) are systematically less effective against adaptive fringe actors than against mainstream political communicators, who lack comparable evasion incentives or capacity. [[Iris2026-pg]] adds a mainstream-media dimension, showing radical-right parties received disproportionate news visibility across the 2024 EU elections independent of polling strength — a reminder that algorithmic and editorial visibility biases can point the same direction even via different mechanisms.

## State power and platform exit

[[Ventura2026-yc]] and [[Bruns2026-yv]] examine visibility consequences when platforms are banned or degraded outright. Brazil's 2024 X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users disproportionately circumvented it — a cautionary tale for state-led content interventions parallel to the algorithmic ones above. Bruns's account of Twitter's "enshittification" into X, and the underwhelming governance of successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky), suggests visibility politics is now also a story about platform succession, not just single-platform policy tweaks. [[Crosset2026-mq]] supplies a comparative-legal frame for this, showing EU and US content legislation instantiate three distinct regimes (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation) that converge on "security bureaucratisation" via transparency and audit requirements rather than direct content control — theoretically grounding why DSA-style transparency mandates (as seen throughout this topic) take the administrative form they do. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] extends this regulatory-design lens to dark patterns and DSA Article 25, showing how HCI concepts can be operationalized for enforcement, including against X's verification design.

## Is "social media" still the right unit of analysis?

A final cluster steps back to ask whether the visibility questions above are being asked of a vanishing object. [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]], [[Tornberg2026-lc]], and [[Helmond2026-ll]] each argue, from different angles, that the platforms shaping political visibility today are no longer "social" in the reciprocal, networked-publics sense that grounded two decades of scholarship: boyd proposes "parasocial media," Gerbaudo theorizes "clustered publics" replacing networked ones, Törnberg and coauthors describe a splintering into algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private spheres, and AI-mediated communication, and Helmond reframes platformization itself as entering an AI-infrastructural phase symbolized by the death of the Like button. [[Swartz2026-zb]] adds that scam logics are becoming co-constitutive with these platform economies, further destabilizing the "authentic user" assumption underlying earlier visibility research. This matters directly for the topic: audits like [[Gauthier2026-iq]], [[Bouchaud2026-lr]], and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] are measuring the last visible remnants of network-based visibility just as platforms pivot toward interest-based, AI-mediated curation — a transition [[Goldberg2026-eb]] frames optimistically as an opportunity to redesign public squares (via bridging systems, collective dialogue tools) and [[Richter2026-bt]] traces genealogically through a decade of Twitter AI discourse that shaped today's divergent US/German regulatory postures.

## Downstream consequences: trust, disinformation, and the media ecosystem

Underlying all of this is the open question of what visibility policy is *for*. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] track mis/disinformation prevalence on European platforms directly, finding TikTok's exposure share rising and persistent engagement advantages for low-credibility accounts — the outcome-level counterpart to the mechanism-level studies above. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] supplies the longest possible baseline, showing declining trust in news predates the internet but accelerated with digital platformization, complicating any simple causal story linking algorithmic visibility to the "trust crisis." [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] and [[Dodds2026-df]] extend this into the AI era, finding audiences trust AI-mediated search more than AI-involved journalism, and diagnosing AI hype itself as a structuring force in journalism's coverage of the very platforms this topic studies. [[Farkas2026-lr]] shows fact-checkers navigating the resulting legitimacy bind, rhetorically defending platform funding as a necessary evil. [[Balluff2026-bv]], [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]], and [[Votta2025-xz]] round out the picture with adjacent visibility-shaping mechanisms — paid media capture, algorithmic health-news gatekeeping, and ad-delivery cost dynamics — that operate alongside, and sometimes independently of, the feed-ranking policies at this topic's core. [[Kristensen2025-ni]], [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]], [[Moran2025-qn]], [[Donovan2025-ws]], [[Volpe2026-um]], and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] each supply further texture — on alternative-platform polarization, democratic backsliding, the collapsing trust-and-safety profession, the history of "misinformation-at-scale," influencer labor under algorithmic pressure, and AI-driven scam ecosystems respectively — situating Meta's 2026 policy controversy within a much longer and more structurally embedded story about who gets seen, and why, on platforms that increasingly govern by infrastructure rather than by rule.
