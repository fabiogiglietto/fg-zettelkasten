# Disinformation Narrative Monitoring

## The narrative turn in disinformation research

A dominant thread running through this collection is a conceptual pivot away from treating disinformation as discrete false claims and toward understanding it as *narrative* — recurring stories that travel across platforms, genres, and borders. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes this case most explicitly, arguing that disinformation succeeds because it functions as identity-affirming, cathartic storytelling signaled by catchphrases and visual shorthand, not because it withstands fact-checking. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes the theoretical apparatus further, borrowing from Ricoeur and narratology to argue that disinformation studies has used "narrative" naively; he proposes a hermeneutic-realist, ethics-sensitive framework that can condemn a story as misleading even when its individual claims are technically true. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes this empirically, showing survey evidence that cumulative exposure to a recurring narrative — rather than any single claim — drives belief. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Goel2025-iq]] converge on a related insight from different angles: misleadingness often emerges not from fabricated facts but from how true evidence is *framed* (Starbird's evidence-frame model of election-fraud rumors) or *repurposed* from mainstream journalism into movement narratives (Goel's co-sharing analysis). Together these papers justify the PROMPT project's core premise — that monitoring must track narratives, not just claims or domains.

## Measuring and modeling narrative spread

A second cluster addresses the methodological challenge of actually detecting and quantifying narratives at scale. [[Waight2025-al]] is the technical anchor here, proposing an LLM-based pipeline (claim distillation plus pairwise comparison) that outperforms text-reuse, topic modeling, and semantic-role approaches at capturing true narrative similarity, demonstrated on the Russian "biolabs" story's diffusion from state media into low-quality and even mainstream U.S. outlets. [[Scalco2026-bd]] offers a complementary demand-side lens, detecting "information voids" — supply/demand imbalances across social media and search — that predict reduced credible content and increased misinformation uptake, as seen in COVID vaccine discourse. [[Rogers2026-cy]], from the sister vera.ai project, contributes a mapping methodology for "post-truth spaces," combining network analysis and provenance investigation to locate influential problematic clusters, demonstrated on Moldovan Facebook discourse around the Ukraine war — a direct precedent for PROMPT's dashboard ambitions. [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] narrows in on real-time coordinated-bot detection on Bluesky as an infrastructural piece of the same monitoring puzzle, while [[Vincent_undated-re]] provides blunt, repeated cross-platform prevalence measurement (TikTok's rising mis/disinformation share, persistent low-credibility engagement advantage) that illustrates why continuous monitoring dashboards are needed in the first place.

## War, elections, and the thematic battlegrounds

Several papers ground these conceptual and methodological concerns in the specific narrative battlegrounds PROMPT tracks. On the Ukraine war, [[Waight2025-al]]'s biolabs case, [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s analysis of TikTok sound as a propaganda vector, [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s dual-audience model of Russian wartime propaganda, and [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s Moldova case jointly sketch how a single geopolitical narrative fragments and mutates across platforms and national information spaces. On elections, [[Starbird2025-jj]], [[Rossini2026-mj]], and [[Rodarte2026-dk]] examine, respectively, U.S. midterm rumor dynamics, how belief in electoral misinformation erodes trust in Brazil's 2022 election even as mainstream news use rebuilds it, and how Brazilian parliamentarians themselves wage narrative contests during crises — collectively showing that elite behavior and citizen sensemaking are two faces of the same narrative economy. [[Tai2026-qk]] adds a systematic look at what institutional and ideological "checks" constrain elite misinformation-sharing, while [[Ramos2026-qo]], written by Maldita.es fact-checkers, offers a ground-level case (the Valencia DANA floods conspiracy) showing exactly how a narrative mutates through escalating claims and argumentative fallacies — and proposes "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" as necessary complements to fact-checking, a position that reads almost as a manifesto for PROMPT-style tooling.

## Platforms, propaganda, and the political economy of amplification

A further set of papers examines *why* narratives spread the way they do once released into platform ecosystems. [[Copland2025-em]] traces how Sky News Australia operationalizes a dual-platform strategy to seed conspiracist content into the transnational right-wing propaganda pipeline, exemplifying Benkler et al.'s "network propaganda." [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] turns the lens on the platforms themselves, showing how Meta's Newsroom discursively externalizes blame for "problematic content" while claiming competence to solve it — a framing that shapes the regulatory environment fact-checking tools must operate within. [[Bennett2018-ys]] and [[Freelon2020-yp]] supply the foundational theory for this whole area: Bennett and Livingston's "disinformation order" positions strategic disinformation as a symptom of institutional legitimacy crisis rather than an isolated media-technological glitch, while Freelon and Wells's special-issue introduction codifies the definitional distinctions (disinformation vs. misinformation vs. fake news) that much of the later literature either adopts or productively complicates. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] offers an important corrective to creator-intent-centric definitions, modeling false-information circulation as an emergent, multi-level cascade shaped by propagators' judgments — a cybernetic framing that anticipates the narrative-as-circulating-object view later papers embrace. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] rounds this out by noting that manipulation campaigns persist by opportunistically appropriating new events, reinforcing the case for longitudinal, narrative-level (rather than claim-level) monitoring.

## Fact-checkers under pressure: practice, legitimacy, and LLMs

The final arc concerns the fact-checking institutions PROMPT's dashboards are built to serve, and the tools now reshaping their work. [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] both examine fact-checkers' precarious institutional position — the former analyzing how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform funding against charges of compromised independence, the latter documenting what actually happens to debunked content post-Zuckerberg's abandonment of Meta's fact-checking program, finding removal is rare and Community Notes is viewed skeptically. [[Xue2025-bp]] probes a related tension at the level of communicative strategy: fact-checks themselves are often emotionally charged, complicating the field's claim to dispassionate objectivity. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] provides a sobering technical assessment directly relevant to any LLM-based monitoring pipeline: general-purpose and even reasoning- or search-augmented LLMs perform poorly at political fact-checking unless given curated retrieval context, and search-enabled models show a left-leaning citation skew — a cautionary finding for any system (including PROMPT's) that leans on LLMs for claim verification or narrative tracking. Finally, [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames the entire research program's geographic blind spot, calling for comparative, cross-platform work beyond WEIRD contexts — a reminder that a pan-European monitoring infrastructure like PROMPT's is itself an attempt to answer this call, mapping how the same narratives (Ukraine, LGBTQI+ rights, elections) take different shape across national media ecosystems.
