# Digital Research Methods & Ethics

## From data philanthropy to data scarcity: the arc of platform access

The oldest throughline in this literature is a story of contraction. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the inflection point directly: Facebook's 2018 closure of the Pages API ended any terms-of-service-compliant way to study the platform and inaugurated what the field now calls the "post-API age." [[Freelon2024-sc]] extends this into a full periodization—prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation—showing that every subsequent regime, including the academic APIs that briefly felt like progress, has remained contingent on corporate mood and regulatory pressure rather than settled as a right. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] retells the same arc specifically for Twitter/X, from "Wild West" openness through a brief "Golden Age" to Musk's commercial paywall, arguing that this is a structural pattern (echoed by Meta's CrowdTangle closure and Reddit's and TikTok's pricing moves) rather than an idiosyncrasy of one platform. Bastos2025-ya offers the valedictory version of this story, cataloguing the REST, Search, Streaming, Academic, and Compliance APIs that made "Twitter studies" possible and mourning their loss. The scale of what was lost is quantified empirically by [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]], whose bibliometric eulogy finds 25% annual growth in Twitter-based scholarship through 2022 followed by stagnation and a 13% decline by 2024—a decline [[Yang2026-tq]] confirms at the level of the broader social-science literature, showing that the share of papers using any social media data plateaued and fell in 2022–2024 after tripling over the prior decade, concentrated specifically in the collapse of Twitter/X and Facebook as sources. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys the resulting landscape of tools and APIs that researchers are left working with. A quieter but telling coda to this story is [[Wang2026-ub]], which shows that even highly motivated academic communities failed to reconstitute themselves on Mastodon after the Twitter exodus—evidence that platform rupture does not simply redirect research populations elsewhere but can genuinely shrink the space of what is observable.

## The hidden distortions inside the data platforms do provide

A second cluster interrogates not whether access exists but whether what is accessed is trustworthy. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]] both dissect Meta's URL Shares Dataset and show that its 100-public-shares anonymization threshold—not differential-privacy noise—is the dominant source of bias, systematically inflating estimates of news and fake-news consumption and producing suspiciously uniform cross-country trends that likely reflect algorithmic ranking changes rather than social reality. [[Rieder2025-ju]] performs an analogous audit of YouTube's search API, finding it "forgetful by design": a temporal decay curve that makes videos unfindable within weeks, ranking-parameter inconsistency, and non-replicable repeated queries, all of which undermine its use for DSA-mandated systemic-risk research. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] extends this skepticism to web-tracking methodology itself, showing that the ex-situ/in-situ scraping gap—not elapsed time—is the dominant source of measurement error in studies of online news exposure, and that this error is non-randomly distributed across content categories. [[Luhring2025-od]] turns the same critical lens on a widely used input to misinformation research, auditing the NewsGuard database's temporal and cross-country stability and showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can distort findings even when continuous scores are stable. [[Scalco2026-bd]] proposes a complementary diagnostic tool, operationalizing "information voids" as measurable demand-supply imbalances across platforms and search data, demonstrating how longitudinal combination of sources can detect systemic failures of the information ecosystem rather than just describe them post hoc. [[Zheng2026-bi]] addresses a structural blind spot underlying much of this work: the absence of any sanctioned way to draw representative samples from YouTube or TikTok, offering random-sampling infrastructure (TubeStats, TokStats) as a corrective to the field's reliance on opportunistic, popularity-biased samples.

## Recruiting people, not just mining traces

A parallel methodological tradition concerns the survey side of digital research. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] establishes an early template for Facebook ad-based recruitment of hard-to-reach populations, using Pixel tracking and custom-audience exclusion to achieve low-cost, well-documented conversion rates—while also showing the limits of interest-based targeting for reaching ideologically distinctive respondents, since the recruited sample did not differ significantly from a general-population benchmark in conspiracy endorsement. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] generalizes this quality-assurance logic across nine opt-in online panels, documenting a representativeness/response-validity trade-off and showing that simple attention checks can substantially improve data quality without sacrificing representativeness—a finding that directly informs how researchers should evaluate recruitment platforms (including ad-based Facebook samples) for different research purposes.

## Regulation as the new terrain of methods debate

As platform discretion has proven unreliable, the center of gravity has shifted toward regulatory mandates, above all the EU's Digital Services Act. [[Annabell2026-ev]] shows that even the foundational concept of "data" in Article 40 is a contested, undefined "floating signifier," with platforms strategically narrowing it to aggregated, quantified, predefined datasets in ways that marginalize qualitative and humanistic research traditions. [[Peters2026-mo]] documents a parallel struggle over "data quality," largely absent from official DSA texts but pushed into the final Delegated Regulation by academic and NGO advocacy against platform resistance. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA-mandated transparency data itself to expose a substantive inequity—that moderation labor is allocated disproportionately to English over Global South languages—demonstrating both the value and the limits of current transparency regimes. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] turn to the lived experience of working inside these new access regimes: the former shows how Meta's "clean room" environments privilege quantitative, code-based, Global-North research while excluding qualitative and cross-platform work, and the latter, studying CrowdTangle's pre-shutdown years, develops a "seeing like an API" framework to show how technical design and governance rules actively constrain what questions researchers can even conceive of asking. Taken together with [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], this cluster argues that regulatory mandates are a necessary but insufficient response: access must also be *usable*.

## Industry entanglement and the researcher's exposure

A further ethical thread concerns the researcher's structural dependency on the industries being studied. [[Heiss2026-qv]] draws explicit parallels to tobacco, pharma, and food science to argue that social media platforms' monopoly on data about themselves creates unprecedented opportunities for industry influence over findings. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] substantiates this empirically at scale, finding that roughly half of high-profile social media papers in top journals have undisclosed industry ties, that this influence is concentrated among a small set of repeat collaborators who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, and that industry-tied work is systematically skewed toward user-blame topics (e.g., misinformation sharing) over platform-level dynamics. [[Park2026-tr]] widens the ethical lens from funding entanglement to legal jeopardy, documenting through interviews how vague computer-crime and IP statutes chill public-interest research (including social computing work reliant on scraping or ToS violations), with consequences ranging from abandoned projects to prosecution—underscoring that "ethics in computational social science" is inseparable from legal risk management.

## Methods for making sense of what trace data actually contains

Several papers address the analytic, rather than access, side of the pipeline. [[Fan2026-af]] proposes treating digital trace data as temporally ordered "user-sequences" rather than cross-sectional aggregates, surveying six computational approaches to recover the dynamic, path-dependent structure that communication theory has long described but rarely measured. [[Fan2025-ut]] offers a lower-level fix for a pervasive confound, showing that linear concept erasure can remove spurious source/language signals from text embeddings and substantially improve similarity-based analyses without degrading unrelated tasks. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes "practice mapping" via vector embeddings as an alternative to conventional, hairball-prone network visualization for multimodal interaction data. [[Arminio2025-tw]] and [[Gardam2025-er]] both push visual methods beyond text-centric defaults: the former shows that vision-language models can recover connotative, interpretable meaning from images that CNN-based clustering misses, while the latter argues more programmatically that image-first platforms like Instagram demand visual-first discourse analysis, illustrated through climate-denial content. [[Balluff2026-if]] tempers this methodological momentum with a critical corrective aimed at the newest tool in the kit, arguing that LLM adoption for text analysis, synthetic data, and simulation in communication research has outpaced reflection on reproducibility, bias, environmental cost, and corporate dependency—explicitly counseling a trade-off mindset that favors smaller, open, task-appropriate models.

## Conceptual heterogeneity and coordination detection as cautionary methodology

A final set of papers models methodological self-scrutiny at the level of entire subfields. [[Hartmann2025-px]] and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] both conduct systematic reviews—of echo chamber/filter bubble research and algorithm literacy research, respectively—concluding that apparent empirical disagreement in each literature is substantially an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than divergent underlying reality, a diagnosis that resonates with this whole topic's concern for methodological rigor over convenience. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] performs the same service for coordinated online behavior, reconciling fragmented industry and academic definitions into a general framework (actors, synergic actions, intent) and showing how arbitrary choices like time-window length materially change what counts as "coordination." [[Luceri2025-tr]] and [[Pante2025-pq]] apply and stress-test these detection methods in harder cases—adapting network-based coordination detection to TikTok's video-first ecosystem, and re-examining prior claims of inter-state coordination on Twitter/X with modern indicators and control datasets—both concluding that methodological rigor, not just data access, determines whether claims about coordinated manipulation are credible. Read together with the access and bias literature above, this cluster closes the loop: having secured (however precariously) the data, and having audited its distortions, researchers must still hold their own analytic constructs to the same evidentiary standard they demand of platforms.
