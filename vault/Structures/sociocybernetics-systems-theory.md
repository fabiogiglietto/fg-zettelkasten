# Sociocybernetics & Social Systems Theory

## Theoretical Throughline

These three papers, though addressing disparate empirical objects — misinformation cascades, machine agency, and multi-agent AI ecosystems — share a common theoretical commitment: social phenomena cannot be reduced to the properties of individual actors (human or artificial) but must be understood as emergent, observer-dependent, and system-constituted. This is the sociocybernetic core that runs underneath the more applied empirical work filed elsewhere, and it is worth tracing explicitly as a line of argument rather than a list of topics.

## Second-Order Cybernetics and the Problem of Observation

[[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] is the clearest expression of classical sociocybernetic method in this set. Drawing directly on von Foerster and Bateson's second-order cybernetics, alongside Luhmann and Chadwick's hybrid media system, the paper insists that 'fake news' cannot be defined by reference to an originating intent — a first-order, creator-centric fallacy. Instead, information is treated as observer-dependent ('a difference that makes a difference'), and the analytical burden shifts to the chain of propagators whose judgments reconstitute the system at each relay. The micro–meso–macro architecture the authors build is itself a small systems-theoretic apparatus: individual truth-judgments (micro) combine into typologies of propagation (meso) that produce emergent, autonomous-seeming cascades (macro) irreducible to any single actor's intent. The key move — rejecting a stable, essentialist category of 'fake news' in favor of composite, cycling judgments — is a direct application of second-order cybernetics' insistence that the system observes itself into being.

## Agency as Social Status: Systems Theory Meets the Machine

[[Anicker2024-vp]] extends this observer-dependent logic to a harder question: what counts as an agent at all. Rather than asking whether AI systems possess agency as an intrinsic property, the paper reframes agency as a socially granted and monitored *license* — a status conferred and revocable within social practice, not a technical capacity residing in the system itself. This is continuous with the Luhmannian and cybernetic sensibility of [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]: just as truthfulness is not inherent in content but enacted through propagators' judgments, agency is not inherent in a system but enacted through the ongoing attributions of a social matrix. The demarcation problem Anicker poses — where to draw the line between agents and nonagents — is thus a systems-theoretic question dressed as a classification problem: the boundary is drawn by the system's own observational practices, not by the entity being observed.

## From Demarcation to Formal Structural Priors

[[Ng2026-og]] picks up precisely where this demarcation problem leaves off, but pushes toward formalization and toward governance stakes. Where Anicker asks whether AI systems can be agents in a social sense, Ng et al. assume agentic status and ask what structural features of *social systems* must therefore condition how such agents are modeled, evaluated, and governed. Their Multi-Agent Social Systems framework — formalized as the triple (f, g, G) of information exchange, influence dynamics, and interaction network — operationalizes the same anti-individualist premise shared by the other two papers: behavior is not a property of isolated agents but emerges from network-constrained, co-evolving interaction. The four structural priors (strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) are essentially a formal restatement of the cybernetic intuition in [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] that cascades are emergent and irreducible, now demonstrated empirically on a 2.1M-post AI social network rather than argued discursively. Notably, Ng et al. explicitly position alignment research (RLHF, DPO) as addressing a dyadic user–operator problem distinct from the population-level governance problem that social systems theory properly addresses — echoing Anicker's insistence that agency and its regulation are irreducibly social-systemic matters, not engineering properties to be solved locally.

## An Arc from Theory to Applied Grounding

Read together, the three papers trace an arc: from a methodological manifesto for treating information circulation as self-referential and emergent ([[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]), to a conceptual reframing of agency itself as a socially constituted status rather than an inherent trait ([[Anicker2024-vp]]), to a formal, empirically-tested framework that operationalizes both moves for the governance of agentic AI populations ([[Ng2026-og]]). This progression — cybernetic method, to social-theoretic demarcation, to formal multi-agent systems theory — constitutes the enduring theoretical substrate beneath the researcher's more applied, empirical internet- and platform-studies work: a conviction, consistent with RC51 sociocybernetics, that networked phenomena (whether misinformation, machine agency, or AI social ecosystems) must be understood at the level of the system and its self-observation, not at the level of the isolated, intending actor.
