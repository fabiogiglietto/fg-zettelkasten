# Platform Governance, APIs & Data Access

## The arc from open APIs to regulated access

The backbone of this collection traces a historical arc: an era of relatively open platform data ("Wild West" or "data philanthropy"), a brief "Golden Age" of academic APIs, the post-2018 "APIcalypse" and its consolidation into a "post-API age," and now an emergent regulatory settlement under the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA). [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the problem at its inception—Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure—and [[Bruns2019-nr]] supplies the critical diagnosis that platforms instrumentalized the Cambridge Analytica scandal to frustrate accountability research rather than merely protect privacy. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodize this history in more granular detail, cataloguing how Meta, Twitter/X, TikTok, and Reddit each cycled through laissez-faire, academic, and pay-to-play regimes, while Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] eulogize Twitter's API specifically—the latter using bibliometric evidence to show a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024, quantifying what the qualitative accounts describe. [[Yang2026-tq]] confirms this pattern at the level of the entire social science literature: empirical social-media research tripled from 2010 but plateaued and declined from 2022, with researchers only beginning to shift toward TikTok, YouTube, and donation-based methods. [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes the resulting landscape of 72 access tools around a "price, proficiency, or permission" framework, showing how access barriers compound to exclude non-elite and non-Western researchers—a concern echoed by [[Tonneau2025-bv]] on moderator-language disparities and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]]'s survey of the DSA-era API landscape.

Against this backdrop, the DSA's Article 40 is treated across many papers as a genuine, if fragile, inflection point. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], and [[Pierri2026-ib]] converge on cautious optimism: Article 40 reframes data access as a legal entitlement rather than platform philanthropy, but implementation is beset by opaque procedures, inconsistent timelines, and strategic platform resistance—friction that [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] insists should be read as evidence regulation is working, not failing. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] complicate this optimism by documenting how the practical form access takes—particularly Meta Content Library's "clean room" architecture—systematically privileges well-resourced, code-fluent, quantitative researchers while excluding qualitative, cross-platform, and Global South scholarship. [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "seeing like an API" framework and [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats infrastructure both show how the *design* of access tools—not just their existence—shapes what can be known, a throughline that also animates [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s audit of YouTube's "forgetful" search API and [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]]'s finding that TikTok's expanded Research API remains riddled with unexplained limitations. [[Peters2026-mo]] adds a documentary account of how "data quality" itself became a contested object in DSA rulemaking, fought over by academics, NGOs, and platforms with different stakes. [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Lukito2026-il]] illustrate the practical costs of this patchwork for substantive research on elections and cross-platform media strategy.

## Industry capture and the political economy of platform-dependent knowledge

A second cluster interrogates not just whether access exists but who it empowers and what epistemic distortions it produces. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] make the sharpest claims here, showing empirically that roughly half of high-profile social-media research has undisclosed industry ties, that ties are concentrated among a small elite of repeatedly-engaged scholars who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, and that this "industrial saturation" (~80%) skews topics toward user-blaming narratives (misinformation sharing) and away from platform-dynamics critique. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this structurally, arguing platform research is uniquely vulnerable to industry influence compared to pharma or tobacco because platforms exclusively control the data needed to study them. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers an internal critique of the flagship Meta2020 partnership, arguing its rigor paradoxically produced low "temporal" and "poetic" validity, incapable of tracking platforms that change faster than peer review—an argument for *proactive* regulation over reactive science. [[Park2026-tr]] complements this political-economy critique with a ground-level account of the legal chilling effects (CFAA-style prosecution risk, institutional ambivalence) that deter independent platform research altogether, while [[Gillespie2010-as]] supplies the foundational discursive critique—how "platform" rhetoric itself launders corporate curation as neutral infrastructure—that much of this later work extends into empirical registers.

## Studying what platforms actually do: moderation, ranking, and reach

A substantial body of empirical work uses whatever access remains to interrogate specific governance mechanisms. On content moderation, [[Gillespie2022-jx]] theorizes "reduction" (algorithmic demotion without removal) as an underexamined but pervasive moderation technique, a lens [[Rogers2026-zz]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] operationalize empirically: the former tracing Meta's successive redefinitions of "problematic content" (fake news → misinformation → borderline) alongside the finding that well-performing borderline content remains disproportionately right-wing; the latter showing partisan and journalistic-quality "penalties" and "rewards" in Facebook's share-to-view translation that fluctuate with known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] extends this into Meta's political content reduction policy, finding a 72% reach decline for Italian MPs that both preceded Meta's announced timeline and was undisclosed in DSA transparency reports—direct evidence of the "transparency deficit" that [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] diagnoses discursively in Meta's Newsroom framing, and that [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" generalizes across Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] show how algorithmic changes leave detectable statistical traces even in ostensibly "black box" systems, supporting calls for DSA-enabled longitudinal audits. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] turn to the post-fact-checking landscape directly, finding that Meta's framing of fact-checkers as censors is largely unsupported empirically, while user "false news" reports mostly flag politically disagreeable—not actually false—content, raising doubts about Community Notes as a substitute. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] furnishes a structural critique of Community Notes itself, showing its bridging-based design systematically undermoderates polarizing content. [[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] extend moderation audits to YouTube, finding inconsistent application of authority labels and the persistence of deplatformed figures (Andrew Tate) through ambient, decentralized circulation that outpaces removal.

On algorithmic effects more directly, [[Gauthier2026-iq]] and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] offer complementary accounts of X's feed algorithm: the former a large field experiment showing causal, asymmetric conservative shifts in political attitudes driven by persistent changes in following behavior; the latter showing that apparent right-leaning algorithmic bias is substantially explained by agitating content and proximity to Elon Musk rather than ideology per se. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] demonstrates that X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear representation of users' political ideology, raising profiling and GDPR concerns, while [[Votta2025-xz]] examines how ad delivery algorithms shape political campaign reach and cost. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] remains a reference point for ideological segregation on Facebook, finding it far higher than web-based studies suggested and concentrated asymmetrically on the right—a finding [[Rogers2026-zz]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] both revisit and partially complicate in later election cycles.

## Platform power, discourse, and the stakes of deregulation

A more conceptual stream theorizes platform power itself. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] and [[Chadwick2013-ns]] supply foundational framings—the "platform society" as a contested terrain of public versus private value, and the "hybrid media system" as the interdependent entanglement of old and new media—against which later essays measure change. [[Helmond2026-ll]] and [[Boyd2026-op]], both anniversary reflections, argue platforms have entered a qualitatively new phase: an AI-centered "platformization" eclipsing the social graph, and a shift from reciprocal sociality to "parasocial media." [[Bennett2018-ys]] and [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicize the disinformation-moderation nexus, the latter arguing misinformation-at-scale is a structural feature of engagement-driven business models rather than an aberration, tracing platforms' retreat from trust-and-safety investment after 2021—a retreat [[Moran2025-qn]] documents ethnographically among T&S practitioners, and [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates as the "death of Twitter" as a space for public debate. [[Crosset2026-mq]] offers a comparative legal-governmentality analysis showing how divergent EU/US content legislation nonetheless converges on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" via transparency and auditing mandates. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues for reconceiving platform collective behavior as "democratic infrastructure" rather than a site for narrow causal-effects research, a call that resonates with [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]'s earlier diagnosis of methodological inadequacy in political communication scholarship confronting micro-targeting and networked publics.

## Emerging harms and governance frontiers

A final set of papers extends the governance conversation to adjacent and emerging harms that stress-test existing frameworks. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] operationalizes DSA Article 25's dark-pattern provisions into a design-factor framework applicable to live enforcement cases like X's blue checkmarks. [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Graham2025-gp]] document how extremist and propaganda actors exploit platform affordances and moderation gaps (TikTok's jihadist "everyday extremism," pro-Kremlin hashtag campaigns), while [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a unifying concept for manipulation that exceeds existing coordinated-inauthentic-behavior taxonomies. [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extend platform governance debates into the "scam economy," arguing fraud is now central rather than peripheral to platform business models and calling for gatekeeper liability. [[Emilio2026-ik]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] look forward to generative AI's reconfiguration of epistemic authority—synthetic realities eroding verification, and Google's AI Overviews redefining search—while [[Goldberg2026-eb]] and [[Richter2026-bt]] consider AI's potential to reshape (for better or worse) digital public squares and the stakeholders driving AI discourse itself. [[Ventura2026-yc]] closes the loop on platform-state relations by showing how Brazil's X ban produced durable partisan "sorting ratchets," a reminder that governance interventions—whether by platforms or states—reshape, rather than neutrally referee, the information environments researchers are racing to understand. Throughout, [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]'s commentary serve as synthesizing bookends, insisting that no single intervention—technical, regulatory, or educational—suffices, and that platform governance, journalism, and research infrastructure must be reformed in concert.
