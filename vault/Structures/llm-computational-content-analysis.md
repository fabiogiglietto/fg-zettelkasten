# LLMs for Computational Content Analysis

## Validating LLMs as measurement instruments

The methodological core of this literature is a validation problem: once an LLM is inserted into a content-analysis pipeline, how do we know its outputs are trustworthy substitutes for what a human coder, a purpose-built classifier, or an established scaling method would have produced? [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] frames this most directly, describing a three-stage LLMs-in-the-loop pipeline (fine-tuned political classifier, embedding-based clustering, LLM-generated cluster labels) applied to Italian Facebook data and arguing that each stage needs its own tailored validation protocol rather than a single blanket check—crowdsourced raters are inadequate once LLMs routinely outperform them, so expert coders become the appropriate benchmark. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] supplies the embedding half of that pipeline with a rigorous comparison, showing that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large yields more semantically coherent clusters of Italian political news than the language-specific UmBERTo model across both election years and clustering algorithms—extending findings about LLM-embedding superiority from English into a non-English, political-communication setting. [[Fan2025-ut]] approaches the same embedding layer from a different angle, showing that pretrained sentence embeddings encode spurious source/language confounds that bias similarity estimates, and that linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove this contamination cheaply and generally, dramatically improving multilingual and multi-source retrieval without degrading unrelated benchmarks. Together these two papers make the case that embedding choice and post-hoc correction are not implementation details but first-order methodological decisions with measurable consequences for downstream clustering and retrieval-based analyses—an argument [[Bruns2025-fz]] extends structurally by proposing "practice mapping," vector-embedding representations of multimodal network interactions, as an alternative to conventional (and visually intractable) network graphs.

Several papers push validation toward the annotation layer specifically. [[Brown2025-jk]] tests whether LLM annotators reproduce demographic biases across four contentious labeling tasks and finds that apparent bias is dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and that item difficulty (human label entropy) swamps demographic effects as a predictor of LLM-human agreement—an important corrective to bias narratives that focus on model choice alone. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] validates a much simpler measurement idea: asking an instruction-tuned LLM to place a text on an ideological dimension and averaging many such judgments, showing this "ask-and-average" approach correlates strongly with established scaling benchmarks across languages and text types (tweets, manifestos, speeches), offering a lightweight alternative to Wordscores/Wordfish-style pipelines. [[Meher2025-qb]] validates a fine-tuning approach instead, showing that QLoRA-adapted Llama 3.1 dramatically outperforms zero-shot prompting for multi-label conflict-event classification, particularly for rare categories, while remaining tractable on consumer hardware—positioning efficient fine-tuning as a successor to both rule-based coding and BERT-style encoders like ConfliBERT. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] extends validation into the visual domain, benchmarking GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision pipelines (RetinaFace, FaceNet512, Google Cloud Vision) for face recognition and person-counting in campaign imagery, finding the multimodal LLM substantially more accurate and less brittle to gender-linked bias than the specialized alternatives.

## What LLMs get wrong, and where curated context matters

A second cluster complicates any simple story of "LLMs match or beat baselines" by identifying systematic failure modes. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows that even reasoning- and web-search-enabled frontier models perform poorly at political fact-checking on PolitiFact claims unless supplied with curated retrieval context, and that search-enabled models exhibit a citation skew toward left-leaning (if credible) sources—suggesting that "just add search" does not solve verification and that curation remains the binding constraint. [[Paci2025-ag]] finds a comparable ceiling effect in pragmatics: LLMs struggle to correctly interpret implicatures and presuppositions in real Italian political speech, falling well short of expert human interpretation even with chain-of-thought prompting, indicating that manipulative implicit content is a genuine blind spot rather than a prompting artifact. [[Waight2026-ts]] identifies a more structural failure: state-coordinated media is memorized in training corpora and measurably shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence when prompted in the language of low-media-freedom countries, meaning LLMs can "launder" state influence into apparently neutral answers—a finding with direct implications for any content-analysis pipeline that treats LLM judgments as politically neutral ground truth. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]], although about a recommender system rather than an LLM per se, reinforces the same worry from the representation-learning side: embeddings can encode politically sensitive dimensions (Left-Right ideology) even when no such attribute was an explicit design target, with regulatory implications that generalize to LLM embedding spaces used in research pipelines.

Taken together with [[Alizadeh2026-es]]—which shows that coding agents can reproduce most social-science findings when given executable materials, but are also prone to sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting and to bias when given paper PDFs—this cluster cautions that LLM competence is highly conditional on context, task framing, and curated inputs, not a generic capability that transfers cleanly across content-analysis tasks.

## Inference of latent political attributes

A distinct strand asks not whether LLMs can code manifest content accurately, but whether they can infer *latent* political attributes from discourse. [[Lee2026-je]] shows GPT-4o and Llama-3.1 can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical text, exploiting culturally politicized cues (Tesla, Taylor Swift, "boomer") and outperforming supervised baselines—raising both an analytical opportunity (studying the politicization of culture) and a privacy risk (scalable micro-targeting from innocuous content). [[Lai2024-to]] tackles a related latent-variable problem at the level of content rather than individuals, estimating YouTube video ideology via correspondence analysis of Reddit sharing patterns, extending ideal-point-style estimation from legislators and users to video artifacts. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes a structurally different latent construct—"narrative polarisation"—using an LLM to apply Greimas' Actantial Model to YouTube transcripts and comments about the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, finding that surface polarization in comments is lower than in videos even as deeper narrative motifs preserve partisan asymmetries; its validation strategy (LLM-human agreement comparable to human-human agreement) is itself a methodological contribution to this topic. [[Waight2025-al]] generalizes the narrative-measurement problem further, arguing that "narrative similarity"—shared claims about shared events—is conflated with lexical, topical, and semantic similarity in existing methods, and building an LLM-based claim-distillation pipeline validated against text reuse, topic modeling, and Relatio on a gold-standard dataset, then applying it to trace Russian "biolabs" narratives into low-quality U.S. outlets in a way exact-reuse methods entirely miss. These papers collectively demonstrate a maturing methodological literature around estimand-estimator alignment (following Grimmer & King, Lundberg et al.) specifically for LLM-based narrative and ideology measurement.

## Multimodality and framing beyond text

Several papers extend content-analysis validation into visual and multimodal territory. Beyond [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]]'s face/person benchmarking, [[Arminio2025-tw]] shows that routing images through a vision-language model to generate connotative textual descriptions—then embedding and clustering those descriptions—captures culturally embedded meaning (e.g., grouping wind turbines and solar panels as "renewable energy") that CNN-based pipelines miss, at a small cost to denotative coherence, while also yielding interpretable TF-IDF cluster summaries that CNN pipelines cannot provide. [[Arora2025-tx]] argues framing analysis itself needs to become multimodal, jointly extracting discourse-analytic frames from text, images, and their interaction in gun-violence news coverage rather than relying on fixed, text-only frame sets. [[Sarmiento2025-as]] complements this with an unsupervised (though not LLM-centric) pipeline for surfacing emergent frames in polarized social media discourse without predefined categories. Read together with [[Fan2026-af]]'s proposal to treat digital trace data as timestamped "user-sequences" via sequence analysis, HMMs, network analysis, process mining, and language-based embedding models, this cluster reflects a broader push to move computational content analysis beyond static, single-modality, cross-sectional measurement—with LLMs and embeddings offered as one route (among several computational alternatives) toward that richer representation.

## Downstream effects: persuasion, manipulation, and platform dynamics

A related set of papers examines what happens once LLMs are not just analytical tools but active participants in political communication. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]]'s large-scale experiments identify information density as the central mechanism of conversational AI persuasion and document a persuasion-accuracy trade-off—the same levers (post-training, information-dense prompting) that increase persuasiveness also degrade factual accuracy—directly relevant to researchers using LLMs to generate stimuli or simulate political communication. [[Allen2025-ot]] discusses a platform-independent experimental method (an LLM-based browser-extension reranker) as a methodological response to closing API access, situating it against the FIES collaboration and arguing no single design can characterize social media's political effects. [[Triedman2025-uy]] documents a novel object of study for this community: Grokipedia, an AI-generated encyclopedia that is heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet cites unreliable and blacklisted sources at elevated rates on controversial and political topics, illustrating both the promise and the risk of LLM-generated reference content entering the information ecosystem researchers study. [[Ramos2026-qo]], from fact-checking practice, argues that claim-level correction is insufficient against evolving disinformation narratives and proposes argument-checking as a complementary methodology—a reminder that LLM-based content analysis sits within a broader toolkit for countering, not just describing, discourse manipulation. [[Minici2024-tf]]'s graph-foundation-model approach to detecting coordinated information operations occupies similar territory, combining language and graph signals to generalize IO detection across campaigns and platforms.

## Reflexivity, scope conditions, and the wider research infrastructure

Finally, several papers step back to interrogate the field's adoption practices rather than propose new techniques. [[Balluff2026-if]] offers the sharpest critique, arguing that LLM adoption in communication research has outpaced reflection on reproducibility, corporate dependency, environmental cost, and bias, and calling for a trade-off mindset that prefers smaller, open, task-appropriate models where they suffice—a useful counterweight to the embedding- and fine-tuning-focused optimism elsewhere in this set. [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Larsson2026-ro]] offer grounded, applied instances of the human-in-the-loop ideal Balluff calls for: the former integrates topic modeling, LLM-assisted labeling, and iterative human codebook refinement for qualitative interview analysis; the latter validates GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment classification against human coders in a non-English longitudinal Facebook study, explicitly framing itself as a methodological demonstration of LLM-assisted content analysis outside English. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the broader LLM-for-annotation literature these applied papers instantiate, while [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] addresses an adjacent but foundational infrastructure question—how opt-in online samples vary in representativeness and validity—that bears on any LLM-annotated pipeline built atop online survey or platform data. The systematic reviews [[Hartmann2025-px]] and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] round out this reflexive layer, showing how conceptual and operational fragmentation (in echo-chamber research and algorithm-literacy research respectively) can masquerade as substantive disagreement—a cautionary parallel for LLM-based content analysis, where similarly loose definitions of "narrative," "frame," or "bias" risk producing incommensurable findings across studies. [[Jayaram2026-wd]], on automated scientific review, and [[Alizadeh2026-es]], on reproduction agents, gesture toward where this methodological infrastructure is heading next: not just LLMs analyzing political content, but LLM-based agents auditing and reproducing the computational social science literature itself.
