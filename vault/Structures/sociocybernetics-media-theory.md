# Sociocybernetics & Media Theory

## From Internet Architecture to Social Systems Theory

The earliest paper in this collection, [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]], is not explicitly cybernetic in vocabulary, but it lays down the foundational commitment that runs through everything that follows: the claim that technical infrastructures cannot be designed or understood independently of the social relations — trust, reciprocity, influence — that flow through them. Its call to integrate social capital metrics into Internet architecture, and its insistence that centrality means something different in a network-engineering sense than in a social-capital sense, already anticipates a recurring theme: that "the social" is not a layer bolted onto technology but a structuring force that co-determines it. The figure of the "network prosumer" — a user who is simultaneously node, resource, and rule-follower — is an early sketch of what later papers will call, in more explicitly systems-theoretic terms, an agent embedded in a self-organizing, recursively coupled system.

[[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] makes the cybernetic inheritance explicit. Drawing directly on von Foerster, Bateson, and Luhmann, it reframes "fake news" not as a property of content or the intent of its creator but as an emergent effect of observer-dependent judgments propagating through a hybrid media system. Its micro–meso–macro model — individual truth-judgments, a four-cell matrix of injector/propagator combinations, and emergent global cascades — is essentially a second-order-cybernetic account of misinformation as a self-organizing communicative system, one in which "information" is irreducibly relational ("a difference that makes a difference") rather than an objective property circulating unchanged. This paper functions as something like the theoretical hinge of the whole set: it names the epistemology (second-order observation, systems-theoretic emergence) that the later, more applied papers presuppose without always restating.

## Platforms as Complex Adaptive Systems

The middle of the arc consists of papers that take social media platforms themselves as the object of complex-systems analysis. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes a morphological shift in the "social form" of online publics — from network-centric, people-visible structures to algorithmically clustered, opaque, item-centric ones — using a Simmelian/Weberian vocabulary of ideal-types that sits comfortably alongside the systems-theoretic lineage established earlier. Where Giglietto treats propagation as emergent from individual judgments, Gerbaudo treats *visibility itself* as an emergent, statistically constructed property of the platform's recommender architecture, with watch time replacing the explicit social tie as the operative signal.

[[Tornberg2026-lc]] extends this diagnosis into a full paradigm crisis: if "social media" as a concept depended on user-generated content circulating through a legible social graph, that configuration is dissolving under three simultaneous pressures — algorithmic broadcasting, generative-AI content, and retreat into semi-private enclaves. The paper's insistence that these three "post-social" formations require *analytically distinct* methods rather than a single unified theory is itself a complexity-theoretic move: it refuses premature closure around one causal mechanism, echoing the multilevel, anti-reductionist stance of [[Frischlich2025-vn]], which explicitly frames misinformation as a complex adaptive system irreducible to any single analogy (virus, warfare, pollution). Frischlich et al.'s four-level (micro/meso-social/meso-platform/macro) mapping reads as a direct methodological cousin of Giglietto's micro–meso–macro model, applied to policy rather than definition.

[[Ng2026-og]] pushes the same complex-adaptive-systems logic into a new empirical domain — multi-agent AI systems — arguing that social theory, not individualist AI alignment frameworks, supplies the correct structural priors (strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) for modeling agentic populations. Its formal (f, g, G) triple and its empirical validation on a 2.1-million-post AI social network make it the most mathematically explicit heir to the sociocybernetic commitments voiced informally back in Sofia2012: social structure as a *prior*, not an afterthought, for any technical system involving interacting agents.

## Governance, Infrastructure, and Democratic Function

A second cluster asks what these complex, self-organizing platform-systems mean for democratic governance. [[Crosset2026-mq]] analyzes EU and US content-moderation legislation through the lens of security governance, showing how states increasingly regulate the *circulation* of information rather than its content — a move entirely consonant with a systems view in which what matters is flow, feedback, and administrative coupling ("security bureaucratisation") rather than direct intervention on discrete messages. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] makes a parallel argument at a more programmatic level, urging platform-and-democracy scholarship to move past linear causal-effects research toward treating "platform collective behavior" itself as democratic infrastructure — an explicit call to adopt the systems/infrastructure framing that the rest of this note's papers have been building empirically. [[Esau2025-tf]] contributes the normative complement: if destructive polarization is an emergent property of aggregate discourse dynamics (rather than simply a sum of bad individual actors), then the antidote — deliberative reciprocity, inclusive listening — must likewise operate at the level of relational quality within the system, not merely individual message correction.

## Extending the Frame to Machine Agents

The most recent contributions push the sociocybernetic apparatus toward its next frontier: artificial agents. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reformulates the question of AI agency as a "demarcation problem" resolved not by intrinsic technical capacity but by socially granted and monitored status — a thoroughly constructivist, systems-theoretic move that treats agency itself as an emergent social attribution rather than a substance. Read together with [[Ng2026-og]], these two papers mark the point where the topic's long-standing preoccupation with self-organizing social systems is explicitly extended to non-human participants, closing a loop that began with Sofia's "network prosumer": the question of what kind of entity a node in a network *is* has moved from humans-with-technology to machines-as-social-agents.

## Synthesis

Read as a sequence, this body of work traces a single, patient argument: platforms, publics, misinformation, moderation regimes, and now AI agents are best understood not as discrete objects to be causally dissected but as recursively coupled, self-organizing systems whose properties — visibility, truth, polarization, agency, security — are emergent and observer-dependent. The early architectural pragmatism of [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] and the explicit second-order cybernetics of [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] set the terms; the platform-studies papers ([[Gerbaudo2026-fo]], [[Tornberg2026-lc]], [[Frischlich2025-vn]]) apply them to the algorithmic present; the governance papers ([[Crosset2026-mq]], [[Bechmann2026-dr]], [[Esau2025-tf]]) draw out the normative and regulatory stakes; and the newest work ([[Ng2026-og]], [[Anicker2024-vp]]) carries the same structural commitments into the domain of multi-agent and artificial systems — evidence that the sociocybernetic lens, far from being a historical curiosity, is the connective tissue linking this researcher's earliest infrastructural concerns to their current handbook-level theorizing about AI-mediated social order.
