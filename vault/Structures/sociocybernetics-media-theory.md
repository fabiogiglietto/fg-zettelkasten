# Sociocybernetics & Media Systems Theory

## From Observer-Dependent Systems to Algorithmic Publics

The thread begins where sociocybernetics always begins: with the refusal to treat information, truth, or social order as objective properties sitting "out there" waiting to be transmitted. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] makes this foundational move explicit, reaching back to second-order cybernetics (von Foerster, Bateson) and Luhmann to argue that "fake news" cannot be defined by the intent of a creator, because truthfulness is always an observer's judgment, reconstituted at every point in a propagation chain. Its micro–meso–macro model — individual judgment, a four-cell matrix of injector/propagator combinations, emergent global cascades — is a small-scale demonstration of a much older sociocybernetic habit: refusing single-level causal stories and instead tracking how local decisions recursively produce system-level order. This same multi-level instinct resurfaces, decades of platform evolution later, in [[Frischlich2025-vn]], which explicitly rejects the dominant "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" analogies for misinformation on the grounds that each smuggles in a single-level ontology (virus biology, military strategy, environmental toxicity) and thereby forecloses the multilevel, recursively coupled dynamics — individual cognition, group belonging, platform architecture, macro-institutional strategy — that a properly complex-systems account requires. Read together, these two papers bookend the empirical turn of the topic: Giglietto supplies the epistemological grammar (observer-dependence, emergent cascades), Frischlich supplies the programmatic call to formalize that grammar as an explicit multilevel model rather than an implicit metaphor.

## Architecture and Morphology: How Platforms Reshape the Social Form

Two papers examine how the *structure* of platforms — rather than the content flowing through them — reconfigures sociality itself, a question close to Luhmannian concerns with social form and differentiation. [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] is the topic's architectural precursor: written at the threshold of the prosumer era, it argues that Internet infrastructure design should be driven by social-capital variables (trust, reach, influence, engagement) rather than purely technical ones, and it flags a structural mismatch between networking notions of centrality (degree, betweenness) and their social meaning (bridging, bottlenecking) — an early instance of the recurring sociocybernetic problem of translating between technical and social levels of description. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] picks up this same problem a decade later at a far more advanced stage of platformization, theorizing TikTok's shift from "networked publics" to "clustered publics" as a change in the *social form* of online publics in an explicitly Simmelian sense. Where Sofia worried about aligning technical and social centrality, Gerbaudo documents a system in which interpersonal ties (following, friending) have been functionally superseded by statistically inferred behavioral similarity — depersonalization, opacity, and fragmentation as the structural consequences of an architecture optimized for watch-time signals rather than explicit social choice. Together the two papers trace an arc from "how should social capital shape infrastructure" to "how has infrastructure dissolved social capital into algorithmic clustering," registering sociocybernetics' long-standing interest in the coupling (and decoupling) of technical and social systems.

## Beyond Causality: Platforms as Democratic Infrastructure

[[Bechmann2026-dr]] extends the anti-reductionist stance to the study of platforms and democracy directly, arguing that the causal-effects paradigm dominating platform-and-democracy research — asking whether exposure to X causes outcome Y — is structurally inadequate to grasp platforms as *democratic infrastructure*: recursive, self-organizing systems of collective behavior rather than simple transmission channels. This is a policy-facing translation of the same sociocybernetic commitment animating Giglietto and Frischlich: causal chains are the wrong unit of analysis for phenomena that are properly emergent and multilevel. Read against Gerbaudo's account of clustered publics, Bechmann's intervention suggests that the DSA-era regulatory imagination needs concepts of collective, structurally-mediated behavior that current media-effects research — and perhaps current regulation — does not yet possess.

## Extending the Frame: Social Theory as Structural Prior for Machine Agents

The most recent additions push sociocybernetic reasoning toward a genuinely new object: artificial agents populating social systems. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes machine agency not as a technical capability but as a socially granted and monitored status — a "demarcation problem" resolved by social attribution rather than by inspecting the system itself, a move that is thoroughly cybernetic in its insistence that agency is constituted relationally, at the level of observation and social practice, not intrinsically. [[Ng2026-og]] operationalizes this insight at scale, arguing that agentic AI systems must be modeled with social theory as a *structural prior* rather than as isolated task-completers, formalizing multi-agent AI populations as an (f, g, G) triple and empirically demonstrating — with 2.1M posts from an AI social network — exactly the kind of emergent, network-constrained, co-evolving, distributionally unstable dynamics that sociocybernetics has long attributed to human social systems. Taken together, Anicker and Ng suggest that the conceptual apparatus built across this whole topic — observer-dependent judgment, emergent multilevel cascades, morphological transformation of publics, infrastructure as collective behavior — is now being asked to do double duty: not just describing how humans behave inside algorithmically mediated systems, but describing how synthetic agents inherit, replicate, and complicate those very systemic dynamics. This is the natural extension point of the doctoral line of inquiry: sociocybernetics not as a historical framework retrofitted to social media, but as the theoretical resource increasingly necessary for making sense of hybrid human–machine social systems as they emerge.
