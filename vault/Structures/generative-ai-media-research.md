# Generative AI in Media & Disinformation

## Synthetic Persuasion as an Emerging Object of Study

This cluster gathers a still-forming strand of work that treats generative AI not as a topic to be reported on but as an infrastructural force reshaping how persuasion, disinformation, and trust operate online. Its anchor is [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]], which documents how coordinated Facebook groups promoting online gambling shifted from manual image-sourcing to industrial-scale generative-AI production almost overnight after ChatGPT's late-2022 launch. The paper's hybrid LLM-embeddings-clustering pipeline is itself a methodological statement: it shows how the same technology reshaping the object of study (visual persuasion) can be repurposed as an instrument for studying it at scale — a reflexivity that runs through several other papers here and motivates the pipeline-integration side of this topic area. Its typology of persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, gamification, cultural localization) and its diagnosis of Meta's asymmetric governance between paid and organic content extends earlier scholarship on platform capitalism and algorithmic amplification into a domain — coordinated inauthentic commercial promotion — that sits adjacent to, but analytically distinct from, political disinformation.

## Political Persuasion at Scale: What Actually Moves Opinion

A second strand asks, with unusual empirical rigor, what actually makes conversational AI persuasive in political contexts. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Lin2025-xp]] converge on a striking, almost counter-intuitive finding: persuasion is driven less by personalization or model scale — the capacities most feared in public discourse — than by information density, post-training/reward modeling, and prompting strategy. Both papers document a persuasion–accuracy trade-off (Hackenburg) and a partisan asymmetry in claim accuracy (Lin), suggesting that the "manipulation" risk of LLMs is real but operates through mundane mechanisms (fact-dense argumentation, conversational give-and-take) rather than through the sophisticated psychographic targeting long associated with Cambridge-Analytica-style fears. [[Orlando2025-ul]] complicates this picture from a systems angle: using generative agent-based modeling, it shows that LLM agents can spontaneously reproduce coordination signatures of real information operations — network densification, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification — from mere mutual awareness rather than explicit deliberate scripting. Read together, these three papers reposition the "AI persuasion threat" away from individual-level microtargeting and toward emergent, structural, and content-density mechanisms — a reframing directly relevant to how [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]'s gambling networks scale so effectively without needing bespoke targeting.

## Deepfakes, Synthetic Reality, and the Erosion of Verification

A parallel strand interrogates what generative fabrication does to publics' capacity to know. [[Emilio2026-ik]] offers the most totalizing frame, proposing "synthetic reality" as a four-layer stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and naming the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic content proliferates, societies may rationally discount *all* digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself and enabling strategic deniability. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] approaches the same problem epistemologically, arguing that existing fact-checking categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot capture GenAI outputs and proposing "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category requiring relational, indicator-based evaluation rather than binary verification. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] tempers the alarm empirically: in a controlled comparison, AI-generated still images did not outperform text or decontextualized video in credibility or engagement except for highly salient, high-footage-availability issues (the Ukraine war), suggesting the "unprecedented persuasive power" of GenAI visuals is more contingent than assumed — a useful corrective to read alongside Emilio's and Dierickx's more totalizing claims. [[Wack2026-bt]] pushes further into the social, showing in a large Kenyan survey experiment that deepfake impact is not fixed at production but negotiated collectively through surrounding comments and partisan priors — "collective sensemaking" rather than individual credulity. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] grounds these epistemic questions in concrete harm and policy: mapping the deepfake financial-fraud "scam ecosystem," it argues for shifting liability from individual victims to platform, telecom, and financial gatekeepers, directly paralleling Giglietto's argument that platform governance structure (not just content) is the true lever of harm.

## Trust, Disclosure, and Institutional Framing

A third cluster examines how audiences and institutions calibrate trust in AI-mediated information. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework extending mass-media and social-media trust research to GenAI. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] test this empirically in adjacent but distinct configurations: Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-involved news, while Dutch audiences penalize *all* task-specific AI disclosures in journalism, with fact-checking disclosure incurring the steepest trust cost — together suggesting that trust in AI is highly configuration- and task-dependent rather than a single monolithic attitude, complicating simple "transparency will fix it" policy assumptions. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shifts the lens to how news media themselves construct the public epistemology of LLMs, finding that mentalistic-agentic framings are present but not dominant, and vary by outlet/editorial practice rather than region or culture — a finding that qualifies alarmist assumptions (echoed in Emilio2026 and Topinka2026) that journalism is simply amplifying AI hype uncritically.

## Genre, Affect, and the Aesthetics of Generative Culture

A distinct but connected strand theorizes the aesthetic and affective register of AI-generated content itself, moving beyond deception-based models of harm. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Tang2026-gu]] examine "AI slop" as cultural production: Galip, drawing on Ngai's theory of the gimmick, reads slop/brainrot as simultaneously working "too little" (deskilled, labour-saving) and "too hard" (straining for attention), exposing contradictions of "too late capitalism" while probing latent critical potential; Tang documents the industrialization of this labour in China, where creators engineer virality as a manageable, benchmarked process via matrix account systems — a ground-level complement to Giglietto's finding of exponential, formulaic post-ChatGPT visual production in gambling promotion. [[Topinka2026-hb]] theorizes the political variant, "slopaganda," arguing that its power lies not in deceiving viewers (unlike deepfakes) but in affectively "attuning" to feed environments via RLHF-shaped models that sense what resonates — intensifying rather than disrupting existing ideological formations. Read against [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]'s account of hyper-real, surreal gambling imagery deployed alongside established persuasion drivers, these papers suggest a shared logic: generative AI's political-economic power lies less in novel deception than in the industrial-scale, affectively tuned recombination of familiar persuasive and ideological forms.

## Platform Reconfiguration and Comparative Political Contexts

Two further papers zoom out to platform- and regime-level transformation. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues that "social media" as an analytic category is dissolving under three pressures — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private retreat, and AI-mediated communication — with GenAI severing the platform–user link entirely ("platforms without users"), a structural diagnosis that helps explain why coordinated networks like those in Giglietto's gambling study can scale organically without needing traditional social engagement. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] offers a platform-specific counterpoint, finding that pro-immigration content dominates Canadian TikTok despite fears of algorithmic hostility, while flagging generative AI's emerging use for racist "speculative worldbuilding" — a smaller-scale echo of the ideologically inflected localization (e.g., the Urdu-language gambling cluster) documented in Giglietto2026. [[Beacken2026-zb]] resists technological determinism directly, showing across six democratically weakened states that GenAI propaganda adoption is shaped by local political-economic structure rather than following a uniform global trajectory — a needed caution against over-generalizing findings like Hackenburg's or Lin's Western-democracy experiments. Finally, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] closes the loop by imagining a constructive rather than corrosive role for LLMs in public discourse — bridging systems, collective dialogue tools, AI-assisted moderation — offering a normative counterweight to the largely diagnostic and critical papers elsewhere in this collection, and marking the outer edge of where this strand's inquiry, and the accompanying *IA Generativa e Media* course, might expand next: from documenting synthetic seduction and erosion toward evaluating design interventions for epistemic resilience.
