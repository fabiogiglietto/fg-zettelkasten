# Political Communication & Election Campaigns

# Political Communication & Election Campaigns

## The arc: from platform effects to platform infrastructure

Read as a whole, this corpus traces a shift in how the field studies social media's role in elections — moving from asking "does social media cause polarization/misinformation effects?" toward asking "what can we even see, and who gets to see it?" The earlier register is exemplified by the Meta collaboration studies — [[Appel2026-qr]] / [[noauthor_undated-bm]] (the deceptive-networks study of 2020), [[Allcott2025-jb]] (ad-removal null effects), and [[Bakshy2015-rn]] (the original Facebook filter-bubble paper) — each producing carefully bounded causal or descriptive estimates. [[Munger2025-cz]] and [[Allen2025-ot]] step back to interrogate this very research program: Munger's "temporal validity" critique argues that platform research decays too fast to cumulate, while Allen situates Piccardi et al.'s browser-extension approach as a workaround to platforms' closing data ecosystems. This meta-level concern becomes explicit infrastructure-building in [[Lukito2026-nb]] (cataloguing 72 access tools along price/proficiency/permission axes), [[Philipp2026-tl]] (surveying DSA-mandated APIs), [[Schulte2026-df]] (multi-platform German election data as an "infrastructure problem"), and [[Bechmann2026-dr]], which argues explicitly for reconceiving platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure rather than a site for isolated causal-effect studies. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] is a bridge case: using the new Meta Content Library (a DSA-enabled tool) to show that an undisclosed reach-suppression policy silently reshaped Italian parliamentarians' visibility for years — vindicating both the promise and the continuing opacity of regulated access.

## Algorithms, feeds, and the causal-effects debate

A cluster of papers directly tests whether feed algorithms and platform affordances causally shape political attitudes, largely in dialogue with (or reaction against) the 2020 Meta null-effects consensus. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X's "For You" feed finds real, asymmetric, and persistent conservative shifts — explicitly reconciling its positive findings with Meta's nulls by locating the mechanism in algorithmically induced *following* behavior rather than in-feed exposure per se. [[Brady2026-ln]] extends this experimental tradition to Bluesky, showing engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social norms, while a "diversified extremity" algorithm can partially repair this — reframing the mechanism as norm misperception rather than direct persuasion. [[Ventura2025-sw]] pushes the causal-experimental logic into messaging apps via a WhatsApp deactivation design in Brazil, and [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents a very different kind of platform-level shock: Brazil's X ban, showing how state-imposed bans can produce durable partisan "sorting ratchets." Against this, [[Green2025-ap]] and [[Oswald2025-km]] complicate the very object of measurement — arguing that source-level partisanship scores mask story-level heterogeneity (curation bubbles) and that visible discourse is generated by a tiny, unrepresentative fraction of users (the production-consumption gap) — while [[Schulz2026-ts]] shows self-selection into posting manufactures "false polarization" that overstates true attitudinal division. Together these suggest the field is converging on a more skeptical, mechanism-focused successor to the blunt "social media causes polarization" hypothesis, echoed in [[Falkenberg2026-ka]]'s call for a more integrated, globally equitable polarization research agenda.

## Elite strategy: media capture, factionalism, and narrative contests

A second thread examines how political elites strategically exploit hybrid media environments rather than being merely acted upon by algorithms. [[Rodarte2026-dk]]'s study of Manaus shows Brazilian parliamentarians contesting journalistic gatekeeping through three distinct epistemic postures, while [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" shows how CRT-adopting Republicans built profitable ties to hyper-partisan media, and [[Bennett2025-xs]] scales this into a general theory of "digital surrogate organizations" pulling conservative parties toward illiberalism — with [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country analysis giving this a cross-national empirical anchor by showing radical-right populism (not populism or right-ideology alone) predicts elite misinformation sharing. Media capture appears in classic form in [[Balluff2026-bv]]'s Austrian "Inseratenaffäre" analysis (advertising-for-coverage exchange) and in the visibility asymmetries documented by [[Iris2026-pg]] (Radical Right overrepresentation in 2024 EP election coverage) and [[Balluff2026-ev]] (alternative-legacy media entity overlap around Nord Stream 2). The "fake news" label itself becomes an object of study rather than a diagnostic category in [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]], both reframing accusations of fakeness as rhetorical weapons for delegitimization rather than descriptions of falsity — a move paralleled by [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]'s foundational argument (via second-order cybernetics) that "fake news" scholarship over-focuses on creator intent at the expense of propagation dynamics, and operationalized empirically in [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]]'s coordinated-link-sharing methodology and its updated workflow in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]].

## Italy and Brazil as recurring national laboratories

Italy and Brazil anchor much of the corpus's longitudinal ambition. The Italian thread — [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] (2013, hybrid TV/Twitter), [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] (2018, insularity and populist amplification), [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] and [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] (2018/2019/2022, coordinated behavior), [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] (methodological pieces on embeddings and LLM pipelines applied to 2018/2022 data), and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] (Meta's policy effects on 2018-2025 visibility) — constitutes essentially a single evolving research program tracking the same election cycles with increasingly sophisticated tools, moving from manual/network analysis to LLM-assisted classification. The Brazilian thread is equally cumulative: [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] (2018 ad auditing), [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] (2022 election misinformation, intolerance, and trust panels), [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Ventura2026-yc]] (WhatsApp and X ban experiments), and [[Marino2026-he]] (three-year longitudinal analysis of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook affect) collectively chart how Bolsonaro-era disinformation strategies eroded institutional trust and how post-Bolsonaro digital ecosystems reorganized. Germany appears more episodically — [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] (visual campaign strategy, 2021) and [[Schulte2026-df]] (multi-platform infrastructure, 2025) — suggesting comparatively less accumulated longitudinal depth than the Italian or Brazilian cases, while [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s three-country (Germany, Netherlands, Poland) panel around the 2024 EP elections offers a genuinely comparative counterpoint.

## Misinformation, moderation, and the limits of crowdsourced governance

A tight sub-cluster interrogates content moderation design itself. [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] both stress-test X's Community Notes: the former shows Republicans are still flagged disproportionately even under a bridging algorithm (undermining the bias-correction rationale for abandoning fact-checkers), the latter argues structurally that bridging-based systems *by design* undermoderate the most polarizing content, generalizing the concern across 13 countries with electoral stakes. [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers a rare empirical look at deplatforming's downstream "network ripple effects," showing suspensions can flip authority structures entirely rather than merely subtracting banned nodes. [[Kim2026-wg]] and [[Tai2026-qk]] extend the moderation conversation to voter suppression advertising and elite misinformation sharing respectively, both emphasizing that institutional/targeting context — not just content — determines harm. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] closes a loop opened by this cluster: even *coverage of* interference campaigns, independent of their actual persuasive success, can itself asymmetrically deepen perceived polarization.

## Sensemaking, narrative, and the texture of online discourse

Several papers move from platform-level metrics to the discursive fine-grain of how political meaning is made. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop complementary frameworks — evidence-frame analysis and "deep storytelling" — for showing that election-integrity rumors are less about false facts than about frames and collaborative narrative performance layered onto often-accurate evidence. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes a structurally similar insight computationally, using Greimas' actantial model via LLM to show video content is far more narratively polarized than the comments beneath it. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] and [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] both push against simple echo-chamber narratives from different angles — Reddit polarization as "sedimented" precondition rather than emergent outcome; "woke" discourse as separately and synergistically politicized across Twitter/YouTube. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] extend this discursive attention to TikTok specifically, showing platform affordances shape (but do not simply polarize) immigration and securitization narratives.

## Advertising, persuasion, and the rise of generative AI

A distinct and rapidly growing thread examines political advertising and, increasingly, conversational AI as persuasive infrastructure. Classic ad-effects work ([[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Kim2026-wg]], [[Votta2025-xz]], [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]]) is now flanked by a wave of LLM-persuasion studies: [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] systematically decompose what makes conversational AI persuasive (information density, post-training, prompting) and show AI can out-persuade even expert human debaters, while [[Lin2025-xp]] demonstrates cross-national persuasion effects (US, Canada, Poland) exceeding traditional ad benchmarks. These papers converge on a shared, unsettling finding: persuasiveness and factual accuracy trade off, and models advocating right-leaning candidates are systematically less accurate — a finding that resonates with [[Renault2025-uh]]'s partisan-asymmetry results in a different domain. [[Much2026-gu]]'s podcast study offers an important complement, showing incidental, non-political "gendered media spaces" functioned as an ideological sorting/persuasion channel for young men in 2024 — a mechanism distinct from, but structurally similar to, deliberate AI or ad-based persuasion.

## Methods as a throughline

Finally, a substantial methodological seam runs beneath nearly every substantive thread: whether and how LLMs can serve as measurement instruments for political communication research. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] (ideological scaling via prompting), [[Lee2026-je]] (inferring political alignment from nonpolitical text), [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] (embeddings and LLM-in-the-loop pipelines), [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] (multi-modal and unsupervised framing analysis), and [[Paci2025-ag]] (testing LLMs' limits on implicit/manipulative political language) collectively document both the promise and the fragility of treating generative models as annotators, coders, and scaling instruments for political text — a methodological question that increasingly determines what substantive claims about polarization, persuasion, and media capture can even be made.
