# Political Communication & Elections

## Overview

Across these ninety-five papers, the MINE programme's anchor topic has grown from a methodological curiosity — can Facebook Likes predict who wins? [[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]] — into a sprawling, multi-national research infrastructure for studying how elections are contested, narrated, and potentially corrupted in digitally mediated public spheres. The papers trace three entangled arcs: (1) a *theoretical* arc moving from hybrid media systems to disinformation orders to platform-collective-behaviour frameworks; (2) a *methodological* arc running from hand-coded content analysis through network-coordination detection to LLM-in-the-loop pipelines; and (3) an *empirical* arc of recurring national case studies — Italy, Brazil, Germany, and the US — that function as a long-running comparative panel.

## From Hybrid Media Systems to the Disinformation Order

The conceptual backbone begins with Chadwick's claim that political communication is neither old nor new media but their interdependent hybrid, with power flowing through "political information cycles" rather than fixed news cycles ([[Chadwick2013-ns]], [[Chadwick2015-ii]]). Hallin and Mancini's three-models framework ([[Hallin2004-kg]]) supplies the comparative scaffolding that later studies invoke to justify treating Italy, Austria, or Brazil as non-US test cases for theories built on American data. Giglietto's own trajectory tracks this hybridity empirically in Italy: from second-screen talk-show engagement ([[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], [[Giglietto2015-320951c4]]) to a 2020 diagnosis of how big data reshapes political communication practice and scholarship ([[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]).

By the mid-2010s the field's optimism about networked publics curdles into concern about an emergent "disinformation order" ([[Bennett2018-ys]], [[Freelon2020-yp]]), crystallized by Benkler, Faris and Roberts's influential argument that the 2016 crisis was asymmetric and institutional rather than technologically determined, rooted in a radicalized right-wing media ecosystem rather than Russian bots or Facebook's algorithm per se ([[Benkler2018-lw]]). This asymmetry thesis recurs as an empirical throughline: Törnberg's 26-country study finds that misinformation sharing by parliamentarians is driven specifically by the *interaction* of populism and right-wing ideology ([[Tornberg2025-ir]]), Knüpfer traces how "Critical Race Theory" became a connective-faction issue binding Republican elites to hyper-partisan media ([[Knupfer2025-vt]]), and Heft's hyperlink analysis shows US right-wing sites anchoring a loosely transnational right-wing news ecology ([[Heft2021-ky]]). Humprecht's cross-national resilience framework operationalizes exactly this insight structurally, finding the US a uniquely vulnerable outlier among eighteen democracies ([[Humprecht2020-gd]]) — a finding echoed in Fletcher's half-century trust decline, where the US again proves exceptional rather than representative ([[Fletcher2026-lv]]).

## The Minimal-Effects Counter-Current

Running parallel to (and often explicitly against) alarmist disinformation narratives is a robust "minimal effects" tradition. Allcott and Gentzkow's foundational calculation that the average American saw only about one fake news story in 2016 ([[Allcott2017-yz]]) is echoed by Guess's finding that untrustworthy-site exposure was concentrated and modest ([[Guess2020-rr]]), Grinberg et al.'s discovery that fake news engagement was dominated by a tiny "supersharer" fringe ([[Grinberg2019-ua]]), and Eady's demonstration that exposure to the Russian IRA campaign was dwarfed by domestic news and showed no measurable attitudinal effect ([[Eady2023-xg]]). This lineage culminates in the landmark Meta-collaboration studies around the 2020 US election: Allcott's null finding for political ad removal ([[Allcott2025-jb]]), Guess's null finding for chronological-vs-algorithmic feeds ([[Guess2023-ur]]), González-Bailón's reach-and-diffusion mapping ([[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]), and Appel's demonstration that deceptive-network exposure correlations vanish once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled ([[Appel2026-qr]]). Yet this consensus is unsettled by newer causal work: Gauthier's X field experiment finds the algorithmic feed *does* shift attitudes rightward via a persistent "following" mechanism that explains why switching off the algorithm fails to reverse effects ([[Gauthier2026-iq]]), and Brady's Bluesky Registered Report shows engagement-based ranking distorts perceived social norms around partisan hostility, suggesting the null results from 2020 may reflect platform- or period-specific conditions rather than a general law ([[Brady2026-ln]]). Bail's bot-exposure experiment complicates the normative hope that cross-cutting exposure heals polarization, showing backfire effects especially among Republicans ([[Bail2018-fk]]) — a finding in tension with Bakshy's earlier, more optimistic Facebook "echo chamber" study emphasizing user choice over algorithmic suppression ([[Bakshy2015-rn]]), Flaxman's modest-segregation findings ([[Flaxman2016-lm]]), Messing's demonstration that social endorsements override partisan source cues ([[Messing2014-jc]]), and Barberá's twin papers establishing ideal-point estimation from Twitter networks and documenting topic-dependent echo chambers ([[Barbera2015-je]], [[Barbera2015-fw]]).

## Coordination, Inauthenticity, and the Italian Election Panel

A distinct methodological lineage—anchored overwhelmingly in Italian elections—develops tools for detecting coordinated inauthentic behaviour as an alternative to content-based disinformation detection. Giglietto's sequence of papers on the 2018 and 2019 Italian elections establishes "coordinated link sharing behaviour" as an ecological, actor-agnostic signature of manipulation ([[Giglietto2019-0bd8b768]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]]), extended into a continuously updating detection workflow for the 2022 snap election ([[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]]) and complemented by partisan-insularity analysis of the 2018 campaign ([[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]). Nizzoli's UK study refines the underlying theory, showing coordination and automation are orthogonal and that coordination exists on a continuum rather than a binary ([[Nizzoli2020-cf]]). This Italian panel increasingly absorbs LLM-based methods: comparing embedding models for clustering Italian political news ([[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]]), validating LLM-in-the-loop pipelines against expert coders ([[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]), and most recently documenting how Meta's political-content-reduction policy silently suppressed Italian MPs' reach while extremist accounts adapted by posting more ([[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]) — a finding that dovetails with Bechmann's call to study platform collective behaviour as democratic infrastructure rather than isolated causal effects ([[Bechmann2026-dr]]).

## Brazil as a Parallel Anchor: WhatsApp, Narrative, and Epistemic Authority

Brazil supplies a second deep case, distinguished by the centrality of WhatsApp and by elite contestation of epistemic authority rather than purely automated manipulation. Rodarte's study of the Manaus crisis shows parliamentarians competing with journalists for gatekeeping authority through distinct narrative styles—derivative reporting, direct authority claims, and brokerage ([[Rodarte2026-dk]])—a dynamic that resonates with Divon's comparative account of lifestyle influencers becoming "political brokers" during crises in Israel and China ([[Divon2026-wx]]). Kansaon traces coordinated WhatsApp activity from misinformation to real-world mobilization culminating in the January 8 attacks ([[Kansaon2025-id]]), while Ventura's WhatsApp deactivation experiment pushes misinformation research beyond feed-based platforms entirely ([[Ventura2025-sw]]). Rossini's panel study finds mainstream news use bolsters electoral trust even among Bolsonaro voters, positioning journalism as a resilience mechanism against elite-driven disinformation ([[Rossini2026-mj]]), and Marino's three-year longitudinal study of a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network undercuts the assumption that hyperpartisan communities are stable echo chambers, revealing volatile, event-driven engagement around Lula's inauguration and the coup attempt ([[Marino2026-he]]). Ventura's companion study on the 2024 X ban demonstrates how platform governance itself can produce durable partisan "sorting ratchets" ([[Ventura2026-yc]]).

## Rumors, Narratives, and the Return of Framing

A CSCW-inflected cluster reframes misinformation as a sensemaking and narrative problem rather than a veracity problem. Starbird's evidence-frame framework shows election-integrity rumors arise from politically-charged framing of often-accurate evidence ([[Starbird2025-jj]]); Prochaska extends this to "deep storytelling," arguing that *how* a story is told matters as much as its content, and that 2022 rumors increasingly relied on audiences supplying the 2020 "deep story" from minimal cues ([[Prochaska2025-ef]]), while a companion piece identifies a stable grammar of Actor-Object-Action tropes recurring across election cycles ([[Prochaska2026-pr]]). Gaber's "strategic lying" concept similarly treats falsehood as agenda-priming tactic rather than cognitive failure, tracing Brexit and the 2019 UK election ([[Gaber2022-bk]]), and Suau Martinez's narrative-exposure survey and Mosca's "fake news as rhetorical weapon" model push the field further toward treating disinformation as strategic communication embedded in hybrid media ecosystems ([[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]], [[Mosca2026-yh]]). Berlinski's experimental work shows unsubstantiated fraud claims erode electoral confidence selectively among sympathetic partisans, resistant to fact-checking ([[Berlinski2023-rj]]) — directly relevant to understanding why corrections so often fail, a pattern Tsfati's synthesis explains via mainstream media's paradoxical amplification of fake news through correction coverage ([[Tsfati2020-uo]]).

## Measurement Innovations: Scaling Ideology and Visibility with LLMs

A fast-growing methodological seam uses LLMs to scale classic measurement problems. Le Mens shows instruction-tuned LLMs can position texts on ideological dimensions simply by asking and averaging ([[Le-Mens2025-qz]]); Eady builds a joint ideology-measurement model from URL-sharing behaviour spanning media, politicians, and the public ([[Eady2025-vm]]); Lai extends latent ideology estimation to YouTube videos via Reddit sharing patterns ([[Lai2024-to]]); Achmann-Denkler validates GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision models for visual political communication on Instagram ([[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]]); and Arora proposes multi-modal framing extraction beyond fixed, text-only frame sets ([[Arora2025-tx]]). Paci's sobering counterpoint shows current LLMs still struggle badly with implicit, pragmatically loaded political discourse in Italian speeches ([[Paci2025-ag]]), a caution relevant to Schemer's broader demonstration that measurement choices (which partisan-slant score, which polarization operationalization) can swing effect sizes sevenfold without changing their sign ([[Schemer2026-mh]]) and to Gaisbauer's call for multi-level "political cartography" beyond outlet-level partisan sharing measures (Gaisbauer2025-xz).

## Platform Governance, Data Access, and the DSA Turn

A final, increasingly urgent cluster addresses the infrastructural conditions of this entire research programme. Lukito's "price, proficiency, or permission" framework catalogues how platform data access structurally excludes non-Western and under-resourced researchers ([[Lukito2026-nb]]); Philipp surveys how the EU Digital Services Act has reshaped (and partially reopened) researcher access to election data ([[Philipp2026-tl]]); Schulte's multi-platform German election study argues the central bottleneck is not insufficient research but absent reusable infrastructure ([[Schulte2026-df]]); and Votta examines how opaque ad-delivery algorithms shape campaign reach and cost independent of advertiser intent ([[Votta2025-xz]]). This governance turn culminates in Bechmann's call to reframe platforms as democratic infrastructure rather than objects of narrow causal-effects study ([[Bechmann2026-dr]]), with Giglietto's Meta Content Library study on Italian MPs serving as a concrete test case for what collaborative, DSA-enabled transparency can and cannot reveal ([[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]).

## Persuasion in the Age of Conversational AI

A newly emerging frontier asks whether conversational AI changes the fundamentals of political persuasion previously assumed to be minimal. Hackenburg's large-scale study isolates information density as the central persuasive mechanism and documents a troubling persuasion-accuracy trade-off ([[Hackenburg2025-dj]]); a companion study shows frontier AI out-persuading even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with effects generalizing to real donations ([[Hackenburg2026-ud]]); and Lin's four-country field experiment finds brief AI dialogues shift candidate preference beyond what traditional advertising achieves, while right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less accurate—echoing the asymmetry findings from the radical-right misinformation literature above ([[Lin2025-xp]]). Together with Gattermann's finding that far-right electoral performance itself shapes voters' disinformation concerns ([[Gattermann2025-yx]]) and Darius's cross-platform finding that radical populist parties enjoy disproportionate audience support especially on TikTok ([[Darius2026-xl]]), these studies suggest the programme's next anchor question is less "does social media cause polarization" and more "how do algorithmically amplified, AI-mediated, and visually dense communication environments systematically advantage radical and populist actors" — a question these ninety-five papers, read together, show the field has been approaching from Italy, Brazil, Germany, the US, and beyond for over a decade.
