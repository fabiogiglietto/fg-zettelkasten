# Political Communication & Elections

## The Italian core: insularity, coordination, and a decade of election monitoring

The topic's oldest empirical throughline runs through Italian elections monitored by the same research lineage across a decade. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] establishes the pre-history, showing how the 2013 campaign's "second screen" Twitter conversation remained narrow and TV-driven rather than an autonomous public sphere. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] then supplies the topic's foundational construct — partisan *insularity* — showing that Five Star Movement and League-aligned news sources were more insular on Twitter and that insularity predicted a Facebook engagement signature (shares over comments) distinct from cross-partisan sources. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] reframes the problem from content to *behavior*, introducing coordinated link-sharing detection across the 2018 and 2019 campaigns and showing coordinated networks disproportionately spread problematic domains — a methodological pivot extended into a continuously-updating detection workflow for the 2022 snap election in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], which surfaces new coordinated clusters (an M5S hyperpartisan network, a religious clickbait operation, a Church-of-Almighty-God proselytism network) that static lists would miss. The same research program turns increasingly reflexive about its own tools: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] validate LLM-based classification, embedding, and clustering pipelines against the 2018/2022 Facebook corpora, wrestling with what "narrative" even means when produced by a model rather than a human coder. [[Mosca2026-yh]] extends the substantive argument into rhetoric, proposing that Italian "fake news" accusations function as strategic delegitimization rather than descriptions of misinformation. Most recently the program has turned toward platform accountability itself: [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses the Meta Content Library to show that Meta's political-content-reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' Facebook reach by 72% — and began ten months before Meta's announced global rollout — while [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends the same logic to the US, showing partisan-audience URLs are systematically under-amplified relative to journalistic-quality URLs, with the gap widening during Facebook's 2020 "break-the-glass" interventions. Together these papers trace a shift from *describing* partisan insularity to *auditing* the platform mechanisms that produce and suppress it.

## Brazil after Bolsonaro: distrust, WhatsApp, and platform bans

A second empirical anchor follows Brazilian elections into the post-Bolsonaro period. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] opens the arc with crowdsourced ad auditing during the 2018 campaign, already flagging the opacity of targeted political advertising. The 2022 election and its aftermath then generate a cluster of studies converging on a common finding: belief in electoral misinformation, not exposure to social media per se, drives democratic harm. [[Rossini2026-jn]] shows that fraud beliefs predict rising political intolerance over a three-wave panel, with messaging-app news use working indirectly through those beliefs; its companion [[Rossini2026-mj]] shows the same misinformation beliefs erode trust in the certified results, while mainstream news use — even among Bolsonaro voters — rebuilds it, positioning journalism as a source of democratic resilience against an illiberal incumbent's own disinformation. [[Ventura2025-sw]] pushes the empirical lens off public feeds entirely, using a WhatsApp deactivation experiment to show that misinformation dynamics extend well beyond Facebook- and Twitter-centric feeds, and [[Kansaon2025-id]] documents the organizational payoff of this closed ecosystem, tracing coordinated WhatsApp messaging from doxxing of Supreme Court justices to real-world mobilization around the January 8, 2023 attacks. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] examines a different crisis moment — the Manaus healthcare collapse — to show government critics, Bolsonaro allies, and local brokers each construct distinct epistemic-authority claims that bypass journalistic gatekeeping. Finally, [[Ventura2026-yc]] extends the arc past Bolsonaro's defeat into Brazil's 2024 platform politics, showing that the state's ban on X produced an asymmetric "sorting ratchet": conservative users circumvented the ban while liberals went silent, permanently shifting the platform's political center of gravity rightward — a sobering coda on how state intervention itself can deepen partisan segregation. [[Marino2026-he]] closes the loop by showing that even the paradigmatic hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro Facebook community was not a stable echo chamber but an emotionally volatile formation that fractured around the January coup attempt, undercutting static assumptions about hyperpartisan insularity that the earlier Italian literature had helped establish.

## Beyond Italy and Brazil: Germany, Scandinavia, and the European campaign landscape

The topic's geographic reach widens considerably through German and Scandinavian election studies and comparative EU work. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] uses multimodal LLMs to study candidate visibility on Instagram in the 2021 German federal campaign, while [[Schulte2026-df]] treats the 2025 German election less as a substantive case than as an infrastructure problem, cataloguing the bottlenecks of scraping X and TikTok in tandem and identifying a distinctive TikTok "politainment" style in which critical text pairs with calm vocal delivery. Nordic multiparty systems supply a useful contrast to polarized two-party contexts: [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] shows that "Angry" Facebook reactions—disproportionately earned by populist parties—predict sharing more than "Love" does across Norway, Sweden, and Denmark, while [[Larsson2026-ro]] traces a decade of the Norwegian Progress Party's Facebook communication, finding negativity rising sharply under a new leader and driving shares/comments even as likes track positive content — a leader-effect finding that resonates with Kalsnes's populist-anger result. Populist and far-right visual strategy recurs across the EU 2024 elections: [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] documents the Sweden Democrats' platform-tailored securitizing memes on TikTok versus argumentative posts on Facebook/X, [[Iris2026-pg]] finds the Radical Right disproportionately visible in mainstream news coverage of the 2024 European Parliament elections relative to polling and results, and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance directly to voters' disinformation concerns. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] extends this into the UK, showing that media narratives *about* digital election interference asymmetrically polarize citizens depending on which party is framed as beneficiary — regardless of whether interference itself worked. Comparative and adjacent national cases round out the picture: [[Balluff2026-bv]] uncovers an Austrian "journalism for sale" scandal via computational content analysis, [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents Serbia's SNS mimicking authentic grassroots support to evade CIB detection, [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes Philippine influence operations as political brokerage, and [[Makeev2026-ma]] examines Russia's dual domestic/foreign wartime propaganda logic — together suggesting that election-adjacent manipulation is a global, not Western-specific, phenomenon that pure US/Italy/Brazil case studies risk obscuring.

## Theorizing polarization: concepts, critiques, and measurement wars

A substantial cluster interrogates polarization itself as a construct rather than an outcome to be measured straightforwardly. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s systematic review is the sharpest meta-critique: across 89 studies, depolarization recommendations are almost never grounded in the authors' own evidence, resting instead on unexamined deliberative-democratic ideals — a caution that should inform how every empirical claim in this topic is read. [[Hartmann2025-px]] performs an analogous service for echo chambers and filter bubbles, arguing that scholarly disagreement about their existence stems largely from inconsistent operationalization rather than differing realities, and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] extends the critique geographically, calling for polarization research that escapes its near-total dependence on US data. Several papers propose new measurement primitives. [[Elfes2026-jb]] introduces "narrative polarisation" — divergence in how actors are cast via Greimas's actantial roles — and finds, counterintuitively, that YouTube comments *reduce* surface polarization relative to videos even as deeper narrative motifs preserve partisan difference; [[Dehghan2026-sy]] finds an analogous complication on Reddit, where cross-ideological overlap is minimal even between discursively similar subreddits, undermining r/politics's presumed role as a cross-cutting town square and instead describing a "sedimentation" of polarization from the entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] proposes a "hostile misinformation effect" — a partisan-perception analogue to the hostile media effect — finding citizens across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland believe their in-group is more targeted by misinformation than their out-group, moderated by interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation. [[Schemer2026-mh]] shows methodologically that the *strength* (not direction) of media-slant/polarization relationships in US panel data swings widely across 504 measurement specifications, echoing Hartmann's and van Eck's calls for operational transparency. [[Esau2025-tf]] and [[Green2025-ap]] offer more normatively oriented interventions: the former proposes deliberative reciprocity as an antidote to "destructive polarization," while the latter shows that domain-level partisanship scores mask substantial story-level heterogeneity in "curation bubbles," reframing homogeneous consumption as networked identity performance rather than pure threat. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] adds a platform-specific angle, asking whether alternative platforms themselves drive discursive polarization.

## What do platforms actually do? Algorithmic causal effects and the Meta2020 legacy

A methodologically central cluster asks what feed algorithms causally do to political attitudes, anchored by the landmark null findings of the 2020 Meta collaboration. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] is the field's founding result, showing individual click choices — not the News Feed algorithm — are the dominant filter on cross-cutting exposure. [[Allcott2025-jb]] extends this experimental tradition to political advertising, finding six weeks of ad removal produced no detectable attitudinal effects before the 2020 election, while [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] characterizes the diffusion structure of that era's misinformation at scale. [[Munger2025-cz]] then delivers the sharpest metascientific verdict on this whole collaboration: its findings lack "temporal validity" on platforms that change too fast for one-off experiments to generalize, and inconsistent operationalizations of "echo chamber" across the papers undermine "poetic validity" — an argument that dovetails with Hartmann's and van Eck's measurement critiques above. Later studies complicate the null-effects consensus. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds X's algorithmic feed *does* shift attitudes conservative-ward via a persistence mechanism — users follow conservative accounts that outlast the algorithm switch — explaining why Meta's null result may not generalize to X's ownership and design; [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] similarly finds right-leaning algorithmic advantage on pre-X Twitter is explained by agitating content and Musk's own interaction patterns rather than partisanship per se. [[McNally2025-dn]] challenges the "black box" framing of recommender systems directly, showing Facebook's documented News Feed changes produce detectable ~19-24-day lagged effects on Guardian engagement. [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles algorithmic from user-choice effects on YouTube using a novel audit design, finding strong content "rabbit holes" but no independent echo-chamber or radicalization pathway — though a platform-wide conservative tilt regardless of user identity. [[Brady2026-ln]] closes this cluster with a prosocial design intervention: a "diversified extremity" Bluesky algorithm reduces amplification of moralized/toxic content and improves norm-perception accuracy without harming enjoyment, reframing the mechanism from direct attitude change to distorted perception of social norms. [[Votta2025-xz]] adds that ad-delivery algorithms themselves shape political-ad reach and cost, a market-design layer largely invisible in academic debate.

## Misinformation as strategy: elites, narratives, and crowdsourced moderation

Running parallel to the algorithmic-effects debate is a reconceptualization of misinformation itself as elite strategy and cultural narrative rather than discrete false claims. [[Marwick2026-qd]] is foundational here, arguing disinformation succeeds as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative*, an argument extended empirically by [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]], who show 2020–2022 US election-fraud rumors operate through evidence-frame interactions and "deep stories" that shift from explicit story-building in 2020 to implicit allusion in 2022. [[Goel2025-iq]] complements this narrative turn methodologically, showing mainstream — not fake-news — articles are disproportionately co-shared to support misleading narratives, a phenomenon domain-level classification entirely misses. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] locates the elite-strategy argument in comparative party politics: across 26 countries, neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts parliamentarians' misinformation sharing, but their *combination* — radical-right populism — does, while [[Tai2026-qk]] asks a related institutional-checks question about US elected officials. [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] examine concentrated, targeted manipulation infrastructures — Meta-identified deceptive networks and geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads in the 2016 and 2020 US elections — with Kim's use of verified turnout data addressing exactly the exposure-measurement gap that undermined earlier null IRA findings. The crowdsourced-moderation turn receives sustained scrutiny: [[Renault2025-uh]] finds Republicans are flagged 2.3x more than Democrats under X's Community Notes even though the bridging algorithm requires cross-partisan agreement, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows this is a structural feature — Community Notes systematically undermoderates precisely the polarizing content least likely to earn cross-partisan consensus, with electoral-integrity implications as the system is rolled out globally. Rounding out this cluster, [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as a bot-amplified narrative-capture tactic, [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-audit accounts produced network-wide "ripple effects" beyond the removed nodes, [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] models social bots as agenda-builders against Ohio midterm campaigns, [[Wang2026-ub]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] examine adjacent dynamics of platform migration and hashtag-based dissent, [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] reviews Facebook Reactions research showing anger consistently drives populist engagement, and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames the entire subfield's WEIRD bias as a problem needing comparative correction — a call this topic's Brazilian, Serbian, and Philippine cases begin to answer.

## Mapping the information ecosystem: ideology, visibility, and alternative media

A methods-adjacent cluster develops tools for locating actors and outlets within ideological space and tracing the reach of alternative media. [[Eady2025-vm]] provides perhaps the topic's most technically ambitious contribution, a joint measurement model placing media, politicians, and ordinary users on a common ideology scale from URL-sharing behavior, finding ideologically extreme legislators in less competitive districts share disproportionately more polarized news. [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent-ideology estimation to YouTube videos via Reddit sharing patterns, while [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] critiques prior news-circulation research for oversimplifying political leaning and neglecting story-level dynamics — a critique [[Mosleh2024-op]] answers empirically by showing partisan/low-quality news engagement diverges substantially across seven platforms, not just Twitter and Facebook. Visibility and bias in coverage itself is examined by [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]], who finds UK newspapers selectively cite COVID scientists along partisan-precautionary lines, and by [[Jurg2025-ur]], whose audit of YouTube's 2024 EU election moderation finds inconsistent application of publisher-context labels across languages and vague removal communication. Alternative media's structural position rounds out this cluster: [[Zhu2026-tn]] shows audience overlap between alternative and mainstream outlets is low but context-dependent, rising where far-right populists have institutionalized political access, and [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates assumptions of divergence further, finding German alternative and legacy outlets reference largely overlapping named entities around Nord Stream 2 even as their network structures differ.

## Methods frontier: LLMs and computational text analysis for political discourse

A distinct methodological arc tests what large language models can and cannot do for political-communication research. On the optimistic side, [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs reliably infer hidden political alignment even from nonpolitical text, exploiting culturally politicized cues; [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows prompting-and-averaging LLMs can position political texts on ideological scales competitively with established scaling methods; and [[Lin2025-xp]] finds brief LLM dialogues produce persuasion effects exceeding traditional political ads across four elections, driven mainly by factual argumentation rather than manipulation — though right-leaning LLM advocacy is systematically less accurate. On the more cautionary side, [[Paci2025-ag]] finds current LLMs substantially underperform human experts at interpreting implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political speech, a pragmatic-competence gap that qualifies enthusiasm elsewhere in this cluster. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] push automated framing analysis toward multimodality and unsupervised discovery respectively, both arguing that predefined frame categories and text-only analysis miss substantial editorial and rhetorical structure — a methodological ambition that connects back to [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]]'s multimodal candidate-visibility work and to the Giglietto-group's own LLM-pipeline validation efforts in the Italian cluster above.

## Data access, platform governance, and the DSA turn

Finally, a growing set of papers treats data access itself as the topic's central methodological and political constraint. Bastos2025-ya retrospectively frames Twitter's open API era as an anomaly whose closure ruptured "Twitter studies" as a subfield. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] survey the resulting landscape of DSA-mandated and platform-permission tools, with Lukito's "price, proficiency, or permission" framework showing production-side data vastly outstrips consumption-side data and access remains WEIRD-centric. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] offers the field's most optimistic institutional read, arguing current friction around DSA Article 40 reflects regulation finally constraining platform power rather than regulatory failure, while [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues for reconceiving platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure rather than a target of narrow causal-effects designs — a call that resonates with Munger's critique of the Meta2020 partnership and with the Giglietto group's own turn toward auditing platform governance interventions directly. Read together, this final cluster suggests the topic's next empirical anchor may be less about any single election than about the shifting institutional conditions — DSA compliance regimes, vetted-researcher programs, content libraries — under which all of the studies above become possible at all.
