# Political Communication & Elections

## The Italian roots and the MINE arc

The programme's founding empirical gesture is a shift from asking "is this content false?" to asking "who is acting together, and how?" [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] inaugurates this with its concept of coordinated link sharing behavior around the 2018 Italian election and 2019 European election, arguing that disinformation research should move from content- and actor-level detection toward an ecological account of coordinated collective action. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] complements this with an attention-economy reading of the same 2018 campaign, showing how populist supporters (especially the Five Star Movement) strategically mobilize shares versus comments to amplify favorable and contest unfavorable coverage — insularity and partisanship as two faces of the same hybrid-media dynamic theorized earlier in [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s study of Twitter-as-second-screen during the 2013 campaign. The 2018 and 2022 Italian elections recur as a longitudinal testbed for methodological innovation: [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends coordination detection into a continuously updating workflow; [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] turn to embedding models and LLM-in-the-loop pipelines for clustering and labeling Italian political news narratives, explicitly grappling with how to validate AI-assisted content analysis. Giglietto's methodological reflections bookend this arc: an early conceptual paper on the "three consequences of big data" for political communication practice and scholarship [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]], and a recent working paper using the DSA-mandated Meta Content Library to detect an undisclosed shift in Facebook's reach for Italian MPs [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] — closing the loop from platform-behavior detection to platform-accountability auditing.

## Beyond Italy: comparative election studies

The programme's geographic center of gravity widens steadily outward. Germany supplies two methodologically adjacent studies of the 2021 and 2025 federal elections: [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs for visual political communication on Instagram, while [[Schulte2026-df]] treats the 2025 election as a case study in the infrastructural difficulty of multi-platform (X/TikTok) electoral discourse analysis, arguing that observability itself should be built as reusable research infrastructure. Brazil becomes the single most intensively studied non-US case: [[Rodarte2026-dk]] traces how parliamentarians contest epistemic authority during the Manaus COVID crisis; [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use panel surveys around the 2022 election to link misinformation belief to political intolerance and eroded trust in results; [[Kansaon2025-id]] documents WhatsApp-based coordination linking fake news to real-world mobilization (including the January 8 attacks); and [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Ventura2026-yc]] (by the same author) bracket the Brazilian arc with a WhatsApp-deactivation field experiment and a study of the partisan "sorting ratchet" produced by the 2024 X ban. Other national cases extend the comparative reach further: Serbia's SNS party and its mimicry-based coordinated inauthentic behavior [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]], China's 2022 COVID protests and dual-use digital repression [[Kulichkina2026-zk]], the Philippines' influence operations reconceived as political brokerage [[Gaw2025-ru]], Austria's "pay positive to go negative" media-capture scandal [[Balluff2026-bv]], Scandinavian Facebook emotion dynamics [[Kalsnes2025-zb]], Norway's decade-long Progress Party sentiment study [[Larsson2026-ro]], the Sweden Democrats' platform-tailored TikTok securitization campaign [[Bouchafra2026-ts]], and cross-national radical-right visibility in the 2024 European elections [[Iris2026-pg]] and audience-support advantage [[Darius2026-xl]]. Taken together these studies push the field past its US/UK default and toward the WEIRD-critique explicitly voiced in [[Humprecht2025-ml]] and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]], and operationalized comparatively already in [[Humprecht2020-gd]] and the foundational media-systems typology of [[Hallin2004-kg]].

## Platform experiments and the limits of causal inference

A second major vein scrutinizes what large-scale platform collaborations can and cannot tell us. The Meta2020 (FIES) studies on feed algorithms [[Guess2023-ur]] and political advertising [[Allcott2025-jb]] found strikingly null effects of chronological feeds and ad removal on polarization and knowledge — a pattern [[Munger2025-cz]] subjects to sustained meta-scientific critique, arguing that one-off, heavily pre-registered industry collaborations have low "temporal" and "poetic" validity and cannot keep pace with platform change. [[Appel2026-qr]]'s analysis of deceptive networks in the same 2020 cycle complicates the null-effects story by showing that most exposure is indirect (via reshares) and that naive engagement proxies obscure real reach concentration. Later independent and platform-cooperative work pushes back from different directions: [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s X field experiment finds a durable, asymmetric conservative shift from algorithmic feeds (because users' follow-graphs change irreversibly), reconciling earlier nulls with newer positive findings; [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky Registered Report shows engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social norms, and demonstrates that a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can partially correct this; and [[Allen2025-ot]] situates Piccardi et al.'s browser-extension LLM-reranking method as a "platform-independent" middle path between lab experiments and platform collaborations, explaining divergent findings by intervention level and moderation regime. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] offers the broadest methodological provocation: that causal-effects paradigms themselves are insufficient, and platform collective behavior should instead be studied as democratic infrastructure. This debate connects backward to classic findings that first destabilized simple echo-chamber and filter-bubble narratives — [[Bail2018-fk]]'s backfire-effect experiment, [[Flaxman2016-lm]]'s modest segregation estimates, [[Barbera2015-fw]] and [[Barbera2015-je]]'s topic-dependent polarization and ideal-point methodology, and [[Messing2014-jc]]'s finding that social endorsement cues override partisan source cues — and forward to [[Brown2026-br]]'s careful disentangling of echo chambers, rabbit holes, and radicalization on YouTube.

## Data access as a research-political problem

A distinct but entangled cluster treats the infrastructure of data access itself as the object of study, propelled by the post-API-age crisis and the EU Digital Services Act. [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Lukito2026-nb]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] variously survey DSA-mandated APIs, catalogue 72 access tools along "price, proficiency, permission" lines, and argue that current turbulence in Article 40 implementation reflects regulation finally constraining platform power rather than failing. These normative and infrastructural arguments give practical texture to [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]'s empirical use of the Meta Content Library and to [[Schulte2026-df]]'s documentation of scraping bottlenecks, forming a coherent sub-programme on researcher access as a democratic-infrastructure question in its own right, continuous with [[Munger2025-cz]]'s critique of one-off collaborations and [[Bechmann2026-dr]]'s infrastructural reframing.

## Conceptualizing disinformation, misinformation, and "fake news"

Running beneath the empirical studies is a long conceptual argument about what "misinformation" even is. The foundational statements — [[Bennett2018-ys]]'s "disinformation order," [[Freelon2020-yp]]'s definitional essay distinguishing disinformation from misinformation and propaganda, and [[Allcott2017-yz]]'s economic model of fake news — are joined by a wave of papers arguing that source- or domain-level operationalizations miss where the real problem lies. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reframes disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic narrative rather than discrete false claims; [[Goel2025-iq]] shows mainstream news is itself repurposed into misleading narratives via co-sharing with fake news; [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop evidence-frame and "deep story" frameworks for how true facts become misleading through collective sensemaking around US election integrity rumors; and [[Mosca2026-yh]] inverts the whole paradigm by treating "fake news" accusations themselves as strategic rhetorical weapons. Against this, the empirical "minimal effects" tradition — [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Eady2023-xg]] — repeatedly finds fake-news exposure and sharing concentrated among small, already-committed subpopulations, with limited attitudinal consequence, a finding extended to images by [[Yang2023-cg]] and to diffusion dynamics by [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] bridges these camps by showing mainstream media's corrective coverage is itself a major disseminator. Several papers locate misinformation as an elite/party-strategic phenomenon rather than a diffuse audience pathology: [[Tornberg2025-ir]] ties misinformation sharing to radical-right populism specifically; [[Knupfer2025-vt]] traces how hyper-partisan media become "digital surrogates" radicalizing intra-party factions; [[Tai2026-qk]] examines institutional checks on elected officials' misinformation sharing; and [[Bennett2025-xs]] synthesizes these into a "connective faction" account of democratic backsliding that explicitly supersedes individual-cognition-centered disinformation research.

## Coordinated inauthentic behavior as a transnational method

Building directly on the MINE programme's Italian coordination-detection innovations, a cluster of papers exports and refines the method globally: [[Nizzoli2020-cf]]'s UK 2019 study formalizes coordination as continuous rather than binary and shows it is orthogonal to automation; [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as strategic opinion warfare; [[Oprea2025-lf]] applies an "Authenticity Matrix" to Romanian 2024 EU election Facebook shares; and [[Rodarte2026-dk]], [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]], and [[Kansaon2025-id]] extend coordination analysis into Brazilian and Serbian contexts. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] adds a theoretical twist, treating bots as a fourth category of agenda-builder alongside organizations, press, and public.

## Advertising, voter suppression, and campaign targeting

A narrower but consequential thread concerns paid political communication and its targeting. [[Kim2026-wg]] provides the clearest causal evidence of harm, documenting geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads in 2016 and linking exposure to turnout declines; [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] examine ad auditing and delivery-algorithm effects in Brazilian and general contexts; and [[Berlinski2023-rj]] shows unsubstantiated elite fraud claims erode election confidence among receptive partisans even when fact-checked.

## The generative-AI turn

Most recently, the programme's election focus has absorbed the question of conversational AI as a persuasive and analytic tool. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] systematically dissect the mechanisms (information density, post-training) behind LLM persuasiveness, finding AI can out-persuade even expert human debaters while trading off accuracy; [[Lin2025-xp]] extends this across four national elections, finding persuasion driven by facts rather than manipulation tactics, with a troubling right-leaning inaccuracy asymmetry; and methodological papers — [[Le-Mens2025-qz]], [[Paci2025-ag]], [[Arora2025-tx]], [[Lai2024-to]] — explore LLMs as tools for scaling ideology estimation, pragmatic/implicature analysis, multimodal framing, and video-ideology inference, signaling where the MINE programme's computational toolkit is headed next.
