# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The News Frame as a Site of Contest

A first cluster of papers treats journalism itself as the primary battleground where generative AI's meaning is fixed, contested, and circulated. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical account, showing that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs — attributing minds, intentions, or feelings to chatbots — are real but far from dominant, and that their distribution tracks outlet-level editorial culture more than national or regional difference. Crucially, anthropomorphism here is polysemous: the same metaphor can uncritically launder a corporate narrative or serve as a weapon of critique ("dumb," "bullshitters"). [[Wang2025-zy]] scales this inquiry cross-nationally, mapping UK, US, Chinese, and Indian coverage onto Cave and Dihal's utopia/dystopia axis and showing that these hopes-and-fears tropes are inflected by each country's distinct political-economic stakes rather than converging on a single global narrative. Read together, these two papers suggest a layered causality: beneath any national "AI imaginary" sits an intra-national struggle among outlets and journalists over which frame — economic, mentalistic, existential, cautionary — gets airtime.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this terrain conceptually, arguing that what looks like discourse is better understood as *infrastructure*: AI hype is not simply a narrative bubble but a systemic force that redistributes legitimacy and resources through sourcing practices, professional anxiety, and communities of practice. This matters for the other news-focused papers, because it explains a mechanism behind Nguyen's finding of outlet-driven variation and behind Wang's national divergence — hype's infrastructural embedding in newsroom routines (habitual reliance on tech-executive sources, competitive pressure to cover OpenAI) predates and conditions any particular frame. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] supplies a case study of exactly this dynamic at the level of a single product: Google's AI Overviews. Its tripartite discourse analysis (Google, journalists, SEO marketers) shows the same sourcing/legitimacy struggle Dodds theorizes playing out concretely, with journalists occupying the "hype watcher" role Dodds identifies, SEO marketers driven by commercial anxiety, and Google deploying "experimental" hedges to manage accountability — while the user's voice and questions of environmental or democratic cost are conspicuously absent, an absence that echoes Dodds's point about hype's tendency to render labor and infrastructure invisible.

## Beyond the Newsroom: Industry Self-Narration and Platform Logics

A second strand shifts from journalism to the discourse communities that generate the raw material journalists later frame. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] dissects the Sam Altman controversy as a moment when industry-internal imaginaries — Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, Techno-Hagiography — surfaced and were actively deployed through strategies of "premediation" and "preclusion" to displace present harms into speculative futures or claim exclusive authority over remediation. This connects directly to [[Hepp2026-oi]], which distinguishes the "loud futuring" of CEOs (the register Stanusch's LinkedIn material largely captures) from a "quiet futuring" diffused through Bay Area labs, EA/Rationalist communities, and physical sites like AGI House — arguing the two are bidirectionally coupled, with quiet futuring supplying the long-termist vocabulary that loud futuring later condenses into public soundbites. Hepp's insistence that quiet futuring presents itself as apolitical while being deeply constitutive of AI's power geometries gives historical depth to Stanusch's synchronic snapshot of Longtermism's ideological work. [[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal Twitter analysis of 2012–2021 supplies the prehistory to both: it shows that industry dominance of AI discourse was not inevitable but diverged sharply by country, with Germany institutionalizing toward academic/advocacy/governmental actors while the US moved toward influencer-driven individualization — a trajectory that plausibly seeded the very "loud futuring" ecosystem Hepp and Stanusch examine in its mature, post-ChatGPT form.

[[Helmond2026-ll]] operates at a different register, theorizing the infrastructural rather than discursive substrate beneath these imaginaries: platformization's shift from open-web signal capture to "Big AI" ecosystems (satellites, defense contractors, energy-hungry compute) that materially underwrite the imaginaries analyzed elsewhere. Where Stanusch and Hepp track what industry says about AI's future, Helmond tracks what industry actors are concretely building, arguing that platform businesses are re-architecting themselves around AI in ways that make prior "platformization" vocabulary analytically threadbare — a useful corrective to purely discursive accounts, reminding this cluster that imaginaries are entangled with (and sometimes obscure) infrastructural reconfiguration.

## Publics, Attitudes, and the Limits of Discourse

[[Fattorini2026-bo]] pivots from elite/media discourse to lay reception, showing that Italian publics hold a "critical ambivalence" toward AI that is not simply downstream of media exposure — a persistent gap exists between exposure and self-assessed understanding, and acceptance is highly domain-contingent (embraced for security, resisted for journalism, art, and labor management). This complicates the top-down assumption, implicit in the media-framing papers, that newspaper or industry imaginaries straightforwardly produce public belief; instead, symbolic residues (the humanoid robot image), direct tool-use experience, and demographic position mediate reception in ways that outrun any single discursive frame.

## Theorizing the Medium and Its Agency

A final pair of papers steps back from empirical discourse analysis to ask what generative AI *is*, conceptually, as object of imagination. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes the perennial "is AI an agent?" question as a demarcation problem in social theory, arguing agency is not a technical property but a socially granted and monitored license — a move that retroactively illuminates why Nguyen's mentalistic framings matter so much: attributing agency to an LLM in a newspaper article is not a neutral description but itself part of the social practice that grants (or withholds) agentive status. [[Manovich2026-ih]] and [[Galip2026-ix]] then offer two contrasting media-theoretical accounts of generative AI's distinctive properties as a creative medium. Manovich, bracketing ethical and social critique, identifies AI as the first medium with "built-in" cognition — an encyclopedic media-historical knowledge that is simultaneously enabling (unprecedented degrees of freedom) and structurally conservative (anti-avant-garde, biased toward academic/photographic convention, entangling style and content). Galip's account of "slop" and "brainrot," by contrast, insists on the ambivalence and cultural stigma Manovich's formalist approach brackets, using Ngai's gimmick theory to show the same medium simultaneously working "too little" (deskilling, laziness) and "too hard" (algorithmic attention-capture) — an aesthetic contradiction that mirrors, at the level of form, the industry contradictions Stanusch and Dodds locate at the level of discourse (claiming effortlessness while depending on hidden labor and infrastructure). Read against each other, Manovich's "medium that thinks" and Galip's "degenerative" gimmick stake out opposite evaluative poles of the same underlying observation: generative AI's outputs are neither pure creative liberation nor pure cultural waste, but a structurally conservative, labor-effacing, recursively unstable medium whose cultural meaning — like its news coverage, industry narrative, and public reception — remains persistently contested rather than settled.
