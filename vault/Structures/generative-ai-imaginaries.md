# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The News Media as an Imaginary-Making Machine

A cluster of these papers treats journalism not as a passive mirror of AI but as an active co-producer of its sociotechnical imaginaries. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical anatomy of this process, tracking how mentalistic-agentic language attributing minds to LLMs circulates across a large multinational corpus. Its key intervention is to refuse a flat "anthropomorphism is bad" verdict: the same metaphors that uncritically launder tech-industry narratives can also power adversarial critique (calling chatbots "bullshitters"), and variation in framing tracks editorial idiosyncrasy more than national culture. [[Wang2025-zy]] complements this at a more macro, comparative register, mapping utopian/dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian coverage through Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework, arguing that these imaginaries are systematically shaped by each country's political-economic stakes rather than by any single global narrative. Where Nguyen and Wang analyze content, [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes the object of study itself: AI hype is not merely a discourse to be content-analyzed but a *structuring infrastructure*—a set of sourcing routines, professional anxieties, and resource-legitimating mechanisms embedded in journalism's community of practice. This editorial's call for "breakdown and repair" (rendering hype's mechanisms visible rather than simply debunking claims) offers a meta-critical vantage point that implicitly asks what a study like Nguyen's mentalistic-framing taxonomy, or Wang's utopia/dystopia mapping, is actually diagnosing: individual rhetorical choices, or symptoms of an entrenched sourcing economy that privileges tech executives as default AI experts.

## Where Imaginaries Are Made: Industry, Community, and Platform

A second strand shifts the site of inquiry from press coverage to the discourse communities that generate the raw material journalists later report on. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] dissects a single controversy—Sam Altman's November 2023 ouster and reinstatement—as a moment when latent industry imaginaries (Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, Techno-Hagiography) surfaced and were actively managed through "premediation" (deferring critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning industry as the only legitimate problem-solver). This dovetails with [[Hepp2026-oi]], which zooms out from single controversies to the durable spatial and social infrastructure of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring"—the labs, EA/Rationalist networks, and appropriated places (start-up houses, community hubs) that supply the ideational reservoir loud CEO pronouncements later amplify. Both papers converge on a claim that runs against Nguyen and Wang's more surface-level content findings: the imaginaries circulating in mainstream coverage are not organically emergent but are actively cultivated, defended, and exported from a fairly narrow set of industry-proximate communities, with Effective Altruism/Rationalism (per Hepp) or Longtermist AGI-risk framing (per Stanusch) functioning as shared ideological substrate. [[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes this dynamic, showing that the industry capture of AI discourse visible in 2023-2025 was not inevitable but the endpoint of a decade-long trajectory: US Twitter discourse moved from institutional diversity toward individual tech-influencer dominance, while German discourse institutionalized around academia, advocacy, and government. This longitudinal, comparative finding supplies a causal backstory for why Wang's cross-national utopia/dystopia patterns and Stanusch's industry-imaginary typology look the way they do in Anglo-American contexts specifically.

[[Weinbrand2026-sf]] narrows this industry/platform lens onto a concrete product—Google's AI Overviews—showing how discursive contestation among Google, journalists, and SEO marketers reframes search itself as an epistemic and infrastructural battleground. Its finding that user, environmental, and democratic concerns are systematically *absent* from all three actors' discourse echoes Dodds's point about hype's constitutive invisibilities (hidden labor, environmental cost), suggesting a structural pattern across this literature: imaginaries are defined as much by what they exclude as by what they proclaim.

## A Media-Theoretical Counterpoint

[[Manovich2026-ih]] stands apart from the discourse-analytic papers by bracketing social and ethical framing altogether in favor of treating generative AI as a *medium* with its own McLuhan-esque structural properties—probabilistic variability, "media cognition," aesthetic conservatism, style/content entanglement. This is a productive friction point for the topic as a whole: where Stanusch, Hepp, and Dodds insist that AI imaginaries are inescapably political and infrastructural, Manovich's move is precisely to ask what remains sayable about generative AI once the ideological register is set aside. Read alongside the discourse-analytic papers, his account can be seen as itself a distinctive *imaginary*—the medium-theoretical one—that the field's dominant framings (utopian/dystopian, agentic/non-agentic, hype/critique) tend to obscure.

## From Framing to Reception

Finally, [[Gilardi2026-hw]] closes the loop by empirically testing whether any of this discursive contestation actually reshapes audience judgment. Its finding that disclosure of AI authorship boosts short-term curiosity-driven engagement without shifting underlying quality perceptions or durable acceptance complicates the assumption—implicit across the imaginaries literature—that media framings straightforwardly cultivate public belief. Taken together, the nine papers trace an arc from *where imaginaries are produced* (Silicon Valley communities, industry controversies, platform actors) through *how they are mediated* (news framing, hype infrastructures) to *what they leave out* (labor, environment, users) and *what remains once framing is bracketed* (Manovich's medium properties) or *tested empirically against audiences* (Gilardi), giving this cluster a genuinely dialectical structure rather than a mere accumulation of case studies.
