# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## From Public Epistemology to Platform Theory: Mapping the Imaginaries of Generative AI

The papers gathered here treat generative AI not as a stable technical object but as a site of ongoing cultural negotiation — something imagined, narrated, and theorized differently depending on who is speaking and through what institutional apparatus. Three broad vantage points recur: the news media as a public epistemic arena, Silicon Valley's own internal discourse communities, and media-theoretical attempts to specify what kind of medium generative AI actually is. Across these vantage points, a shared methodological commitment emerges: imaginaries are not mere ornament on top of technology but constitutive forces that redistribute legitimacy, resources, and attention.

### News Media as a Contested Epistemic Arena

A cluster of papers treats journalism as the primary site where publics encounter and make sense of generative AI. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical account, showing that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs — attributing humanlike cognition to chatbots — are real but far from dominant, and that their deployment is driven more by outlet-level editorial culture than by regional difference; anthropomorphism, crucially, serves uncritical hype, routine explanation, and adversarial critique in roughly equal measure. [[Wang2025-zy]] complements this with a comparative lens across the UK, US, China, and India, mapping coverage onto Cave and Dihal's utopia/dystopia framework and showing that national sociopolitical context systematically shapes which hopes and fears get foregrounded. Both papers converge on the idea, inherited from Jasanoff's sociotechnical imaginaries literature, that news coverage is not a passive mirror but a co-producer of public understanding — though they diverge on emphasis, with [[Nguyen2026-vm]] probing the texture of agency-attribution and [[Wang2025-zy]] foregrounding geopolitical variation in narrative valence.

[[Dodds2026-df]] pushes this further by reframing "AI hype" itself as a structuring infrastructure of journalism rather than a discursive bubble: hype is embedded in professional practice, sourcing routines that privilege tech executives, and a temporal logic of inevitability that forecloses the question of whether AI futures should arrive at all. This systemic reading dovetails with [[Richter2026-bt]], which traces who actually drove AI discourse on Twitter in its pre-GenAI formative decade (2012–2021) and finds a striking divergence: German discourse institutionalized around academic, advocacy, and governmental actors, while US discourse individualized around industry and later tech-influencer voices — a divergence the authors argue pre-structures each country's subsequent regulatory posture toward GenAI. Where [[Dodds2026-df]] diagnoses hype as professional infrastructure, [[Richter2026-bt]] supplies a longitudinal, stakeholder-level explanation for why some national discourses were more susceptible to capture by industry voices than others.

Two further papers extend the news-media frame into audience reception and theory-building. [[Mattis2026-gu]] shows experimentally that task-specific AI disclosures in journalism uniformly depress perceived trustworthiness, with effects moderated by political position and AI literacy rather than topic — a finding that complicates any simple story of audiences "accepting" AI-generated news. [[Suk2026-ai]] steps back to propose a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework for trust in generative AI, explicitly building on — and marking a departure from — decades of mass-media and social-media trust research, positioning GenAI as a new kind of information intermediary that existing trust theories only partially capture.

### Industry Discourse and the Geography of Silicon Valley Imagination

A second cluster turns from the reception of AI narratives to their production within the industry itself. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] uses the November 2023 Altman controversy as a diagnostic event, identifying Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry-aligned imaginaries, sustained through complementary strategies of "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (absorbing critique by positioning industry as uniquely capable of resolving it) — though counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter show this discursive monopolization remains incomplete. [[Hepp2026-oi]] broadens the aperture spatially and ethnographically, distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs (the register [[Stanusch2026-ec]] largely analyzes) from a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, communities, and pioneer groups — showing how Effective Altruism and Rationalist milieux supply the long-termist ideational reservoir that loud futuring later condenses and amplifies, all while presenting itself as apolitical. Read together, these two papers suggest a two-tier imaginary-production system: a visible spectacle of controversy and charisma sitting atop a less visible but equally consequential infrastructure of place, community, and ideology.

[[Weinbrand2026-sf]] narrows the industry-discourse lens to a single contested artifact — Google's AI Overviews — tracing how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers each construct competing frames (seamless innovation vs. epistemic risk vs. commercial anxiety), with the user's voice and societal/environmental stakes conspicuously absent across all three. This absence of the lay user's perspective echoes a concern that resurfaces later in the folk-conceptualization literature below, suggesting a structural blind spot across industry-adjacent discourse generally.

### Theorizing the Societal and Infrastructural Shift

Several papers move from discourse analysis to structural theorization of what generative AI changes about media, platforms, and publics. [[Tornberg2026-im]] offers the most systematic account, arguing for a tendential transition from "platform society" to "AI society" organized around cognitive lock-in (economic), the enclosure of theory and research as infrastructural service (epistemic), and "alignment politics" displacing visibility politics (political) — unifying these through the figure of cognition itself becoming the site of enclosure. [[Helmond2026-ll]] arrives at a kindred diagnosis from within platform studies proper: revisiting her own decade-old concept, Helmond argues platformization is entering an AI-centered phase (exemplified by the Like button's retirement and Google's space-based AI infrastructure ambitions), and proposes rescuing the term from conceptual erosion by redefining it as a situated form of Manovich's "transcoding." Both papers insist on continuity-with-rupture: AI reorganizes rather than replaces platform-era infrastructures and logics, a stance that cuts against purely technologically deterministic accounts of an AI rupture.

[[Goldberg2026-eb]] takes a more normatively oriented, forward-looking view, imagining LLMs as a potential "second paradigm shift" for digital public squares — a framing notably more optimistic than the hype-critical and enclosure-critical registers of [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Tornberg2026-im]], though it shares with them a recognition that synthetic participation and AI-mediated deliberation carry real risks of diluted legitimacy and manufactured consensus.

### Generative AI as Medium: Aesthetic and Everyday Theorizations

A final cluster turns to generative AI's cultural imagination from the standpoint of content and experience rather than institutional discourse. [[Manovich2026-ih]] provides the volume's most explicit media-theoretical argument, bracketing ethical critique to ask what generative AI affords and resists as a creative medium: its probabilistic variability/control trade-off, its built-in "media cognition" of art history, its structural conservatism, and its entanglement of style and content. This formalist, McLuhan-indebted account sits in productive tension with [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Li2026-zv]], both of which theorize the devalued end of generative output — "slop" and "brainrot" — as cultural phenomena in their own right. [[Galip2026-ix]] draws on Sianne Ngai's theory of the gimmick to read slop as simultaneously working "too little" and "too hard," exposing the contradictions of cultural production under "too late capitalism," while asking whether these degraded forms retain critical or resistant potential. [[Li2026-zv]] grounds this theorizing empirically in Reddit discourse, showing that ordinary users conceptualize slop as a fuzzy, affectively contested category — defective, algorithmically amplified, resisted, yet sometimes enjoyed — introducing the notion of an "uncanny of provenance" that existing platform-labeling frameworks cannot resolve. Where Manovich asks what the medium makes possible at its most accomplished, Galip and Li ask what happens at its most degraded and most ordinary — together bracketing the medium's full affective and aesthetic range.

[[Bucher2017-qh]], chronologically prior to the generative AI boom but foundational to this cluster's method, supplies the conceptual ancestor for much of this work: the "algorithmic imaginary" as users' affectively charged sense-making about systems whose code remains hidden. Its insistence that imaginaries are productive forces — shaping both user behavior and the algorithm itself through recursive feedback — anticipates the folk-conceptualization approach of [[Li2026-zv]] and offers a methodological bridge between the institutional-discourse papers above and the user-experience-centered papers here: in both registers, what people *feel* and *imagine* about AI systems is treated not as epiphenomenal but as part of the sociotechnical system's actual operation.

### Threads Across the Topic

Read together, these sixteen papers suggest that "the" cultural imaginary of generative AI does not exist in the singular. Instead there are stratified, partially overlapping imaginaries — produced in newsrooms, Bay Area community spaces, corporate PR, academic theory, and Reddit threads — each with distinctive rhetorical mechanisms (mentalistic framing, premediation/preclusion, cognitive lock-in, the gimmick) for managing the same underlying uncertainty about what generative AI is and what it portends. The recurring methodological lesson is that these imaginaries must be studied as active and consequential — shaping funding, regulation, trust, and aesthetic reception — rather than as decorative narrative layered atop a stable technical substrate.
