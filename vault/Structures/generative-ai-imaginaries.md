# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## From News Frames to Sociotechnical Imaginaries

The starting point for this cluster is journalism itself as a site where generative AI's meaning is negotiated rather than simply reported. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical anchor here: a large-scale content analysis showing that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs — language that attributes minds, intentions, or feelings to chatbots — are real but far from dominant, and that their distribution tracks outlet and editorial culture more than national or regional difference. This matters because it complicates a common assumption in the wider literature (implicit in several other papers in this set) that anthropomorphism is the master trope of AI journalism; Nguyen shows it is one register among many, sometimes even deployed critically to mock or deflate AI claims. [[Wang2025-zy]] takes a complementary but more macro-comparative approach, mapping utopian/dystopian "hopes and fears" (after Cave and Dihal) across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers and showing that national sociopolitical context, not a single global narrative, structures which hopes and which fears get airtime. Read together, these two papers suggest that the "sociotechnical imaginary" of GenAI in the press is neither monolithic nor simply cheerleading or fear-mongering — it is a patchwork shaped by newsroom practice, national stakes, and which AI product (overwhelmingly ChatGPT, per Nguyen's data) happens to anchor the story.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes the whole question: rather than asking what content journalists produce about AI, it asks what work "hype" itself does as an infrastructural, resource-mobilizing force embedded in journalism's community of practice — sourcing conventions that privilege tech executives, the double bind of journalists as both hype-makers and hype-watchers, and the invisibilization of labor, environmental cost, and material infrastructure beneath the hype. [[Chadwick2013-ns]], despite its mismatched title, appears to be a near-identical articulation of this same argument (AI hype as systemic infrastructure rather than discourse, drawing on Star's infrastructure studies and Lave and Wenger's communities of practice), and can be read as a companion or earlier iteration of Dodds's special-issue framing — together they push the "news frames" literature from content analysis toward a political economy of hype production.

## Industry's Self-Narration and the Geography of Discourse

A second strand turns from press coverage to how the AI industry and its adjacent communities narrate themselves. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] treats the November 2023 Altman controversy as a natural experiment that surfaces industry-aligned imaginaries — Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, Techno-Hagiography — and shows how these operate rhetorically through "premediation" (deferring critique to speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning industry as sole legitimate problem-solver), while counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter reveal this discursive monopoly is incomplete. This dovetails with [[Hepp2026-oi]], which shifts scale from a single controversy to an entire regional ecology: the "loud futuring" of CEOs studied by Stanusch is, Hepp argues, only half the picture, sustained by a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and Effective Altruist/Rationalist networks that supplies the ideational reservoir (AGI timelines, x-risk, TESCREAL) later amplified by loud actors — while presenting itself as apolitical. Where Stanusch documents imaginaries as they surface in a crisis, Hepp locates their ongoing, spatially anchored production. [[Richter2026-bt]] supplies a longitudinal, comparative backstory to both: tracing US and German Twitter discourse from 2012–2021, it shows that industry dominance of AI discourse is not a constant but a historically produced and country-specific trajectory — the US drifting toward individualized tech-influencer discourse, Germany toward institutionalized academic/advocacy/governmental voices — a divergence that plausibly conditions why the imaginaries Stanusch and Hepp find so entrenched in the Bay Area look different when the discourse migrates elsewhere.

## Platforms as Contested Infrastructures of Knowledge

A third pair of papers examines how specific generative-AI products reconfigure epistemic authority. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] dissects the discursive battle over Google's AI Overviews among Google itself, journalists, and SEO marketers, arguing that AIOs are not a neutral technical upgrade but a contested redefinition of search, trust, and platform power — with the user's own voice and any concern for democratic or environmental stakes conspicuously absent from all three actors' framings, an absence that echoes Dodds's point about hype's systematic invisibilizations. [[Triedman2025-uy]] performs an empirical parallel on Grokipedia, showing how an ostensibly neutral "AI-generated encyclopedia" is in practice heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet skewed toward unreliable and ideologically aligned sources especially on controversial topics — a concrete demonstration of how imaginaries of AI as objective or authoritative knowledge infrastructure can diverge sharply from what the systems actually do.

## Theorizing the Medium Itself

The final arc moves from discourse *about* generative AI to media-theoretical accounts of what kind of medium it is. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets the ethical and political critiques that animate most of the papers above in favor of a formalist analysis: generative AI as the first artistic medium with a built-in cognitive capacity, structurally conservative and entangled in style/content in ways that recall — and depart from — prior media like photography and generative code art. This is a useful counterweight to the discourse-analytic papers, insisting that any cultural imaginary of GenAI must also reckon with its distinctive medium-properties, not just its rhetorical reception. [[Galip2026-ix]] can be read as a dialectical answer from cultural studies: where Manovich finds a medium that "thinks," Galip's account of "slop" and "brainrot" — read through Ngai's theory of the gimmick — finds a medium that works simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," producing content whose degeneracy is itself culturally symptomatic of "too late capitalism." Between them, these two papers bookend the collection's arc: from the sociotechnical imaginaries traced in news and industry discourse (Nguyen, Wang, Dodds/Chadwick, Stanusch, Hepp, Richter) and their infrastructural instantiations (Weinbrand, Triedman), to genuinely medium-specific theorizations of what generative AI *is* as a cultural object — cognitive prosthesis or gimmick, encyclopedic memory or slop machine — a tension that runs, largely unresolved, across the whole topic.
