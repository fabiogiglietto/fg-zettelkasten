# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The Media Imaginary: How Newspapers Frame Generative AI

At the center of this cluster sits a straightforward but consequential question: what does journalism *do* to generative AI as it renders it intelligible to publics? [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical answer, tracking mentalistic-agentic framings across 18,000+ articles and finding that while anthropomorphic language is present, it is neither dominant nor univocal—it serves critique as often as hype, and its distribution tracks outlet culture more than national or regional difference. This complicates any simple story of "the media anthropomorphizes AI." [[Wang2025-zy]] approaches the same terrain from a comparative sociotechnical-imaginaries angle, mapping utopian/dystopian tropes (via Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework) across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian coverage, and finding that national political-economic conditions inflect which hopes and which fears get narrated. Together these two papers suggest that "the AI imaginary" is not a single global narrative but a patterned set of local variations organized around a shared techno-capitalist master frame—economic utility versus risk/ethics, utopia versus dystopia—whose emphasis shifts by outlet and by country.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this discourse-analytic project structurally: AI hype is not merely a narrative bias to be corrected but an infrastructure embedded in journalism's professional practice, its sourcing routines, and its political economy—journalists are simultaneously hype-makers and hype-watchers. This dovetails with [[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal stakeholder analysis of pre-ChatGPT Twitter discourse, which shows that the "who" behind AI narratives—industry executives, academics, government, individual influencers—diverged sharply between the US and Germany well before the generative AI boom, prefiguring later regulatory divergence. Read alongside Nguyen and Wang, Richter's paper suggests that today's mentalistic framings and utopia/dystopia oscillations are not simply features of the technology's novelty but the sedimented residue of a decade of stakeholder composition.

## Industry Discourse and the "Quiet" Work of Futuring

A second strand shifts from journalism-as-mediator to the industry's own self-narration. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] dissects the Sam Altman firing/rehiring controversy as a moment when industry-internal imaginaries—Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, Techno-Hagiography—surfaced and were actively managed through "premediation" (deferring critique to speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning industry as the only legitimate problem-solver). [[Hepp2026-oi]] extends this by insisting that the "loud futuring" of CEOs studied by Stanusch is only half the picture: an equally consequential "quiet futuring" circulates through Bay Area labs, community houses, and Effective Altruism/Rationalist networks, supplying the long-termist vocabulary (AGI timelines, x-risk) that loud futuring later condenses and amplifies. Both papers converge on a key claim: AI imaginaries are not free-floating cultural narratives but actively produced and managed by specific actors and communities, even as they present themselves as apolitical or purely speculative.

[[Weinbrand2026-sf]] narrows this industry-discourse lens to a single artifact—Google's AI Overviews—showing how corporate, journalistic, and SEO-marketer discourses compete to define a new search feature as innovation, epistemic risk, or commercial threat, with the user's own voice and environmental costs conspicuously absent from all three registers. This absence echoes a broader pattern visible across Dodds, Stanusch, and Hepp: industry-proximate imaginaries of generative AI systematically externalize labor, environmental cost, and the mundane present in favor of speculative futures (existential risk, seamless innovation, AGI arrival) or the branding of individual founders.

## Platform Theory's Wider Angle

Two essays step back from generative AI specifically to theorize the platform ecosystem it is reshaping. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits "platformization" a decade on, arguing that the retirement of Facebook's Like button and Google's space-based AI infrastructure ("Project Suncatcher") mark a shift from the social-graph paradigm to an AI-centered platform logic—a reminder that the cultural imaginary of generative AI is entangled with a broader infrastructural and political-economic realignment ("Big AI") involving new actors like Nvidia, Anduril, and Palantir. [[Boyd2026-op]] complements this by arguing that the platforms hosting much of the discourse examined elsewhere in this cluster have themselves become "parasocial media," where scrolling and algorithmically curated content replace reciprocal exchange—a structural condition that helps explain why AI imaginaries circulate as spectacle and influencer content (cf. Richter's tech-influencer dominance, Hepp's community figurations) rather than as deliberative public discourse.

## Reception: Trust and Public Ambivalence

Where the previous strands examine production of imaginaries, [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Suk2026-ai]] turn to reception. Fattorini's survey work on Italian publics finds a persistent gap between exposure to AI discourse and actual understanding, alongside "critical ambivalence"—support for regulation rather than prohibition, context-dependent acceptance, and continued reliance on symbolic imagery (humanoid robots) that lags behind actual use of generative tools. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework for trust in generative AI that explicitly borrows from decades of mass-media and social-media trust research, implicitly arguing that the imaginaries traced elsewhere in this cluster (utopian/dystopian tropes, hype, industry mythologization) are precisely the raw material publics draw on when calibrating trust. Fattorini's empirical finding that symbolic representations persist even as practical use grows gives concrete texture to Suk's more abstract multilevel claim.

## Theorizing the Medium and Its Agency

The cluster closes with two papers that pull back from discourse analysis toward more foundational theorization. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes the question of AI "agency" itself as a demarcation problem in social theory: agency is not an intrinsic property but a socially granted and monitored license to act. This reframing quietly undercuts much of the mentalistic-agentic language studied by Nguyen and the techno-hagiographic mythologizing studied by Stanusch—both can be read as attempts (successful or contested) to grant or withhold this social license. Finally, [[Manovich2026-ih]] offers the volume's most sustained media-theoretical intervention, bracketing ethical and social critique to ask what generative AI *is* as a medium: an inherently probabilistic system trading variability for control, structurally conservative and anti-avant-garde, and—distinctively among artistic media—one that "thinks" via built-in encyclopedic cognition of art history. Manovich's insistence on medium-specific properties (style/content entanglement, resistance to logical surrealism) provides a formal counterpoint to the discourse-analytic papers: where Nguyen, Wang, Stanusch, and Weinbrand ask how generative AI is *narrated*, Manovich asks what its actual affordances make narratable in the first place—suggesting that the aesthetic conservatism baked into the medium may itself help explain why so much surrounding discourse oscillates between familiar utopian and dystopian tropes rather than genuinely novel ones.
