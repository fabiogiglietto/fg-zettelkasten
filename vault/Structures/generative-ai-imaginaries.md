# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The Discursive Construction of Generative AI

The papers gathered here converge on a deceptively simple premise: generative AI does not arrive in culture pre-interpreted, but is actively imagined, narrated, and framed into being by an assemblage of journalists, industry actors, communities, and theorists. Rather than treating "hype" or "imaginary" as a superficial gloss on a stable technical object, this cluster asks how discourse itself becomes constitutive of what generative AI *is* taken to be — its agency, its risks, its creative status, its politics.

### News Media as Epistemic Infrastructure

A strong empirical core examines journalism as the primary site where public understanding of generative AI is negotiated. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular account, showing that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs — attributing minds, intentions, or feelings to chatbots — are real but far from dominant, and that their deployment is driven more by newsroom and individual journalistic practice than by regional culture; anthropomorphism, crucially, can serve critique as easily as hype. [[Wang2025-zy]] scales this up comparatively, mapping utopian/dystopian "AI imaginaries" across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers through Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework, revealing that national political economies inflect which futures get told. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes the entire phenomenon theoretically: AI hype is not merely a narrative bubble but a *systemic infrastructure*, embedded in journalism's community of practice, its sourcing routines (which repeatedly privilege tech executives), and its geopolitical framings — journalists are simultaneously hype's producers and its watchdogs. Together these three papers trace an arc from measuring framing effects, to comparing national imaginaries, to theorizing hype as structural force — each pushing toward the conclusion that media discourse doesn't just reflect AI's meaning but actively allocates legitimacy, resources, and attention.

Two further studies extend this media lens to specific platforms and products. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows how Google's AI Overviews are contested terrain between corporate seamlessness-talk, journalistic alarm over hallucination, and SEO-marketer anxiety — with the user's own voice and any democratic or environmental concern strikingly absent from all three discourses. [[Triedman2025-uy]], though presented as an empirical content analysis rather than a discourse study, functions as a material test of imaginaries in action: Grokipedia's derivative-yet-ideologically-skewed relationship to Wikipedia shows what happens when a particular political imaginary of AI ("truth-seeking," anti-institutional) is operationalized into an actual knowledge infrastructure, complete with elevated citation of blacklisted and low-quality sources on controversial topics.

### Industry Self-Narration and the Geography of Imagination

Where the media papers ask how AI is represented *to* publics, [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]] turn to how the industry represents itself. Stanusch's controversy-mapping of the Altman firing/rehiring episode identifies Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry imaginaries, and names two rhetorical strategies — premediation (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and preclusion (absorbing critique by claiming sole competence to resolve it) — through which industry discourse manages to occupy both present and future simultaneously, though counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter show this control is incomplete. Hepp complements this with an ethnographic account of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring": beneath the loud CEO pronouncements that dominate news coverage (and that Stanusch analyzes) lies a landscape of labs, EA/Rationalist communities, and appropriated physical places whose discursive thickenings around AGI, job loss, and geopolitics supply the very ideas that loud futuring later amplifies. Read together, these two papers suggest a circuit: quiet community discourse generates raw imaginative material, which crisis moments like the Altman controversy condense into publicly visible, strategically deployed narratives.

[[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes this industry-centrism by asking who actually spoke for AI *before* the generative boom. Its longitudinal Twitter analysis (2012–2021) complicates any assumption of uniform industry dominance: Germany institutionalized toward academic/advocacy/governmental voices while the US shifted toward individual tech-influencer discourse — a divergence the authors argue prefigures each country's distinct post-2022 regulatory and discursive trajectory, tying today's imaginaries to path-dependent stakeholder formations a decade in the making.

### Publics, Ambivalence, and Reception

[[Fattorini2026-bo]] shifts the vantage point from elite discourse-producers to lay reception, surveying Italian publics and finding neither technophobia nor uncritical enthusiasm but "critical ambivalence" — high exposure without proportionate understanding, symbolic reliance on humanoid-robot imagery among the less experienced, and context-dependent acceptance that tracks direct use. This grounds the more elite-focused papers above by showing how the imaginaries they trace filter down into (and are reworked within) everyday sense-making, mediated by education, age, gender, and hands-on experience with the tools themselves.

### Theorizing the Medium and Its Cultural Detritus

A final pair pulls back from empirical discourse analysis to ask what kind of *thing* generative AI's outputs are, culturally and aesthetically. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets ethical and social critique to theorize generative AI as a medium in the McLuhanite sense, identifying built-in "media cognition," structural conservatism, and style-content entanglement as properties distinguishing it from photography or painting — a media-theoretical imaginary that treats the technology's affordances, not its politics, as the object of analysis. [[Galip2026-ix]] moves in nearly the opposite direction, taking up the stigmatized categories of "slop" and "brainrot" through Sianne Ngai's theory of the gimmick to read generative culture's cheapest, most degraded outputs as symptomatic of "too late capitalism" — simultaneously working too little (deskilling, effortlessness) and too hard (algorithmic attention-capture). Where Manovich seeks the medium's positive, art-historical specificity, Galip seeks its negative, degenerative underside; together they bookend the cluster's move from discourse *about* AI to the cultural and aesthetic imaginaries *produced by* AI itself — suggesting that the "cultural imaginary" of generative AI is not confined to what is said about the technology, but is also enacted in the very forms, genres, and sensibilities its outputs bring into being.
