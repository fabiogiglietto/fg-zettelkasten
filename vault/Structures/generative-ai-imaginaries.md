# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The Discursive Construction of Generative AI

Generative AI does not arrive in public consciousness as a neutral technical fact; it arrives already wrapped in narrative, metaphor, and institutional interest. The papers gathered here trace how this wrapping happens across sites — newsrooms, industry controversies, Silicon Valley's social fabric, platform discourse, trust research, and media theory itself — and how each site produces its own version of what generative AI *is* and what it is *for*.

### News media as imaginary-makers

Several papers treat journalism as a primary arena where sociotechnical imaginaries of AI are forged. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical account, showing across 18,032 articles that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs — attributing minds, intentions, or feelings to chatbots — exist but are far from dominant, and that their deployment is shaped more by outlet-level editorial culture than by national or regional difference. Crucially, anthropomorphism is not univocally uncritical: it can index corporate storytelling, routine simplification, or pointed critique (calling a model a "bullshitter"). This complicates any simple story of media as hype amplifier. [[Wang2025-zy]] scales the comparative lens to four national presses (UK, US, China, India), operationalizing Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework to show that utopian/dystopian tropes are patterned nationally, reflecting distinct political-economic stakes rather than a single global narrative. Read together, these two papers suggest a discourse that is neither uniform hype nor uniform anxiety, but a mosaic shaped by institutional and geopolitical variables — the "techno-capitalist master frame" Nguyen identifies is real, but its secondary inflections (risk vs. opportunity, education vs. economy) are locally negotiated.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this whole terrain: hype is not simply a discursive bubble journalists report on, but an infrastructural force with which journalism itself is entangled — mobilizing funding, redistributing legitimacy, and structuring who counts as a legitimate source (overwhelmingly tech executives). This piece functions as a meta-commentary on the other news-focused papers, arguing that studying "framing" alone risks missing how sourcing practices, professional anxiety, and geopolitical positioning (e.g., China's "loyal-facilitator" journalism vs. Germany's "European values" framing) sediment hype into durable practice. It also names what tends to be absent from coverage — labor, environmental cost, alternatives — a silence echoed elsewhere in this cluster.

### Industry as imaginary-producer

Where the news-media papers ask how AI is represented, [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]] ask how the industry represents itself, and to what strategic effect. Stanusch's controversy-mapping of the Sam Altman firing/rehiring reveals a repertoire of industry imaginaries — Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, Techno-Hagiography — sustained through two rhetorical moves: premediation (deferring critique into speculative futures) and preclusion (positioning industry as sole legitimate problem-solver). This is a sharper, more agonistic version of the "absences" Dodds identifies: climate impact, labor, and historical precedent are structurally excluded from industry self-narration, though X/Twitter counter-imaginaries (Governing Urgency, Intersectionality) show the monopoly is contested. Hepp extends this analysis geographically and sociologically, distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs (the register Stanusch's Techno-Hagiography captures) from a "quiet futuring" diffused through Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and Effective Altruist/Rationalist networks. Hepp's contribution is to show that the ideas amplified in loud, public controversy — AGI timelines, existential risk, the US-China race — are incubated in an ostensibly apolitical, spatially anchored substrate that in fact does deeply political work. Together these two papers suggest that Silicon Valley's imaginaries are not a monolith but a layered production system, in which controversies like Altman's function as moments where usually-implicit imaginaries become visible and issuefied.

[[Richter2026-bt]] provides a historical corrective to both: examining Twitter discourse from 2012–2021, before the generative AI boom, it shows that industry dominance in AI discourse is not a constant but a country-specific trajectory — Germany institutionalized toward academic/advocacy/governmental actors while the US moved toward individualized tech-influencer discourse. This paper implicitly explains *why* the imaginaries mapped by Wang and Stanusch diverge nationally and why US discourse in particular is so susceptible to charismatic, CEO-centered narrative (Hepp's "loud futuring," Stanusch's Techno-Hagiography): the stakeholder ecology enabling it was already in place a decade before ChatGPT.

### Platform-level and infrastructural framing

[[Weinbrand2026-sf]] narrows the lens to a single contested artifact — Google's AI Overviews — showing how three actor groups (Google, journalists, SEO marketers) construct competing frames around trust, authority, and platform power. This case study operationalizes, at micro scale, the same dynamics Dodds and Nguyen describe at the level of the whole discourse: corporate framing emphasizes seamlessness while hedging accountability via "experimental" labels; journalists supply the critical countervoice found sporadically in Nguyen's corpus; and, notably, the user's perspective and societal/environmental costs are absent — the same structural silence Dodds and Stanusch flag as a general feature of AI hype discourse. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a complementary theoretical move, proposing that trust in generative AI be studied multilevel (individual, institutional, societal), building on media-trust scholarship. It functions as a bridging framework: where Weinbrand shows discursively how actors compete over epistemic authority, Suk supplies the conceptual vocabulary for eventually measuring whether and how such framings translate into public trust.

### Theorizing AI as medium and cultural form

Two papers step outside the framing/discourse paradigm to theorize generative AI's own properties as a cultural-aesthetic object. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets ethical and social critique to ask what kind of *medium* generative AI is, arguing it is the first artistic medium with built-in cognition — an "encyclopedic" media memory that is simultaneously creatively enabling and structurally conservative, resistant to avant-garde defamiliarization. This is a useful counterweight to the discourse-analytic papers: it treats the "imaginary" not as something projected onto AI from outside (by journalists, executives, or platforms) but as something latent in the medium's formal properties themselves, which in turn shape what kinds of cultural production and imagination become possible.

[[Galip2026-ix]] performs an inverse move, taking up the debased, "degenerative" end of generative culture — AI slop and brainrot — and reading these dismissed genres through Sianne Ngai's theory of the gimmick as forms that work simultaneously "too little" and "too hard." Where Manovich locates AI's cultural specificity in high-end aesthetic production, Galip locates it in the mass-produced dross that saturates platforms, arguing that even this ostensibly meaningless output stages real contradictions of labor, value, and time under "too late capitalism." Read against the hype-and-imaginary papers, slop and brainrot function as a kind of unglamorous, unmarketable shadow discourse — the material residue that industry techno-hagiography and journalistic sensationalism alike tend not to mention, yet which arguably constitutes generative AI's most visible everyday cultural presence.

### Across the cluster

Taken together, these ten papers describe a layered ecology of imagination: industry produces imaginaries strategically (Stanusch, Hepp) and via infrastructural hype (Dodds); news media refract, amplify, and occasionally contest these imaginaries unevenly (Nguyen, Wang); platforms stage micro-battles over authority and trust in the discourse's connective tissue (Weinbrand, Suk); historical stakeholder patterns explain why these dynamics diverge by country (Richter); and media theory and cultural criticism (Manovich, Galip) push past framing analysis to ask what generative AI *does* as a medium and what its most degraded cultural products reveal about the contradictions all the other papers circle but rarely name outright — labor, value, temporality, and the always-incomplete work of controlling AI's meaning.
