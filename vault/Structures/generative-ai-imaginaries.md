# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The News Frame: How Journalism Constructs AI's Public Meaning

A cluster of papers treats news media as a primary site where generative AI's cultural meaning is negotiated rather than simply reported. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] provides the most granular empirical account, mapping 18,000+ articles to show that "mentalistic-agentic" framings—attributing minds to LLMs—are real but far from dominant, and that their deployment tracks outlet editorial culture more than national or regional difference. Crucially, Nguyen et al. complicate any simple condemnation of anthropomorphism: the same rhetorical move can uncritically reproduce corporate narratives, serve as routine explanatory shorthand, or function as adversarial critique (calling models "bullshitters"). This nuance pushes back against a flatter reading of AI hype as monolithic promotionalism.

[[Wang2025-zy]] scales the inquiry comparatively, applying Cave and Dihal's hopes/fears framework across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers to show that the utopia/dystopia axis is itself nationally inflected—sociotechnical imaginaries, in Jasanoff's sense, are never generic but always locally accented by political and economic stakes. Read alongside Nguyen et al., a picture emerges in which the "global AI story" is actually a bundle of divergent national stories organized around a shared techno-capitalist master frame but diverging in secondary emphasis (risk/ethics in the West, application/education in Asia).

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this entire body of work at a higher level of abstraction: hype is not merely a discursive artifact to be measured in a corpus but a *systemic infrastructure*—embedded in newsroom sourcing practices, professional anxiety, and resource allocation—that actively configures which futures become thinkable. This matters for how we read Nguyen2026-vm's and Wang2025-zy's findings: low rates of mentalistic framing or cross-national variation in tone do not mean hype is weak; hype can operate structurally (via who gets quoted, which futures are treated as inevitable) even where explicit anthropomorphic language is rare. Dodds et al.'s call for "breakdown and repair" also implicitly critiques the more descriptive, classificatory ambitions of the content-analysis papers, urging attention to what is rendered invisible—labor, environmental cost, alternative futures—rather than only what is said.

## Industry Self-Narration and Platform Discourse

A second strand turns from journalism to the discourse *produced by* the AI industry itself. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] uses the Sam Altman controversy as a diagnostic event, showing how industry-aligned discourse manages critique through "premediation" (deferring concerns to speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning the industry as uniquely capable of solving the problems it creates). This dovetails with Dodds et al.'s notion of "temporal narratives of inevitability," but Stanusch et al. add a controversy-mapping method that reveals counter-imaginaries—governance urgency, intersectional critique—showing that industry's narrative monopoly is contested, not total.

[[Hepp2026-oi]] extends this account geographically and ethnographically, distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs (the register Stanusch2026-ec largely analyzes) from a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, EA/Rationalist communities, and pioneer groups. Hepp's argument that quiet futuring supplies the ideational reservoir that loud futuring later amplifies offers a genealogy for the longtermist and AGI-inevitability discourses that Stanusch2026-ec identifies circulating on LinkedIn. Together they suggest AI imaginaries are produced through a layered ecology—informal community discourse feeding into public-facing corporate narrative—that mirrors Dodds et al.'s insistence on hype as infrastructural rather than merely textual.

[[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes this ecology, tracing the pre-ChatGPT Twitter discourse (2012–2021) in the US and Germany and showing that industry dominance of AI conversation is not a natural or inevitable condition but a divergent national trajectory: German discourse institutionalized toward academia and advocacy while US discourse individualized toward tech influencers. This paper functions as a kind of prehistory to the more GenAI-specific studies, suggesting that the stakeholder compositions Hepp2026-oi and Stanusch2026-ec describe for the 2020s were already being set in the platform discourse of the preceding decade—and that these early asymmetries help explain the transatlantic divergence Wang2025-zy finds in press coverage.

[[Weinbrand2026-sf]] sits at the seam between journalistic and industry discourse, analyzing how Google, tech journalists, and SEO marketers frame AI Overviews. Its finding that Google performs "seamless infrastructure" while journalists supply the critical counter-frame recapitulates, at platform scale, the same dual dynamic Dodds et al. describe for journalists as both hype-makers and hype-watchers. Weinbrand's striking observation that user voice and environmental/democratic concerns are structurally absent across *all three* actor discourses echoes Galip2026-ix's and Dodds2026-df's shared concern with what hype renders invisible.

## Beyond Discourse: Artifacts and Media-Theoretical Accounts

Two papers depart from discourse analysis proper to examine AI's cultural imaginary as it is inscribed in artifacts and aesthetic theory. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s empirical dissection of Grokipedia is less about framing AI in language than about how an AI imaginary (Musk's anti-"woke" alternative-knowledge narrative) gets materialized in an actual knowledge infrastructure—showing citation practices (including "LLM auto-citogenesis" citing Grok/Musk tweets) that operationalize ideological framing as sourcing bias. This is a useful limit case for the topic: an imaginary doesn't just circulate as narrative but can be built directly into a competing epistemic infrastructure, extending Weinbrand2026-sf's point about generative AI's contest over epistemic authority from search into encyclopedic knowledge itself.

[[Manovich2026-ih]] and [[Galip2026-ix]] both bracket the sociological/political register almost entirely in favor of medium-theoretical and aesthetic analysis, yet they arrive at strikingly opposed evaluative postures. Manovich, explicitly setting aside ethical critique, treats generative AI as a novel medium with its own cognitive and structural properties—"media cognition," structural conservatism, style/content entanglement—continuing a McLuhan/Boden lineage of medium specificity. Galip and coauthors, by contrast, take up the degraded, "slop"-laden output that Manovich's more affirmative framing tends to bracket, using Ngai's theory of the gimmick to hold together contradictory readings of AI content as simultaneously "too little" (deskilled, effortless) and "too hard" (algorithmically straining for attention). Where Manovich isolates the medium's positive affordances for serious cultural production, Galip et al. dwell in its disreputable excess, and in doing so implicitly answer the absences noted by Weinbrand2026-sf and Dodds2026-df: the labor, environmental cost, and "digital trash" that respectable industry and journalistic discourse alike tend to occlude resurface here as the very substance of critical inquiry.

## Threads Across the Set

Read together, these ten papers trace an arc from macro-level discourse mapping (news framing, national imaginaries) through meso-level community and industry self-narration (Silicon Valley, controversy, platform contest) down to the level of the artifact and the aesthetic object (Grokipedia, generative images, slop). What unites them is a shared skepticism toward any single, stable "AI imaginary": Nguyen2026-vm, Wang2025-zy, and Richter2026-bt insist on granular variation by outlet, nation, and era; Stanusch2026-ec and Hepp2026-oi insist on the coexistence of dominant and counter-imaginaries; and Manovich2026-ih and Galip2026-ix insist that even at the level of the object, generative AI resists singular characterization—it is simultaneously a thinking medium and a slop machine. Dodds2026-df's call to render hype's infrastructural mechanisms visible, and Weinbrand2026-sf's and Galip2026-ix's shared attention to structural absences, suggest a common critical horizon for the topic: understanding not just what generative AI is said to be, but what work—economic, ideological, aesthetic—that saying does, and what it leaves unsaid.
