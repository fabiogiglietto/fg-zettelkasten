# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## From Algorithms to Generative AI: A Genealogy of the Imaginary

The concept of a technological "imaginary" that this topic cluster mobilizes has roots predating generative AI itself. [[Bucher2017-qh]] establishes the foundational move: algorithms are not merely technical objects but are *felt*, encountered in moments of breakdown, surprise, and affective friction, and these imaginings recursively shape both user behaviour and the systems themselves. This affect-centered, non-dismissive stance toward lay "misunderstandings" of technology (imaginaries as productive rather than false) sets the methodological and theoretical template that the generative-AI papers in this cluster inherit and extend, even when they shift register from Facebook's news feed to chatbots, image generators, and search.

## Mapping Generative AI in the News

A substantial cluster of papers treats journalism as a primary site where sociotechnical imaginaries of generative AI are produced and contested. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular account, showing through large-scale content analysis that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs—attributing human-like cognition to chatbots—are real but far from dominant, and that they serve heterogeneous rhetorical functions, from uncritical reproduction of tech-industry narratives to adversarial critique. Crucially, the paper finds that variation in such framing tracks editorial and individual journalistic practice more than national or regional culture, a finding that complicates any easy mapping of "culture" onto coverage. [[Wang2025-zy]] takes the comparative-national angle directly, using Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework to quantify utopian/dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers, demonstrating that imaginaries are refracted through distinct political-economic contexts even as a shared utopia/dystopia grammar structures the coverage globally. Read together, Nguyen and Wang suggest a layered picture: a dominant techno-capitalist master frame operating globally (per Nguyen's finding on economic-commercial dominance), inflected by both national context (Wang) and local newsroom practice (Nguyen).

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this entire body of journalism research by insisting that "AI hype" is not simply a discursive pattern to be mapped but a systemic infrastructure embedded in journalism's community of practice—shaping sourcing, resource allocation, and legitimacy before technologies even materialize. This editorial usefully contextualizes the empirical findings of Nguyen, Wang, and others: the repetitive sourcing of tech executives, the near-total concentration of LLM coverage on OpenAI's products (which Nguyen documents quantitatively via ChatGPT's 82.5% mention share), and the "open-ended technological inevitability" framing are not incidental discursive choices but symptoms of how hype operates as professional infrastructure. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] narrows this lens to a single case—Google's AI Overviews—showing three actor groups (Google, journalists, SEO marketers) contesting the meaning of generative search through distinct sentiment and conceptual vocabularies, while converging in their shared silence on user perspectives and environmental costs, echoing Dodds's point about hype's structural invisibilizations.

Trust emerges as a related but distinct thread. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a theoretical multilevel framework (individual/institutional/societal) for studying trust in generative AI by extending media-trust scholarship, while [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] empirically tests a related question, finding Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-involved news—a finding that resonates with Weinbrand's account of search's growing epistemic authority and suggests that imaginaries of generative AI are not uniform across its different institutional embodiments (search vs. newsroom).

## Imagining AI from Within the Industry

A second strand turns from journalistic framing to the imaginaries circulating within the AI industry itself. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] uses the November 2023 Sam Altman controversy as a critical moment that surfaces industry-aligned imaginaries—Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, Techno-Hagiography—and theorizes the dual strategies of "premediation" and "preclusion" by which industry discourse manages critique by displacing it into speculative futures or absorbing it as something only industry can resolve. This offers a sharper, more adversarial counterpart to [[Hepp2026-oi]], whose ethnographic account of San Francisco's "quiet futuring" shows how the same long-termist, AGI-centric imaginaries (traceable to Effective Altruism and Rationalism) are sedimented not just in viral controversies but in the durable social fabric of labs, co-living houses, and community venues. Hepp's argument that this landscape is simultaneously fragmented/contradictory and coherent as ideology—presenting itself as apolitical while being deeply political—gives empirical and spatial depth to Stanusch's more discourse-analytic findings about Longtermism and regulatory ambivalence. Both papers insist that industry imaginaries actively *do work*—shaping which futures and harms become sayable—rather than merely describing anticipated technology.

[[Richter2026-bt]] supplies a longitudinal, pre-history to these industry-dominated imaginaries, tracing how US and German Twitter discourse on AI diverged across 2012–2021: Germany institutionalizing toward academic/advocacy/governmental actors, the US consolidating around industry and then individual tech influencers. This paper usefully historicizes Stanusch's and Hepp's synchronic snapshots of 2023–2026 US discourse, suggesting that the industry- and influencer-saturated imaginary documented by both papers was not inevitable but the product of a decade-long stakeholder trajectory with a clear national specificity—one that, the paper argues, continues to shape divergent regulatory postures today.

## Generative AI as Medium: Theoretical and Aesthetic Framings

A third, more conceptual strand steps back from empirical discourse analysis to theorize generative AI's distinctive properties as a medium and a cultural form. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets ethical and social critique to ask what generative AI *is* as a creative medium, identifying structural properties—probabilistic variability/control trade-offs, "media cognition" of art history, structural conservatism, and style-content entanglement—that mark it as a qualitatively new kind of medium, "the first artistic medium that itself thinks." This medium-theoretical register stands in productive tension with [[Galip2026-ix]], which theorizes the degraded end of the same productive apparatus—AI "slop" and "brainrot"—through Sianne Ngai's theory of the gimmick, arguing that these forms work simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," exposing contradictions of labour, value, and temporality under "too late capitalism." Where Manovich isolates generative AI's formal possibilities at the level of the individual artwork, Galip examines its devalued, mass-produced output as a cultural-affective genre, together bracketing the medium's high and low registers.

[[Tornberg2026-im]] offers the broadest theoretical frame, arguing that generative AI constitutes a tendential societal rupture from the "platform society" toward an "AI society," reorganizing economic (cognitive lock-in), epistemic (generative rather than predictive logics, research-as-a-service), and political (alignment politics) registers. This paper functions as something of a capstone for the cluster: it explains at a structural level why the imaginaries documented elsewhere—industry long-termism (Stanusch, Hepp), journalistic hype infrastructure (Dodds), and platform epistemic authority (Weinbrand)—cohere as symptoms of a single underlying transformation in how cognition itself is enclosed and monetized, rather than as scattered discursive phenomena.

## Synthesis

Across this body of work, a layered argument emerges about how generative AI is culturally imagined: dominant techno-capitalist and agentic framings circulate through journalism (Nguyen, Wang, Dodds, Weinbrand) and industry discourse (Stanusch, Hepp, Richter), are variably trusted by audiences depending on institutional configuration (Suk, Sbaraini-Fontes), and are theorized at a more fundamental level as both a new medium of thought (Manovich), a degraded cultural genre (Galip), and a societal rupture (Törnberg)—all building on the earlier recognition, from Bucher's algorithmic imaginary, that such imaginings are never merely descriptive but constitutively shape the technologies and institutions they purport to depict.
