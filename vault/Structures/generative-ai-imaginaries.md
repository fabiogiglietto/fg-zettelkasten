# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The Discursive Field: News Media as Imaginary-Machines

A first cluster of papers treats journalism as the primary site where publics encounter — and where sociotechnical imaginaries of — generative AI are forged. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical account, tracking mentalistic-agentic framing across 18,032 articles and finding that anthropomorphic language, rather than being a uniform symptom of hype, serves heterogeneous functions: uncritical repetition of corporate narrative, routine explanatory shorthand, and adversarial critique. Crucially, the paper locates variation in framing not in national-cultural difference but in outlet-level editorial practice, complicating any simple "culture X thinks about AI this way" narrative. [[Wang2025-zy]] pushes in the opposite comparative direction, operationalizing Cave and Dihal's utopia/dystopia taxonomy across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers to show that national political-economic conditions do systematically inflect which hopes and fears get emphasized. Read together, these two papers stage a productive tension: is the AI imaginary primarily an organizational-professional artifact (Nguyen) or a nationally-inflected ideological one (Wang)? Both agree, however, on an underlying "techno-capitalist master frame" — economic-commercial themes dominate regardless of secondary variation, and coverage remains disproportionately anchored to a single referent (ChatGPT/OpenAI), suggesting that even comparative diversity operates within a narrow discursive aperture set by industry itself.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this entire terrain by arguing that hype should not be read as discourse or narrative at all, but as *infrastructure* — a systemic force redistributing resources and legitimacy through newsroom sourcing practices, professional anxiety, and repeated reliance on tech-executive voices. This move complements Nguyen's finding that framing variation tracks editorial practice: Dodds explains *why* — journalism's community of practice absorbs AI hype structurally, well before any individual journalist's framing choices are made. The editorial's call for "breakdown and repair," rendering hype's mechanisms visible, gives this cluster a normative horizon that the more descriptive framing-analysis papers largely bracket.

## Industry Self-Narration and the Politics of Futuring

A second thread turns from press coverage to how AI industry actors construct their own imaginaries. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] examines the Sam Altman controversy as a diagnostic moment, identifying Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry-aligned imaginaries, and theorizing "premediation" and "preclusion" as twin strategies by which industry discourse displaces present harms into speculative futures while positioning itself as uniquely capable of solving them. This dovetails with [[Hepp2026-oi]]'s account of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring" — the less visible, community-level discursive work of labs, Effective Altruism networks, and Rationalist circles that supplies the ideational reservoir loud CEO pronouncements later amplify. Where Stanusch captures the imaginary at a moment of rupture, Hepp supplies its ordinary, spatially-anchored infrastructure, showing how AGI timelines, job-loss anxieties, and geopolitical "AI race" framings circulate through appropriated places (labs, start-up houses, EA community spaces) that present themselves as apolitical while doing intensely political world-making. Both papers converge on longtermism/existential-risk discourse as a mechanism for displacing present-day harms — labor, environment, bias — into speculative futures, echoing Stanusch's explicit finding that climate and labor impacts are conspicuously absent from industry discourse.

[[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes this industry dominance, showing that the pre-GenAI Twitter discourse (2012–2021) was not monolithically industry-driven: Germany institutionalized toward academic/advocacy/governmental actors while the US drifted toward individualized tech-influencer discourse. This longitudinal, cross-national finding provides useful ballast for the Stanusch/Hepp cluster — it suggests that the US imaginary's current industry- and founder-centrism (visible in the Altman-controversy and Bay Area ethnography) is not a natural or inevitable feature of AI discourse but a historically contingent trajectory diverging sharply from other national contexts, with consequences for present-day regulatory asymmetries.

[[Weinbrand2026-sf]] sits at the seam between the press-framing and industry-imaginary clusters, analyzing how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers contest the meaning of AI Overviews. Its tripartite sentiment split — Google's neutral-positive platform rhetoric, journalists' cautious criticism, marketers' conflicted commercial anxiety — mirrors Nguyen's finding of framing heterogeneity by actor/outlet, while its emphasis on absent voices (users, environmental costs, democratic implications) echoes Dodds's and Stanusch's observations about what industry-adjacent discourse systematically forecloses.

## Beyond Discourse: AI as Medium and Cultural Form

The final two papers depart from framing-analysis methodology to theorize generative AI's own aesthetic and cultural specificity, effectively asking what happens *after* the imaginaries have installed a technology as normal or inevitable. [[Manovich2026-ih]] brackets social and ethical critique altogether to ask what generative AI is *as a medium* — arguing it is the first artistic medium with built-in "media cognition," structurally conservative and anti-avant-garde, entangling style and content in ways earlier media did not. This is a deliberate counter-move against the hype/critique dyad structuring the rest of the topic: rather than asking how AI is imagined, Manovich asks what its formal properties actually afford and resist, treating the "encyclopedic" training-data conservatism he identifies as a structural analogue to the "techno-capitalist master frame" the discourse-analysis papers find in coverage — both point to a technology whose defaults quietly reproduce dominant, familiar conventions.

[[Galip2026-ix]] returns to cultural critique but inverts its object, examining not elite industry imaginaries but the debased vernacular genres — "slop" and "brainrot" — that generative AI's mass deployment has produced. Using Ngai's theory of the gimmick, the paper finds the same ambivalence structuring elite discourse (Nguyen's coexistence of hype and critique; Stanusch's premediation/preclusion) reappearing at the level of aesthetic reception: slop is simultaneously deficient and excessive, effortless and labor-intensive, meaningless and possibly resistant. Where Manovich reads AI's conservatism as a medium-specific constraint, Galip reads slop's "sad marvel" quality as symptomatic of the same contradiction under "too late capitalism" — cheap, deskilled outputs riding on immense infrastructural and environmental cost, a materialist echo of what Weinbrand and Dodds identify as the discursive erasure of AI's hidden labor and environmental footprint.

## Synthesis

Across the topic, a consistent structure emerges: dominant imaginaries (techno-capitalist inevitability, founder hagiography, longtermist risk-displacement) are actively produced and stabilized by identifiable actors — industry communities ([[Hepp2026-oi]], [[Stanusch2026-ec]]), platforms ([[Weinbrand2026-sf]]), and journalistic practice ([[Dodds2026-df]], [[Richter2026-bt]]) — while press coverage itself is more heterogeneous and contested than "hype" narratives suggest ([[Nguyen2026-vm]], [[Wang2025-zy]]). Running beneath this is a shared blind spot: labor, environmental cost, and user experience are systematically absent from elite discourse, a silence that the medium-theoretical and cultural-aesthetic papers ([[Manovich2026-ih]], [[Galip2026-ix]]) implicitly interrogate by turning attention to what generative AI actually produces — conservative, encyclopedic images; slop and brainrot — once the imaginaries have done their legitimating work.
