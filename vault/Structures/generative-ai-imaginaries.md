# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The Discursive Construction of Generative AI

A central strand of this literature treats generative AI not as a stable technical object but as something actively *imagined into being* through discourse — news coverage, industry controversy, and platform rhetoric. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] and [[Wang2025-zy]] both work within the sociotechnical imaginaries tradition, but approach it from complementary angles: Nguyen et al. perform a fine-grained content analysis of mentalistic-agentic framing across global outlets, finding that anthropomorphism is real but neither dominant nor uniform — it serves critique as often as hype, and varies more by newsroom culture than by national context. Wang et al., by contrast, operationalise Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework comparatively across the UK, US, China, and India, showing that utopian/dystopian tropes are nationally patterned, reflecting divergent political-economic stakes. Together they suggest that "AI imaginaries" are simultaneously globally recurrent (a shared vocabulary of hope and fear, a shared techno-capitalist master frame) and locally inflected by journalistic practice and geopolitics.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this entire conversation by insisting that hype is not merely a discursive pattern to be mapped but a *structuring infrastructure* — embedded in newsroom sourcing practices, professional anxiety, and resource allocation. This moves the analysis from "what frames appear" to "what institutional work frames perform," a shift that resonates with [[Stanusch2026-ec]]'s account of the Sam Altman controversy, where industry discourse on LinkedIn and X is shown to manage critique through "premediation" (deflecting concerns into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning industry as uniquely capable of resolving the problems it creates). Both papers insist that framing analysis must attend to power: who gets to narrate AI's future, and whose concerns (labour, environment, marginalized workers) are rendered invisible in the process. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] extends this logic to a concrete product controversy — Google's AI Overviews — showing how competing actors (platform, journalists, SEO marketers) stage a contest over epistemic authority and trust, with the user's voice and societal costs conspicuously absent from all three discourses, echoing Dodds et al.'s point about hype's structural blind spots.

## Where Imaginaries Are Produced: Industry, Platforms, Publics

A second cluster asks not just what is said about AI but *where* and *by whom* the imaginaries are forged. [[Hepp2026-oi]] offers the most ambitious account here, distinguishing Silicon Valley's "loud futuring" (CEOs, investors) from a "quiet futuring" occurring in labs, EA/Rationalist communities, and appropriated urban spaces — arguing that the latter supplies the ideational reservoir (especially long-termist AGI narratives) that loud futuring later amplifies. This spatial-figurational account complements [[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal social-network analysis of Twitter, which shows how the pre-GenAI formative decade (2012–2021) already diverged nationally: Germany institutionalising discourse across academia, advocacy, and government, the US consolidating around industry and then individual tech influencers — a trajectory that plausibly set the stage for the very "loud futuring" Hepp describes and for the US-centric sourcing patterns Dodds critiques.

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] pushes hardest against taking any of these discourses at face value, arguing that even critical engagement with AI's "nature," inevitability, disruption, safety, or regulation can function as a *decoy* diverting attention from the political economy of capital accumulation and network power underlying the "Project of AI." This paper serves as a methodological check on the rest of the topic: it warns that mapping imaginaries (Wang, Nguyen) or industry narrative strategies (Stanusch, Dodds) risks becoming complicit if it doesn't also trace the material infrastructures and financial networks those narratives sustain.

## Trust, Everyday Sense-Making, and the Aesthetics of "Slop"

A third thread descends from institutional and industry discourse to the level of ordinary users and conceptual theorizing about trust and cultural quality. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI built on legacies of mass-media and social-media trust research, offering a conceptual scaffold that implicitly connects to [[Bucher2017-qh]]'s earlier foundational concept of the "algorithmic imaginary" — the affectively charged, often inaccurate but socially generative ways ordinary users make sense of opaque systems. Bucher's insistence that imaginaries are productive (not mere illusions) and recursive (shaping the very systems they imagine) anticipates a cluster of recent papers on "AI slop": [[Li2026-zv]] inductively derives folk conceptualizations of slop from Reddit discourse, finding users articulate a coherent but contested category structured around constitutional defects, algorithmic overreach, resistance, and surprising enjoyment — directly extending Bucher's affect-centred methodology to the GenAI content moment. [[So2026-up]] theorizes "SLOP" at a higher level of abstraction, arguing it names not a class of objects but a *relational condition* in which evaluative signals of quality and credibility have broken down — a diagnosis that reframes platform governance's "pollution" model (also critical of detection-based solutions) as fundamentally misdiagnosing the problem. [[Galip2026-ix]] supplies the aesthetic-theoretical complement, reading slop and "brainrot" through Sianne Ngai's theory of the gimmick, as forms that work simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," exposing contradictions of cultural production under "too late capitalism" while leaving open the question of their critical potential.

## AI as Medium: A Counterpoint

Standing somewhat apart from the discourse-and-reception cluster, [[Manovich2026-ih]] brackets social and ethical critique entirely to ask a media-theoretical question: what are the distinctive formal properties of generative AI *as a medium*? His account of built-in "media cognition," structural conservatism, and the entanglement of style and content offers a counterweight to the imaginaries literature — not how AI is talked about, but what it structurally affords and resists as a creative tool. Read against Galip et al.'s gimmick-theoretic account of slop, Manovich's claim that the medium is "structurally anti-avant-garde" and biased toward familiar, academic aesthetics provides an unexpected formal explanation for *why* mass AI output so readily collapses into the derivative, homogenized texture that users (per Li and So) experience as "slop": the medium's inherent conservatism and the social condition of credibility collapse may be two descriptions of the same underlying phenomenon, approached respectively from media theory and cultural sociology.
