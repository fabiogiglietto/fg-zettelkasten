# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The Discursive Construction of Generative AI

If there is a single throughline across this set of papers, it is the insistence that generative AI does not arrive in public consciousness as a neutral technical fact — it arrives already wrapped in narrative, metaphor, and institutional interest. Several papers converge on journalism as the primary site where this wrapping happens. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] provides the most granular empirical account, tracking mentalistic-agentic framings (the tendency to describe LLMs as thinking, wanting, or believing) across 18,000+ articles and finding that such framings, while present, are neither dominant nor uniform — they vary by outlet and editorial culture rather than by region, and serve purposes ranging from uncritical amplification to pointed critique ("bullshitters," "dumb"). This complicates any simple story of media anthropomorphization; framing is shown to be a contested, multivalent practice rather than a single ideological current. [[Wang2025-zy]] complements this by scaling the analysis cross-nationally, mapping utopian/dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers using Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears vocabulary, and showing that these tensions are inflected by distinct national political economies rather than a homogeneous "global AI discourse."

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes the stakes of this cluster: AI hype in journalism, the editorial argues, is not merely a rhetorical excess to be debunked but a *systemic infrastructure* — it redistributes resources, legitimizes actors (notably tech executives over other sources), and forecloses futures by presenting AI's arrival as inevitable rather than contestable. This resonates with [[Richter2026-bt]], which asks the prior question of *who* actually populates AI discourse before GenAI's mainstream breakthrough: a longitudinal Twitter analysis of the US and Germany (2012–2021) finds diverging trajectories — German discourse institutionalizing around academia, advocacy, and government, US discourse consolidating around industry and then individual tech influencers. Read together, [[Nguyen2026-vm]], [[Wang2025-zy]], [[Dodds2026-df]], and [[Richter2026-bt]] form a coherent empirical program: they variously interrogate the *content* of AI framings, their *cross-national variation*, their *systemic function*, and their *stakeholder infrastructure* — collectively de-essentializing "AI hype" into something produced by specific actors, sourcing patterns, and editorial routines rather than a spontaneous cultural mood.

## Industry's Self-Narration

A second cluster turns from journalism-as-mediator to the AI industry's own imaginative labor. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] treats the November 2023 Sam Altman controversy as a natural experiment revealing industry-aligned imaginaries — Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, Techno-Hagiography — and shows how these operate through "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning industry as the only legitimate steward of AI's risks). Crucially, the paper insists that counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter (Governing Urgency, Intersectionality) demonstrate that this monopolization of AI's future is incomplete, not hegemonic. [[Hepp2026-oi]] extends this inquiry ethnographically and geographically, distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs and investors (the register Stanusch's imaginaries largely belong to) from a "quiet futuring" diffused across Bay Area labs, community houses, and pioneer networks — especially Effective Altruism and Rationalism — that supplies the ideational reservoir loud futuring later amplifies. Both papers share a commitment to treating industry discourse not as transparent belief but as strategic, spatially and socially anchored world-making; together they suggest that the imaginaries surfacing in Altman-controversy discourse are not free-floating but trace back to identifiable communities, places, and historical currents (EA/Rationalism, long-termism) that Hepp locates concretely in San Francisco's social geography.

## Platform-Specific and Product-Level Framing

[[Weinbrand2026-sf]] sits usefully between the journalism cluster and the industry cluster, examining how a specific product — Google's AI Overviews — becomes a site of contested meaning-making among Google itself, technology journalists, and SEO marketers. Its finding that Google frames AIOs as seamless innovation while hedging accountability through "experimental" labels, while journalists foreground hallucination and epistemic risk and marketers oscillate between engagement and anxiety, exemplifies at product scale the same struggle over legitimacy and authority that [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Stanusch2026-ec]] describe at the level of industry-wide narrative. Notably, Weinbrand's observation that user perspectives and environmental costs are structurally absent from all three actor discourses echoes Dodds's point about hype's tendency to render hidden labor and infrastructure invisible.

## The View From the Audience

Two papers turn the lens around, asking how these circulating imaginaries land with publics. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] surveys Italian attitudes across two years, finding not technophobia but "critical ambivalence" — rising perception of AI as threat alongside majority support for regulated rather than banned generative text tools, and a persistent gap between exposure to AI-related media and actual understanding. This gap is telling alongside the journalism cluster: if outlets like those studied in [[Nguyen2026-vm]] and [[Wang2025-zy]] are the dominant information channels (as Fattorini's data on TV/radio and social media confirms), then variation in editorial framing has direct, if imperfectly mediated, consequences for public epistemology. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] narrows the aperture further, testing experimentally how audiences respond to disclosed AI authorship in news itself — finding no baseline quality discrimination but a short-term "curiosity bump" in engagement upon disclosure that does not translate into durable acceptance. Read against Dodds's claim that journalists are simultaneously "hype makers" and "hype watchers," Gilardi's finding suggests audiences may share this ambivalent dual stance: intrigued by AI-generated content but not persuaded of its legitimacy.

## Theorizing the Medium Itself

The collection closes with two papers that step outside empirical discourse analysis to ask what generative AI *is* as an object of theory, in each case foregrounding agency and cognition rather than narrative. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets the social and ethical critiques that animate most of the other papers in this topic, instead offering a media-theoretical account of generative AI as a medium with built-in cognitive capacity — the "first artistic medium that itself thinks" — structurally conservative, entangling style and content, and resistant to the kind of avant-garde defamiliarization prior media permitted. This is a productive counterpoint to the discourse-analytic papers: where Nguyen or Wang ask how AI's *agentic character* is rhetorically constructed by journalists, Manovich asks what a genuinely cognitive medium *does* formally and aesthetically, independent of how it is talked about. [[Ng2026-og]] pushes this concern with AI's agentic and social character into a formal register, arguing that agentic AI operating in social environments requires social theory — not single-task alignment paradigms — as a structural prior, formalized through a Multi-Agent Social Systems framework and validated against a large AI social-network dataset. Positioned alongside Manovich, Ng's paper suggests a shared intuition running beneath the whole topic: that generative AI's cultural imaginaries (mentalistic framings, hype narratives, industry mythologies) are not merely projections onto an inert tool but responses — however distorted or strategically deployed — to real structural properties of AI as a cognitive, relational, and increasingly social-agentic medium. The topic's arc, then, moves from how AI is talked about (journalism, industry, platforms), to how those framings are received (publics), and finally to what, medium-theoretically and structurally, might warrant talking about it that way at all.
