# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## Framings of Generative AI: Media, Industry, and Medium

### The problem of imagination itself

The papers gathered here share a starting premise: generative AI does not enter public life as a neutral technology but arrives pre-narrated, wrapped in expectations, metaphors, and anticipatory promises that shape how it is built, regulated, and used before its capacities are even settled. The organizing concept for most of the empirical work is the "sociotechnical imaginary" (Jasanoff and Kim), operationalized differently across sites — newsrooms, industry controversies, Silicon Valley community life, and audience surveys. [[Wang2025-zy]] and [[Nguyen2026-vm]] both take up this concept explicitly to study press coverage, while [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]] extend it into industry discourse and community ethnography. Together they suggest that "the AI imaginary" is not singular: it fractures along national, institutional, and even individual-editorial lines, and its most powerful iterations are less about accurate representation than about who gets to occupy — and monopolize — the future.

### News media as a site of contested framing

Several papers converge on journalism as a key arena where generative AI's meaning is negotiated. [[Nguyen2026-vm]]'s large-scale content analysis of AI news across outlets and continents complicates a common assumption — that news coverage indiscriminately anthropomorphizes AI — by showing that mentalistic-agentic framing is real but minority discourse, driven more by outlet-level editorial practice than national culture, and often deployed critically rather than credulously. This finding sits productively against [[Wang2025-zy]], which maps utopian/dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers using Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework, finding that national political-economic context still shapes which hopes and which fears get emphasized. Read together, these two papers suggest a layered causality: broad techno-capitalist master frames (economic utility, geopolitical competition) are globally shared, but their secondary inflections — mentalistic language, utopian/dystopian valence — are locally and organizationally mediated rather than simply "cultural."

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this entire terrain by arguing that "hype" itself should not be treated as mere discourse or bubble but as an infrastructural force — one that redistributes resources, legitimizes actors, and structures journalistic practice through sourcing routines that privilege tech executives. This systemic view helps explain a pattern visible across the empirical papers: the near-total concentration of LLM coverage on ChatGPT/OpenAI ([[Nguyen2026-vm]]), and the parallel dominance of industry voices in shaping what counts as newsworthy. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] narrows this lens to a single case — Google's AI Overviews — showing how three actor groups (Google, journalists, SEO marketers) construct competing frames of trust, authority, and platform power, with journalists occupying the "hype watcher" role Dodds identifies, and with the user's own voice conspicuously absent from all three discourses. That absence of ordinary users and of environmental/societal costs recurs as a structural silence across this whole cluster of media-framing papers.

### Industry discourse and the geography of Silicon Valley imagination

A second strand shifts from journalism to the industry's own self-narration. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] uses the November 2023 Sam Altman controversy as a natural experiment in industry imaginaries, identifying Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant frames, and theorizing "premediation" and "preclusion" as twin strategies by which industry actors displace present accountability onto speculative futures while simultaneously positioning themselves as uniquely qualified to manage those futures. This dovetails with [[Hepp2026-oi]]'s ethnographic account of Bay Area "quiet futuring," which locates the intellectual reservoir behind such industry rhetoric — especially Effective Altruism and Rationalist communities — in specific appropriated places and social figurations, arguing that the loud, CEO-driven imaginaries analyzed by Stanusch draw on and amplify ideas incubated in quieter, ostensibly apolitical community spaces. Both papers resist treating "the AI industry" as monolithic: Stanusch finds counter-imaginaries (Governing Urgency, Intersectionality) contesting industry framing on X/Twitter, while Hepp stresses the landscape's ideological fragmentation and contradiction even within Silicon Valley itself.

[[Richter2026-bt]] provides a longitudinal, comparative backdrop to these industry-discourse studies, tracing who actually drove AI discourse on Twitter in the US and Germany from 2012–2021 — a formative decade before the GenAI boom analyzed by Stanusch and Hepp. Its finding that Germany institutionalized (academic, advocacy, governmental voices) while the US individualized toward tech-influencer dominance offers a structural explanation for why the "loud futuring" Hepp describes, and the industry-driven controversy management Stanusch documents, are so distinctly American phenomena — and why European coverage in [[Wang2025-zy]] and [[Nguyen2026-vm]] tilts more toward risk/ethics framing.

### Platforms, infrastructure, and the material substrate of imaginaries

[[Helmond2026-ll]] operates at a different register, tracking how the *material and business-model* infrastructure underlying these imaginaries is itself shifting: platformization is entering an AI-centered phase in which behavioral-signal capture gives way to AI-driven inference, and Big Tech repositions toward "Big AI" ecosystems involving new sectors (space, energy, defense) and new actors (Nvidia, Anduril, Palantir). This is a useful counterweight to the discourse-focused papers: it reminds us that the imaginaries chronicled by Stanusch, Hepp, and the news-framing studies are not free-floating narratives but are anchored in — and legitimize — concrete infrastructural and economic repositioning.

### Publics: trust, ambivalence, and the limits of imaginary-talk

A distinct cluster turns from elite/discourse production to public reception. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] characterizes Italian public attitudes as "critical ambivalence," finding a persistent exposure–understanding gap and strongly context-dependent acceptance (high for security, low for journalism and art) — a finding that complicates any simple story of AI hype "trickling down" uncritically from industry and media into public consciousness. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] sharpens this by showing that trust is not undifferentiated even toward AI itself: Italian audiences trust AI-mediated search more than AI-produced news, suggesting that the imaginary of "search" versus "journalism" carries distinct authority structures — a finding that resonates with [[Weinbrand2026-sf]]'s account of Google positioning AI Overviews as quasi-infrastructural rather than merely journalistic. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers the connective theoretical scaffolding for this empirical work, proposing that trust in generative AI must be studied multilevel (individual, institutional, societal) by building on — but not simply importing — the mass-media and social-media trust literatures, implicitly bridging the discourse-analytic papers above with the audience-survey papers.

### AI as medium and as degraded genre: theorizing the artifact itself

The final pair of papers steps back from discourse-about-AI to theorize AI's outputs and affordances directly, offering a media-theoretical counterpoint to the framing-analytic majority. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets ethical and social critique to ask what kind of *medium* generative AI is, arguing it is the first artistic medium with built-in cognition — an "encyclopedic" media cognition of art history that makes it simultaneously unprecedentedly flexible and structurally conservative, biased toward familiar academic-photographic convention and resistant to genuine defamiliarization. This formalist optimism about the medium's distinctive properties stands in productive tension with [[Galip2026-ix]], which theorizes the degraded end of the same output space — "slop" and "brainrot" — through Ngai's theory of the gimmick, arguing that these forms work simultaneously "too little" (deskilled, labour-saving) and "too hard" (straining for algorithmic attention), and that their apparent worthlessness may nonetheless harbor a "soft resistance" to platform hygiene. Read against Manovich, Galip effectively supplies the aesthetic and affective underside of "media cognition": where Manovich finds structural conservatism as a formal property, Galip finds the same conservatism experienced culturally as glut, deskilling, and disgust — two theorizations of the same medium's tendency toward the familiar, one from the vantage of the serious artist-theorist, the other from the vantage of the endlessly scrolling, ambivalent consumer that Fattorini's survey respondents might recognize themselves in.
