# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## Media Framing and the Journalistic Production of AI Imaginaries

A first cluster of papers treats news media as a primary site where generative AI's cultural meaning is negotiated. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical account, tracking mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs across a large multinational corpus and finding that anthropomorphic language is neither uniform nor straightforwardly ideological—it serves uncritical promotion, routine explanation, and adversarial critique in roughly equal measure, and its distribution tracks outlet and journalistic practice more than national culture. This complicates the tidier comparative claims of [[Wang2025-zy]], which maps utopian and dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian coverage using Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework; where Wang treats national press systems as coherent containers for distinct imaginaries, Nguyen's finer-grained coding suggests that editorial and individual-journalist variation may cut across those national boundaries. Both papers nonetheless converge on the core claim that journalism does not merely report AI but co-produces its sociotechnical imaginary (Jasanoff), and both find a persistent techno-capitalist master frame—ChatGPT/OpenAI dominance, economic-commercial emphasis—beneath surface-level national or outlet differences.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this whole line of inquiry by insisting that "AI hype" is not simply a discursive pattern to be content-analyzed but a systemic infrastructure: a set of professional practices, sourcing routines, and resource-allocation mechanisms embedded in journalism's community of practice. This is a productive friction with Nguyen and Wang's more frame-analytic approach—Dodds suggests that even careful mapping of frames and tropes risks missing how hype operates *materially*, through sourcing patterns that privilege tech executives and self-proclaimed experts, through labor made invisible (data annotators, content moderators), and through journalists' contradictory position as both hype-makers and hype-watchers. Read together, these three papers trace an arc from "what stories does the press tell about AI" toward "what does storytelling about AI *do*"—a shift from representation to infrastructure that reappears, in a different register, in the industry-discourse papers below.

## Industry and Community Imaginaries: From Boardrooms to Bay Area Backchannels

A second group turns from journalism to the discourse of AI-producing communities themselves. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] uses the November 2023 Altman controversy as a diagnostic event, showing how industry-aligned discourse on LinkedIn and X coheres around Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography, and theorizing two rhetorical strategies—premediation (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and preclusion (absorbing critique by claiming unique competence to resolve it)—through which industry monopolizes the framing of AI's future while sidelining present-day harms (climate, labor). [[Hepp2026-oi]] extends this inquiry spatially and ethnographically, arguing that the "loud futuring" of CEOs analyzed by Stanusch is only half the picture: a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and Effective Altruist/Rationalist networks supplies the ideational reservoir—AGI arrival, job loss, geopolitical competition—that loud futuring later condenses and amplifies. Hepp's finding that EA/Rationalist affiliation remains deeply entangled with OpenAI and Anthropic staff gives empirical flesh to the abstract "Longtermism" imaginary that Stanusch identifies discursively; the two papers effectively describe the same ideological formation from complementary vantage points (controversy-mapping versus locational ethnography).

[[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes this industry dominance, showing that the US Twitter discourse's tilt toward tech-influencer individualism, and Germany's contrasting institutionalization toward academia and advocacy, were established well before the GenAI boom (2012–2021)—suggesting that the imaginaries Stanusch and Hepp document in 2023–2026 are not novel eruptions but the maturation of decade-long, nationally divergent stakeholder trajectories. This comparative-institutional lens usefully checks any temptation to treat "Silicon Valley discourse" as monolithic or ahistorical. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] narrows the aperture further to a single artifact—Google's AI Overviews—demonstrating how a platform actor (Google), a critical intermediary (tech journalists), and an anxious commercial constituency (SEO marketers) contest the meaning of a specific generative-AI feature, with the user's voice and societal/environmental stakes conspicuously absent from all three discourses. This absence echoes Dodds' point about hype rendering hidden costs invisible, and it shows the industry/journalism imaginaries described above operating concretely around one product rather than in the aggregate.

## Theorizing the Medium: Media Cognition, Agency, and Aesthetic Ambivalence

A third, more conceptual strand asks not how AI is talked about but what kind of thing it *is*, as medium or as agent. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets the ethical-social critiques that animate the journalism and industry-discourse papers above, offering instead a media-theoretical account of generative AI's structural properties: its built-in "media cognition," its probabilistic trade-off between variability and control, its aesthetic conservatism, and its entanglement of style and content. This is a significant methodological counterpoint to the discourse-analytic papers—Manovich argues that understanding AI's cultural position requires attention to what the medium formally affords and resists, independent of the narratives (utopian, dystopian, hype-laden) that surround it. [[Anicker2024-vp]] approaches a cognate question—can AI be an agent?—from social theory rather than media theory, but arrives at a structurally similar move: agency, like Manovich's medium-specificity, is not read off the technology's intrinsic capacities but is constituted relationally, in Anicker's case as a socially granted and monitored license to act. Both papers thus push back against a purely discursive reading of AI's cultural meaning, insisting that either formal-medial properties or socially instituted status must be reckoned with alongside (or prior to) the imaginaries that news media, industry actors, and communities project onto the technology.

[[Galip2026-ix]] occupies an interesting middle position between the discourse-analytic and the theoretical registers: it takes the vernacular categories "slop" and "brainrot"—themselves a kind of folk framing of generative AI's cultural output—and subjects them to theoretical elaboration via Ngai's gimmick, arguing that these stigmatized genres work simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," embodying the contradictions of cultural production under "too late capitalism." Where Manovich examines AI's high-cultural affordances as a medium for art, Galip examines its low/degraded output as an affective and economic phenomenon, and notably finds ambivalent critical potential where dominant discourse sees only waste—an argument that resonates with Nguyen's finding that anthropomorphic or dismissive language ("dumb," "bullshitter") can itself be a form of critique rather than mere hype.

## Trust as a Cross-Cutting Frame

[[Suk2026-ai]] sits somewhat apart as a theoretical bridge, proposing a multilevel (individual/institutional/societal) framework for public trust in generative AI drawn from media-trust scholarship. Its relevance to this cluster lies in naming what much of the above literature examines implicitly: framings, imaginaries, and medium-theoretical accounts all ultimately feed into—and are shaped by—the question of whether and how publics come to trust (or distrust) generative AI as an information intermediary. Suk's call for institutional- and societal-level analysis complements Dodds' infrastructural account of hype and Richter's stakeholder-trajectory analysis, suggesting a natural next step for this line of inquiry: connecting the discursive mapping of imaginaries to their downstream effects on public trust and legitimacy.
