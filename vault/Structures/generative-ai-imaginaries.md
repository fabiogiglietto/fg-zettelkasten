# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The Discursive Construction of Generative AI

Taken together, these twelve papers map a field in which generative AI is not a stable technical object but a contested cultural artifact, continuously reassembled through journalism, industry self-narration, public opinion, and media theory. What unites them is a shared premise, traceable to the sociotechnical imaginaries literature (Jasanoff and Kim), that how AI is *talked about* is constitutive of what AI *becomes* — shaping funding, regulation, labor, and lay understanding alike. The papers divide roughly into three registers: news-media framing studies, industry/community discourse studies, and theoretical interventions on AI as medium or cultural form, with a smaller cluster examining how audiences receive and trust these mediated constructions.

### News media as a site of epistemic struggle

Several papers treat journalism as the primary arena where generative AI's public meaning is negotiated. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular account, showing across 18,000+ articles that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs is real but far from dominant, and that its variation tracks editorial practice more than national culture — a finding that complicates simpler claims of cultural divergence. [[Wang2025-zy]] takes the comparative-national approach more directly, applying Cave and Dihal's hopes/fears framework across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers to show that utopian/dystopian tropes are inflected by distinct political-economic stakes in each country. [[Dodds2026-df]], as an editorial synthesizing a special issue, reframes the whole enterprise: AI hype is not merely discourse to be debunked but a *structuring infrastructure* within journalism's community of practice, visible in sourcing patterns (tech executives over civil society), geopolitical inflections (US inevitability vs. German "European values" vs. Chinese loyal-facilitation), and the systematic invisibilization of labor and environmental costs. Read together, these three suggest a layered picture: beneath surface-level anthropomorphic metaphors ([[Nguyen2026-vm]]) and national narrative variation ([[Wang2025-zy]]) lies a deeper professional-economic logic of hype production ([[Dodds2026-df]]) that journalism both enacts and polices.

This news-focused cluster extends into more specialized sites. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] narrows the lens to Google's AI Overviews, showing how platform, press, and marketer discourses compete to define a single product launch — Google performing seamless innovation while journalists invoke hallucination and disaster, in a contest over epistemic authority that echoes the "Googlization of knowledge." [[Richter2026-bt]] pushes the news-discourse question back in time and onto a different platform, tracing Twitter stakeholder composition in the US and Germany from 2012–2021; its finding that German discourse institutionalized while US discourse individualized around tech influencers supplies a structural precondition for the hype dynamics [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Nguyen2026-vm]] describe for the post-ChatGPT period.

### Industry and community self-narration

A second cluster turns from journalism to the discourse communities that produce AI's industrial imaginaries. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] treats the November 2023 Sam Altman controversy as a diagnostic event, identifying Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry-aligned imaginaries sustained through strategies of "premediation" and "preclusion" that defer present accountability into speculative futures — while noting that counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter show this monopolization is incomplete. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this with an ethnographic, place-based account of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring": beneath the loud futuring of CEOs lies a landscape of labs, communities, and pioneer groups (Effective Altruism, Rationalism) whose AGI, job-loss, and geopolitics discourses supply the raw material that loud futuring amplifies. Both papers resist treating industry discourse as monolithic ideology, instead showing imaginaries as plural, contested, and strategically mobilized — Stanusch via controversy mapping, Hepp via spatial and figurational grounding.

### Theorizing AI as medium and cultural form

A third strand steps back from empirical discourse analysis to theorize generative AI's distinctive properties. [[Manovich2026-ih]] offers the most sustained media-theoretical account, bracketing ethical critique to ask what kind of medium generative AI *is* — arguing it is the first artistic medium with built-in cognitive capacity, aesthetically conservative and encyclopedic at once, with style and content structurally entangled. [[Galip2026-ix]] inverts this gesture, theorizing not the medium's high-art affordances but its degraded, stigmatized outputs — "slop" and "brainrot" — via Ngai's theory of the gimmick, finding in these ostensibly meaningless forms a site where the contradictions of "too late capitalism" become visible. Together, Manovich and Galip bookend the medium's register from connoisseurship to detritus, both insisting that generative AI demands medium-specific theoretical vocabulary rather than being absorbed into generic AI-ethics discourse.

### Publics, trust, and reception

A final set of papers turns to how these imaginaries land with audiences. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] characterizes Italian public attitudes as "critical ambivalence," showing a persistent gap between exposure and understanding and strongly context-dependent acceptance — a finding that resonates with [[Nguyen2026-vm]]'s point that symbolic/anthropomorphic associations (humanoid robots, chatbots) coexist with technical skepticism. [[Suk2026-ai]] and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extend this into the specific question of trust: Suk proposes a multilevel framework borrowing from mass- and social-media trust research, while Sbaraini-Fontes empirically shows Italian audiences trust AI-mediated search more than AI-produced news — a striking asymmetry that dovetails with [[Weinbrand2026-sf]]'s account of Google's AI Overviews as a contested redefinition of search authority. Across this reception-oriented cluster, trust and ambivalence emerge not as residues of insufficient information but as rational responses to the very discursive instability the other papers document — audiences triangulating credibility across platforms, genres, and configurations of AI use, in a landscape where no single imaginary has yet secured dominance.
