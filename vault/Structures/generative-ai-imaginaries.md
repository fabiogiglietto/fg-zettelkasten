# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## The Discursive Construction of Generative AI

If generative AI is, as several papers in this collection insist, a technology whose meaning is still radically underdetermined, then the work of imagination — who gets to narrate its future, in what genre, and to what end — becomes a primary site of power. The papers filed here trace this work across three registers: journalistic framing, industry/community self-narration, and media-theoretical accounts of the technology's own formal properties. Read together, they suggest that "generative AI" is less a stable object than a contested surface onto which competing sociotechnical imaginaries are projected.

### News Media as Co-Producers of AI Epistemology

A cluster of papers treats journalism not as a neutral conduit but as an active site of imaginary-production. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical account, showing that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs — attributing minds, intentions, or feelings to chatbots — are real but far from dominant, and that their deployment is driven more by outlet-level editorial culture than by national or regional difference; crucially, anthropomorphism can serve critique as easily as hype. This complicates any simple "media hypes AI" narrative, a nuance echoed in [[Wang2025-zy]], which maps the utopia/dystopia axis of AI coverage across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers using Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework, finding that national political-economic context systematically shapes which hopes and which fears get airtime. [[Dodds2026-df]] pushes the analytical frame further, reconceiving "AI hype" not as discourse or bubble but as *infrastructure* — a systemic mechanism that redistributes resources and legitimacy, sustained by journalism's own community-of-practice anxieties, its reliance on tech-executive sourcing, and its oscillation between hype-making and hype-watching. Together these three papers move from measuring framing (Nguyen, Wang) to theorizing why framing has structural consequences (Dodds), and they converge on a shared caution: neither uncritical technological determinism nor a flat "it's all hype" dismissal captures how news media actually process generative AI.

### Industry and Community Self-Narration

A second cluster turns from journalism to the imaginaries generated *within* the AI industry and its adjacent communities. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] uses the November 2023 Sam Altman controversy as a diagnostic event, showing how industry discourse manages critique through "premediation" (deferring concerns to speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning industry itself as the only legitimate problem-solver) — strategies that closely parallel Dodds's account of temporal-inevitability narratives in journalism, but staged from the inside. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this by distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs (the register Stanusch analyzes) from a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, communities, and pioneer groups — Effective Altruism and Rationalism especially — that supplies the ideational reservoir loud futuring later amplifies, while presenting itself as apolitical. [[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes this landscape, showing via longitudinal Twitter data that the US discourse's drift toward industry- and influencer-dominated individualization (the very infrastructure Hepp and Stanusch describe) was not inevitable but diverged sharply from Germany's institutionalizing, multi-stakeholder trajectory — a finding that grounds claims about "Silicon Valley imaginaries" in a comparative, path-dependent history rather than treating US discourse patterns as generic to AI discourse as such.

### Platforms, Products, and the Politics of Infrastructure

Two papers examine how these imaginaries crystallize around specific products. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] analyzes Google's AI Overviews as a site where Google, journalists, and SEO marketers contest whether generative search represents seamless innovation or epistemic risk — reprising, at product scale, the utopia/dystopia and inevitability-versus-critique dynamics found in Wang and Dodds, while adding platform-power theory to argue that Google repositions itself as quasi-infrastructural epistemic authority. [[Triedman2025-uy]] provides an empirical case study in how AI-generated knowledge platforms materially instantiate a particular imaginary: Grokipedia's derivative-yet-ideologically-skewed relationship to Wikipedia shows what happens when the "loud futuring" and libertarian-inflected imaginaries documented by Hepp and Stanusch are operationalized into an actual knowledge infrastructure, with measurable consequences for source reliability and political framing.

### Beyond Discourse: AI as Medium

The final two papers step back from framing-as-discourse to ask what generative AI *is* as a cultural form or medium, offering theoretical counterweight to the empirical/discursive papers above. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets ethical and social critique to ask what generative AI affords and resists as an artistic medium, identifying built-in "media cognition," structural conservatism, and style-content entanglement as properties intrinsic to the technology rather than artifacts of its discursive reception — a move that implicitly challenges the entire framing-studies apparatus by insisting some of AI's cultural effects are medium-specific, not narrative-dependent. [[Galip2026-ix]] occupies a related but inverted position: rather than analyzing prestige uses of generative AI, it theorizes the degraded, stigmatized end of generative culture — "slop" and "brainrot" — via Ngai's gimmick theory, finding these forms simultaneously deficient and excessive, and asking whether such devalued genres might harbor unexpected critical potential. Read against Manovich, Galip suggests that the medium-specific properties Manovich identifies (probabilistic generation, conservatism, encyclopedic pastiche) are precisely what curdle into slop when generative AI is deployed at platform scale for attention capture rather than authorial intention.

### An Emerging Arc

Across the set, a rough arc emerges: from mapping how mainstream media frame AI (Nguyen, Wang, Dodds), to how industry and community actors self-narrate and manage critique (Stanusch, Hepp, Richter), to how these imaginaries get materialized in actual platforms and products (Weinbrand, Triedman), to a theoretical reckoning with what the medium itself does and produces, at both its most celebrated (Manovich) and most degraded (Galip) registers. The through-line is a shared refusal of technological determinism: whether in a newsroom, a LinkedIn thread, a search results page, or an AI-generated image, generative AI's meaning is continually being made, contested, and remade — and these papers collectively insist that studying *how* it is imagined is inseparable from understanding what it is.
