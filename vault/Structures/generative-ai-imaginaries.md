# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## Media, News, and the Production of AI Imaginaries

A cluster of these papers treats journalism as a key site where generative AI's public meaning is negotiated rather than simply reported. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical account: across 18,032 articles, mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs exist but are far from dominant, and where they appear they serve heterogeneous functions—corporate boosterism, routine explanatory shorthand, or adversarial critique (calling systems "bullshitters"). Crucially, variation tracks outlet and individual journalistic practice more than national or regional culture, a finding that complicates any simple story of "Western hype vs. Asian pragmatism." [[Wang2025-zy]] approaches the same terrain from the opposite methodological pole, using Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears taxonomy to quantitatively compare utopian/dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers, arguing that these sociotechnical imaginaries are systematically shaped by each country's political and economic stakes. Read together, Nguyen and Wang suggest a layered picture: a dominant techno-capitalist master frame (per Nguyen's finding that economic-commercial themes dominate globally) overlaid with nationally inflected secondary emphases on risk, ethics, or utility—exactly the sociopolitical variation Wang's comparative framework is built to capture.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this whole discussion by insisting hype is not merely a discursive or narrative phenomenon but a "systemic, structuring force" embedded in journalism's communities of practice—sourcing routines, managerial anxiety, professional incentives—that actively redistributes legitimacy and resources. This moves the conversation from *what frames appear in text* (Nguyen, Wang) to *why those frames are structurally produced and sustained*, particularly through repetitive reliance on tech-executive sources and temporal narratives of inevitability. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] provides a focused case study that operationalizes exactly this structural-discursive interplay: tracking how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers contest the meaning of AI Overviews, it shows industry framing hedging accountability through "experimental" labels while journalists supply the critical counter-discourse Dodds identifies as journalism's "hype watcher" function—though notably, Weinbrand finds the user's voice and broader societal/environmental stakes are structurally absent from all three actors' discourse, an invisibility Dodds theorizes at a more general level (hidden labor, environmental costs).

## Industry Self-Narration and the Geography of Imaginaries

Where the news-media cluster examines AI imaginaries as mediated *to* publics, [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]] turn to how the industry imagines itself. Stanusch's controversy-mapping of the Sam Altman firing identifies Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry imaginaries, deployed through strategies of "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning industry as the only legitimate problem-solver)—mechanisms that resonate strongly with Dodds' account of hype as inevitability-narrative, but specify the actors and rhetorical techniques involved. Hepp's ethnography of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring" supplies the missing social infrastructure behind these imaginaries: it locates Longtermism's roots in Effective Altruism/Rationalist communities physically anchored in San Francisco spaces, arguing that the "loud futuring" of CEOs (which Stanusch's controversy analysis captures) draws on a reservoir of ideas incubated in quieter, ostensibly apolitical community discourse. Together these two papers suggest AI imaginaries are not free-floating narratives but are produced in specific social figurations and geographic sites, then selectively amplified during public controversies.

[[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes this industry dominance, showing that the pre-2022 Twitter discourse was not always industry-led—Germany institutionalized toward academic/advocacy/governmental actors while the US drifted toward industry and, increasingly, individual tech-influencer voices. This longitudinal, comparative finding helps explain why Stanusch and Hepp find such a concentrated, insider-driven imaginary ecosystem in the US specifically: the infrastructure for individualized, influencer-centric AI discourse was already consolidating a decade before ChatGPT.

## Publics, Reception, and Ambivalence

[[Fattorini2026-bo]] shifts from elite production of imaginaries to lay reception, showing that Italian publics hold a "critical ambivalence" toward AI—rising tool use alongside rising perception of AI as a societal threat, persistent reliance on symbolic tropes like humanoid robots, and strongly context-dependent acceptance (welcomed in security, rejected in journalism and art). This grounds the more elite-focused discourse analyses above in actual public uptake, suggesting that the mentalistic framings Nguyen finds in news coverage and the utopian/dystopian tropes Wang maps are not merely rhetorical artifacts but correlate with measurable public anxieties and symbolic associations.

## Theorizing the Medium Itself

The final two papers step back from discourse analysis to ask what kind of medium generative AI *is*, offering conceptual counterpoints to the hype-and-framing literature. [[Manovich2026-ih]] brackets ethical and social critique entirely, proposing instead a media-theoretical account of generative AI's distinctive affordances: probabilistic variability-versus-control trade-offs, structural conservatism, built-in "media cognition," and the entanglement of style and content. This is a deliberate counter-move to the hype/imaginary literature—where Dodds, Stanusch, and Hepp ask how AI is narrated and by whom, Manovich asks what the medium structurally permits and resists, treating AI as continuous with photography and painting rather than as a uniquely agentic or threatening actor.

[[Galip2026-ix]] occupies an instructive middle position, taking seriously both the aesthetic/medium-specific dimension (treating "slop" and "brainrot" as a genre) and the cultural-affective dimension (as modes of feeling under "too late capitalism"). Using Ngai's theory of the gimmick, it reads generative AI outputs as simultaneously working "too little" (deskilling, labor-saving) and "too hard" (straining for attention)—a formulation that echoes Dodds' and Stanusch's concern with hidden labor and inevitability narratives, while also gesturing toward Manovich's medium-specific vocabulary (recursive degradation, "Model Autophagy Disorder" as a structural property of the medium rather than merely a discursive failure). In this sense Galip's piece functions as a hinge between the discourse-analytic papers above, which track how AI is talked about, and Manovich's insistence that AI also needs to be understood on its own medium-theoretical terms.
