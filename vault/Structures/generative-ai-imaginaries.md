# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## From News Discourse to Sociotechnical Imaginaries

Several papers here share a core empirical object—journalistic and public discourse about generative AI—but arrive at it from different methodological vantage points and draw different conclusions about how homogeneous that discourse actually is. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular corrective to intuitive assumptions about AI hype: across 18,032 articles, mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs is present but far from dominant (8.8% of LLM-centric coverage), and variation tracks outlet and journalistic practice more than region or culture. This pushes against a flattened "AI is anthropomorphized by the media" narrative, insisting instead that anthropomorphic language does multiple rhetorical jobs—corporate ventriloquism, routine explanation, and critique alike. [[Wang2025-zy]] complements this by zooming out to the level of national imaginaries, using Cave and Dihal's hopes/fears framework to quantitatively compare UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspaper coverage, arguing that utopian/dystopian tropes are patterned by each country's distinct political-economic stakes. Where Nguyen et al. find intra-national variation overriding regional difference, Wang et al. find cross-national difference to be analytically productive—together the two papers suggest sociotechnical imaginaries operate at multiple, sometimes contradictory scales simultaneously, and that aggregate-level comparisons and fine-grained framing analyses are not reducible to one another.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes the stakes of this entire discursive terrain: AI hype is not just a cultural narrative to be measured and mapped but a "systemic, structuring force" embedded in journalism's professional infrastructure—its sourcing routines, funding logics, and community practices. This editorial functions as a methodological rebuke to purely content-analytic approaches, insisting that hype be traced through institutional mechanisms (who gets quoted, which actors are rendered invisible) rather than treated as free-floating discourse. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] operationalizes exactly this kind of institutionally-situated discourse analysis at a narrower scale, tracking how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers discursively contest the meaning of AI Overviews—showing Google's self-presentation as a "seamless," almost infrastructural public good standing in tension with journalists' hallucination-focused skepticism and marketers' commercial anxiety. Notably, like the national-newspaper studies, this analysis finds the user's own voice structurally absent from the discourse, a silence that recurs across several papers in this cluster as itself a target of critique.

## Industry Self-Narration and the Geography of Hype

A second thread turns from media coverage *of* AI to the discourse produced *by* the AI industry and its surrounding communities. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] uses the November 2023 Altman controversy as a diagnostic event, identifying Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry-aligned imaginaries, and theorizing "premediation" and "preclusion" as strategies by which industry actors absorb critique into speculative futures or position themselves as uniquely qualified stewards of present risk. [[Hepp2026-oi]] extends this analysis geographically and ethnographically, distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs (the register Stanusch et al. largely examine) from a "quiet futuring" diffused through Bay Area labs, co-living houses, and community infrastructures—most consequentially Effective Altruism and Rationalist milieus that supply the long-termist, AGI-timeline vocabulary later amplified by loud futuring. Read together, these two papers suggest a kind of discursive division of labor: quiet futuring produces raw ideational material (existential risk, AGI inevitability) that loud futuring then condenses into punchy, media-legible claims—exactly the kind of claims [[Stanusch2026-ec]]'s "Longtermism" imaginary mobilizes during moments of crisis.

[[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal Twitter analysis historicizes this industry dominance rather than assuming it. By tracing US and German AI discourse from 2012–2021, the paper shows that industry capture of the conversation (reaching ~70% of top US accounts, increasingly individualized into tech-influencer voices) was neither inevitable nor uniform—Germany's parallel discourse institutionalized toward academic, advocacy, and governmental actors. This offers a crucial diachronic complement to the largely synchronic snapshots elsewhere in this topic: the imaginaries mapped by Stanusch, Hepp, and the newspaper studies did not emerge from nowhere but were seeded by a decade of asymmetric stakeholder access that plausibly explains later divergences in regulation and public framing (e.g., the EU/US contrast implicit in [[Wang2025-zy]] and [[Dodds2026-df]]).

## Theorizing AI as Medium: Cognition, Degeneration, Enclosure

A final cluster steps back from discourse analysis proper toward media-theoretical and critical-theoretical accounts of what generative AI *is* as a medium or sociotechnical formation, rather than how it is talked about. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets ethical and social critique to ask what structural properties define generative AI as an artistic medium—its built-in "media cognition," its probabilistic trade-off between variability and control, and its aesthetic conservatism. This is a notable methodological outlier within the topic: where every other paper treats framing and imaginary as the object of study, Manovich treats imaginaries as something to set aside in favor of formal/medium-specific analysis, implicitly wagering that the medium's own affordances (not just discourse about it) shape culture.

[[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Tornberg2026-im]] push in the opposite direction, toward explicitly critical theorizations that refuse the industry's self-presentation examined in the Stanusch/Hepp/Weinbrand cluster. Galip and coauthors mobilize Ngai's theory of the gimmick to read "slop" and "brainrot" as aesthetic forms that are simultaneously deskilled and overworked, deficient and excessive—arguing these stigmatized genres expose contradictions in generative AI's political economy that more sanitized industry narratives (the "seamless librarian" imagery in [[Weinbrand2026-sf]], the techno-hagiographic genius-framing in [[Stanusch2026-ec]]) actively obscure. [[Tornberg2026-im]] operates at the broadest theoretical register, proposing a transition from "platform society" to "AI society" organized around cognitive lock-in, generative (rather than predictive) epistemics, and alignment politics—effectively naming the macro-structural conditions that the industry imaginaries of hype, inevitability, and apolitical quiet futuring work to naturalize.

[[Bucher2017-qh]], though predating the generative AI boom and focused on Facebook's recommendation algorithm, supplies this entire topic with its conceptual ancestor: the "algorithmic imaginary" as affectively-charged, non-illusory, and recursively generative of both user behavior and system behavior. Her insistence that imaginaries are not false beliefs to be debunked but productive forces shaping sociotechnical reality anticipates the stance taken by [[Stanusch2026-ec]] (imaginaries as "issuefying" work) and [[Dodds2026-df]] (hype as structuring infrastructure) alike, while her attention to ordinary, affective encounters with algorithmic breakdown offers a methodological counterpoint to the elite-discourse focus (newsrooms, industry executives, Silicon Valley communities) that dominates the rest of this topic—reminding us that the imaginaries of generative AI are ultimately also lived and felt, not just narrated by professionals and theorized by scholars.
