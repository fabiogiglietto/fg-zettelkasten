# Cultural Imaginaries and Framings of Generative AI

## Three Registers of Framing: Public, Insider, Formal

Generative AI does not have a single cultural image—it has several, produced in different institutional settings and legible to different audiences. The three papers gathered here sample distinct strata of this imaginative work: the mediated public sphere of national newspapers ([[Wang2025-zy]]), the semi-private discourse of the communities building the technology ([[Hepp2026-oi]]), and the analytic register of media theory attempting to strip away both hype and critique to ask what kind of medium AI actually is ([[Manovich2026-ih]]). Read together, they trace a movement from *how AI is talked about* to *how it talks about itself* to *how it might be understood on its own terms*.

**Mass-mediated imaginaries.** [[Wang2025-zy]] treats generative AI discourse as a comparative object, mapping the oscillation between utopian and dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers using Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework. Its contribution is scale and comparability: by quantifying imaginaries across national press systems, it shows that the "AI imaginary" is not monolithic but systematically inflected by local political and economic stakes. This is imaginaries as public narrative infrastructure—the stories a society tells itself about a technology it is only beginning to encounter, refracted through existing sociotechnical anxieties (labor, sovereignty, geopolitical competition) rather than through any direct encounter with the technology's affordances.

**Insider futuring.** [[Hepp2026-oi]] pulls the analytic lens dramatically closer, from national press discourse to the situated, ethnographically observed talk of Bay Area labs, houses, and pioneer communities. Where [[Wang2025-zy]] studies imaginaries as broadcast outward, Hepp studies imaginaries as produced from within—a "quiet futuring" that supplies the very tropes (AGI arrival, job loss, AI geopolitics) that later surface, amplified and simplified, in the "loud futuring" of CEOs and, presumably, in exactly the kind of press coverage [[Wang2025-zy]] catalogues. This suggests a genealogical relationship between the two papers' objects: the utopian/dystopian tropes that structure newspaper discourse may often originate in the discursive thickenings Hepp identifies at AGI House, Lighthaven, or EA Global, laundered through loud futuring's spokespeople before reaching a wider public. Hepp's insistence on fragmentation and contradiction—cyberlibertarian, EA/Rationalist, and "public AI" currents coexisting uneasily—also complicates any picture (like the national-comparative one in Wang) that treats an "imaginary" as internally coherent; even within a single geographic and social milieu, the imaginative landscape is riven by ideological difference, unified only by its shared, disavowed political stakes and its function as a reservoir for downstream amplification.

**From imaginary to medium.** [[Manovich2026-ih]] breaks from both papers' concern with social and political narrative altogether, bracketing ethical and critical framings to ask a different question: not what AI *means* culturally, but what it *is* as a medium—what it affords and resists formally. This is a deliberate methodological wager, explicitly positioned against both the modernist assumption that novelty is the measure of artistic value and the current tendency (visible in the other two papers) to read AI primarily through its social and political stakes. Yet Manovich's account is not simply orthogonal to the imaginaries literature; it can be read as a kind of empirical check on it. His finding that generative AI is "structurally conservative"—biased toward familiar academic and photographic conventions, resistant to logical surrealism and defamiliarization—cuts against both the utopian rhetoric of radical creative rupture found in Silicon Valley's loud futuring and the dystopian rhetoric of unbounded, alien capability found in press coverage of existential risk. The medium's actual formal properties (bounded creativity, media cognition, style-content entanglement) are more modest and more specific than either hope or fear narratives suggest, suggesting that cultural imaginaries of AI—whether journalistic or insider—may be poorly calibrated to the technology's concrete affordances as a creative tool.

**An emerging research arc.** Together these three pieces model a division of labor for studying AI's cultural life: comparative discourse analysis at the level of national publics, ethnographic analysis at the level of the communities generating the ideas that publics eventually receive, and media-theoretical analysis at the level of the artifact's own structural logic. A natural next question, raised by juxtaposing them, is how tightly coupled these levels actually are—whether the tropes Hepp finds thickening in San Francisco's appropriated places migrate recognizably into the national press imaginaries Wang maps, and whether either level of discourse is answerable to, or entirely insulated from, the medium-specific realities Manovich describes.
