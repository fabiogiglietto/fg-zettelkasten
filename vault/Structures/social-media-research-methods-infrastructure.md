# Social Media Research Methods & Data Infrastructure

## Social Media Research Methods & Data Infrastructure

### The collapse of the API era and its aftermath

The backbone of this topic is a historical arc running from open platform APIs to today's regulated-but-restrictive "walled garden." [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the inflection point — Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure — coining the "post-API age" and arguing that reliance on fragile, revocable platform access is a structural liability for computational research. [[Bruns2019-nr]] extends this into the "APIcalypse," reframing post-Cambridge-Analytica API lockdowns not as privacy protection but as a deliberate move to frustrate critical, public-interest scholarship, with early alternatives like Social Science One dismissed as PR-managed "data lotteries." [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodize this trajectory in more detail — Wild West, Golden Age of the Academic API, pay-to-play paywall — showing the pattern recurs across Facebook, Twitter/X, YouTube, Reddit, and TikTok, and that each contraction is driven by scandal, leadership change, or commercial calculation rather than principled policy. Bastos2025-ya offers an elegiac retrospective specifically on "Twitter studies" as a subfield built on now-vanished infrastructure, while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the cost empirically: a bibliometric eulogy showing 25% annual growth in Twitter-based publications through 2022 followed by stagnation and a 13% decline by 2024 after Enterprise API pricing hit $42,000/month. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review corroborates this at the level of the whole field, showing social-media-based social science publishing tripling and then plateauing/declining in 2022–2024, with Twitter/X and Facebook chronically overrepresented relative to actual platform use (YouTube, TikTok underused) — a structural mismatch between what is studied and what is used.

Several papers document the specific mechanics of platform-controlled replacements. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]] jointly dissect a subtle artifact of Meta's URL Shares Dataset — the 100-public-shares censoring threshold — showing it inflates apparent fake-news prevalence by roughly 4x and introduces a spurious platform-wide "force" that can masquerade as cross-national or COVID-driven trends. [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "Seeing like an API" study of CrowdTangle users shows how researchers adapted to (and thereby entrenched) a platform-controlled epistemic infrastructure through optimizing, extending, and verifying workarounds, reproducing institutional and Global-South exclusions. [[Bruns2026-pn]] follows the lineage into CrowdTangle's successor, the Meta Content Library, arguing that "clean room" environments — justified by privacy and legal concerns — structurally privilege quantitative, code-literate, well-resourced researchers while blocking qualitative and cross-platform work. [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s audit of YouTube's search API shows comparable epistemic damage from a different mechanism: severe temporal decay in search coverage that makes the API "forgetful by design," undermining both historical research and DSA systemic-risk obligations. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds to this landscape constructively, building TubeStats/TokStats as independent random-sampling infrastructure to solve the "denominator problem" that keyword- and API-based samples cannot.

### Regulation as a contested fix: the Digital Services Act

A cluster of papers treats the EU's DSA, and especially Article 40, as the pivotal — if unstable — attempt to convert data access from platform philanthropy into legal right. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frames current friction optimistically as evidence regulation is finally biting rather than failing, urging a "marathon" posture against platform resistance. [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] supply more operational detail: vetting bottlenecks, circular funding problems, WEIRD-centric skew, and the near-total absence of consumption-side (demand) data tools relative to production-side ones. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys the broader toolkit landscape in this regulatory moment. [[Annabell2026-ev]] and [[Peters2026-mo]] dig into the discursive politics underneath the law itself: Annabell shows "data" is an undefined "floating signifier" that platforms strategically narrow to aggregated, quantified datasets, sidelining qualitative traditions; Peters shows "data quality" is similarly absent from official texts and was pushed into the Delegated Regulation only through academic/NGO advocacy against platform resistance (Meta notably inverting the argument to claim access is futile because its own data is poor). [[Schulte2026-df]] grounds these abstract governance debates in a concrete case — the 2025 German election — arguing observability itself should be treated as reusable research infrastructure rather than rebuilt ad hoc each cycle.

### Industry influence, dependency, and the ethics of entanglement

A parallel strand interrogates not access per se but the corrupting effects of dependency on platforms for data, funding, and legitimacy. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] form a matched pair: the Perspective piece catalogs mechanisms of industry influence (suppressed internal research, asymmetric statistical standards, performative collaboration) analogous to tobacco and fossil-fuel science, while the empirical companion quantifies it — roughly half of high-profile social media papers in Science/Nature/PNAS carry undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small clique of authors, editors, and reviewers, yielding ~80% "industrial saturation" of the field. [[Heiss2026-qv]] makes the comparative case explicit, arguing platforms are uniquely positioned relative to pharma/tobacco/food because they are the sole source of the data needed to study them. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s critique of the Meta2020 partnership operationalizes this concern through a single landmark case: even the most ambitious platform-academic collaboration produced narrow, non-replicable estimates with poor "temporal" and "poetic" validity, suggesting empirical social science is structurally outpaced by platform change — a conclusion that pushes toward proactive regulation rather than reactive study. [[Allen2025-ot]] offers a methodological rejoinder in the same vein, situating Piccardi et al.'s platform-independent, LLM-reranking browser-extension approach as a promising way to study causal effects without platform cooperation at all.

### Adjacent infrastructural and legal risks

Several papers push outward from platform-provided data toward the broader apparatus researchers must build or risk to study social media. [[Park2026-tr]]'s qualitative study of researchers' lived experience under computer-crime and IP law documents real chilling effects — abandoned projects, vulnerability stockpiling, career damage — showing that even independent, scraping-based workarounds to platform restrictions carry serious legal jeopardy. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] and [[Luhring2025-od]] extend data-quality scrutiny to adjacent infrastructures: web scraping systematically distorts news-exposure measurement (dominated by collection-environment effects, not just delay), while the NewsGuard trustworthiness database — widely used as ground truth in misinformation research — is shown to be reasonably stable in continuous form but badly distorted when dichotomized, with a strong US-centric skew. [[Scalco2026-bd]] and [[Oswald2025-km]] probe conceptual validity threats from the demand side: the former operationalizes "information voids" as measurable supply-demand imbalances linked to misinformation prevalence, while the latter warns that the production-consumption gap — a small minority generating most visible content — distorts both lay and scholarly inference about public opinion from social media traces. [[Hartmann2025-px]] performs an analogous diagnostic service for echo-chamber research, arguing that disciplinary disagreement over whether echo chambers exist stems largely from inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than divergent realities — a methodological caution that resonates with [[Barbera2015-fw]]'s earlier demonstration that apparent echo-chamber effects are topic- and time-dependent, not fixed features of platforms.

### Detecting manipulation: the CooRnet/CooRTweet lineage and its critics

A distinct methodological cluster concerns coordinated inauthentic behavior detection, descending from network-based co-action approaches (the CooRnet/CooRTweet lineage). [[Mannocci2026-kc]]'s survey consolidates and critiques this literature, arguing that platform-driven definitions (e.g., Facebook's CIB) are theoretically thin and contingency-based, and proposing a general framework built on actors, synergic actions, and intent, parameterized along authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, and time-variance. [[Thiele2025-ol]] pushes further upstream to the attribution problem — who commissions these campaigns — offering a rational-choice typology linking campaign scale, elaborateness, and disguise to principals' stakes and resources. Several papers extend or stress-test detection methods empirically: [[Yang2025-iv]] proposes coordination signals based on sharing speed/frequency regularities to resist timing-based evasion on Facebook; [[Luceri2025-tr]] adapts network-similarity detection to TikTok's video-first ecosystem, where text-centric methods fail; Bastos2025-ol adds a visual dimension, using computer vision on the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium dataset to find country-specific visual "signatures" in troll-farm profile imagery; and [[Pante2025-pq]] turns the critical lens inward, re-testing prior claims of inter-state collaboration in information operations against modern detection methods and control datasets, finding earlier claims methodologically unsupported.

### Recruitment, measurement, and new computational tools

A smaller set of papers addresses alternative recruitment and measurement infrastructures. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] pioneers Facebook ad-based survey recruitment for hard-to-reach populations (conspiracy believers), demonstrating efficiency (low cost, high conversion via Pixel tracking) but inconclusive validity in actually capturing ideologically distinctive respondents compared to a CAWI benchmark — an early cautionary tale for a technique now widespread. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] shows a complementary use of ad infrastructure for intervention rather than recruitment, scaling prebunking videos to hundreds of thousands of Instagram users with durable effects. [[Kim2026-wg]] uses a custom real-time ad-tracking tool (EScope) to directly measure exposure to voter suppression advertising, illustrating how bespoke measurement infrastructure can resolve null findings produced by indirect exposure proxies in prior IRA research. [[Eady2025-vm]] contributes a scalable, label-free ideology-measurement model based on shared URLs, while [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] argues for multi-level ("political cartography") approaches to news-sharing research that move beyond oversimplified, outlet-only measures of political leaning.

Finally, a cluster addresses the newest infrastructural shift: LLMs as research tools. [[Balluff2026-if]] offers a skeptical overview of unreflective LLM adoption in communication research (reproducibility, bias, environmental cost), a concern echoed in [[Brown2025-jk]]'s more reassuring empirical finding that LLM annotation bias is dataset-specific and small relative to item difficulty, and in [[Alizadeh2026-es]]'s demonstration that coding agents can reproduce published findings at high rates but are vulnerable to sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting. Methodological innovations using LLMs and embeddings for substantive analysis — [[Arminio2025-tw]] on VLLM-based connotative image clustering, [[Fan2025-ut]] on linear concept erasure for deconfounding text embeddings, [[Fan2026-af]] on mining temporal user-sequences from donated digital trace data, [[Sarmiento2025-as]] on unsupervised framing analysis, [[Bruns2025-fz]] on practice-mapping multimodal interactions, and [[Gardam2025-er]] on visual-first methodology for Instagram conspiracy discourse — collectively signal a methodological reorientation toward multimodal, embedding-based, and LLM-assisted analysis as platform data itself becomes harder to obtain in bulk. Elsewhere, studies of platform culture and behavior ([[Volpe2026-um]] on micro-influencer labor, [[Graham2026-fb]] on Reddit "botting" as participatory culture, [[Wang2026-ub]] on the failed Twitter-to-Mastodon migration, [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] on algorithm literacy) round out the topic by using — and implicitly testing the limits of — these same data infrastructures to study platform culture itself, closing the loop between method and object of study. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], finally, offers an early conceptual corrective underlying much of this work: reframing "fake news" research away from creator intent and toward the propagation dynamics that these very tools and infrastructures are built to capture.
