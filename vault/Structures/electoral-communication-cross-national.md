# Electoral Communication & Cross-National Election Studies

## The Italian throughline: from hybrid TV/Twitter to coordinated-behavior detection

The oldest and most sustained strand in this collection is Italian, and it can be read as a single evolving research program. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] establishes the baseline logic — that Italian politics happens in a *hybrid* space where television and Twitter co-constitute each other, with second-screen practices remaining narrow and largely dictated by broadcast logic rather than by insurgent digital publics. This hybrid-media framing becomes the scaffolding for everything that follows: [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] shows how, by 2018, populist parties (M5S, the League) had built genuinely insular news ecosystems on Twitter that translated into distinct Facebook engagement signatures — insular sources get shared more, cross-partisan sources get contested in comments — with M5S uniquely able to mobilize supporters to amplify favorable stories and swarm unfavorable ones. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] then reorients the whole research question: rather than chasing "fake" content or "bad" actors, it operationalizes *coordinated link sharing behavior* as the unit of analysis, showing that coordinated networks disproportionately carry problematic domains regardless of whether they are avowedly political or covertly commercial. This actor-behavior pivot is theorized more broadly in [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]], which names three vectors — organizational blurring, microtargeting, and strategic amplification — that make traditional exposure measurement obsolete, previewing the infrastructural anxieties that recur throughout the whole topic.

By the 2022 snap election, the Italian sub-project had matured into a full detection pipeline: [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends the coordination-detection logic into a continuously-updating workflow, uncovering not just political hyperpartisan networks but clickbait and even religious-proselytism operations riding on the same infrastructure. Methodologically, this period also marks a turn toward LLM-assisted analysis: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks embedding models for clustering Italian political news, and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] confronts the validation problem of LLMs-in-the-loop pipelines directly, arguing that expert (not crowdsourced) annotation is now required because LLMs often outperform low-skilled human coders. [[Paci2025-ag]] pushes this scrutiny of LLMs further, showing that even frontier models struggle badly with the *implicit* content — implicature, presupposition — that saturates real Italian political speech, a sobering counterpoint to the optimism of the embedding papers. The most recent addition, [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], turns the coordination-detection apparatus back onto the platform itself: using the DSA-mandated Meta Content Library, it shows that Meta's political-content-reduction policy silently throttled Italian MPs' visibility months before its announced rollout, while extremist accounts largely evaded the throttling by posting more. [[Mosca2026-yh]] closes the loop conceptually, reframing "fake news" accusations themselves as a strategic rhetorical weapon of negative campaigning rather than a symptom of information disorder — a fitting endpoint for a line of inquiry that began with hybrid media and ends with meta-level discourse about disinformation itself.

## Germany: multimodal and multi-platform campaign visibility

The German sub-project is smaller but methodologically ambitious, and it picks up directly where the Italian visual/coordination work leaves off, applying computational vision and multi-platform infrastructure critique to the 2021 and 2025 federal elections. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o) against specialized computer-vision pipelines for recognizing candidates and counting people in Instagram imagery from the 2021 campaign, then uses the validated method to show that candidate accounts foreground front-runners far more than party accounts, and that stories and posts serve distinct visual-strategic functions. [[Schulte2026-df]] extends this to the 2025 election and to TikTok, but shifts the argument from findings to *infrastructure*: parties strategically differentiate by platform (AfD on TikTok, BSW on X), and the weak correlation between textual, vocal, and facial signals on TikTok demonstrates that multimodal analysis is not optional. Its central claim — that the real bottleneck in studying elections is not scholarly attention but the absence of reusable, standardized research infrastructure — echoes almost verbatim the infrastructural critiques found in the Italian and cross-national strands, tying the German sub-project into the topic's broader methodological anxiety.

## Brazil: misinformation, trust, and platform governance after Bolsonaro

The Brazilian sub-project is the topic's most theoretically integrated cluster, organized around the proposition that Bolsonaro-era disinformation is best understood as elite strategy channeled through specific media infrastructures rather than as generic platform pathology. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] opens the arc in 2018 with a crowdsourced Facebook ad-auditing system modeled on post-2016 US transparency concerns. The post-Bolsonaro cluster proper begins with [[Rodarte2026-dk]], which documents how parliamentarians contested epistemic authority around the Manaus healthcare collapse — government critics mimicking journalism, allies bypassing the press entirely, local brokers sidestepping polarization altogether — establishing that Brazilian elites treat social media as a site of narrative warfare with the press, not merely a broadcast channel. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] form a matched pair from the same three-wave panel around the 2022 election: the former shows belief in electoral misinformation directly increasing political *intolerance*, especially via messaging-app news use; the latter shows the same misinformation beliefs eroding *trust* in the election result, while mainstream news use bolsters trust even among Bolsonaro voters — together making the case that disinformation's damage runs through both affective and institutional channels simultaneously. [[Kansaon2025-id]] and [[Ventura2025-sw]] push the analysis into WhatsApp, the platform least visible to conventional feed-based methods: the former traces coordinated WhatsApp networks from doxxing campaigns to the January 8 uprising, the latter uses a deactivation experiment to causally isolate WhatsApp's distinct informational role. [[Ventura2026-yc]] extends the arc past Bolsonaro's defeat into the 2024 X ban, showing that state-imposed platform bans in polarized democracies produce durable partisan "sorting ratchets" — conservative users disproportionately circumventing the ban, pulling the platform's post-ban news environment rightward even after reinstatement. Read together, the Brazilian sub-project traces a single throughline from ad transparency (2018) through crisis narration and panel-survey attitudinal effects (2021-22) to encrypted-platform coordination and state-level platform governance (2022-24).

## The comparative scaffold: benchmarking against the US and cross-national work

Because the MINE programme's country studies are explicitly conceived as comparative, a substantial penumbra of non-Italian/German/Brazilian papers functions as calibration. The Meta-collaboration US 2020 studies — [[Appel2026-qr]] / [[noauthor_undated-bm]] on deceptive networks, [[Allcott2025-jb]] on null ad-removal effects, [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] on misinformation diffusion — establish the benchmark against which the Brazilian and Italian coordination findings are implicitly read, and [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific critique of that same partnership supplies the topic's most explicit statement of the problem all country sub-projects face: platforms evolve faster than peer review, so single-country, single-moment findings resist generalization. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary study (radical-right populism, not populism or right-ideology alone, predicts misinformation sharing) and [[Nenno2025-xa]]'s 24-country news-values study (flagging the US/Brazil pairing as a genuine outlier cluster) directly validate the choice of Italy, Germany, and Brazil as non-arbitrary, theoretically motivated cases rather than convenience samples. [[Kim2026-wg]] on geo-racially targeted US voter suppression, [[Simeone2025-vo]] on the ripple effects of Twitter deplatforming after the 2020 audit movement, and [[Starbird2025-jj]]/[[Prochaska2025-ef]] on evidence-frame and "deep story" sensemaking around US election integrity rumors round out a US comparative baseline against which the country sub-projects' coordination and narrative-contest findings can be triangulated. Papers such as [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] (Serbia), [[Gaw2025-ru]] (Philippines), and [[Balluff2026-bv]] (Austria) extend the comparative logic still further, each documenting locally distinct manipulation repertoires — mimetic grassroots simulation, brokerage-as-manipulation, covert media capture via advertising — that sharpen what is specific versus general about the Italian, German, and Brazilian cases.

## Cutting across borders: infrastructure, method, and theory

A final cluster is less about any single election than about the shared *conditions of possibility* for studying all of them. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] map the post-API "price, proficiency, permission" landscape under the DSA, directly motivating the workflow choices visible in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] contributes a general LLM-scaling method for positioning political text that complements the Italian embedding/clustering work. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] gesture toward methodological gaps (multi-level news cartography, algorithmic ad delivery) that the country sub-projects have only partially addressed. And two papers point toward where the topic is heading next: [[Lin2025-xp]]'s cross-national (US/Canada/Poland) experiment on LLM persuasion dialogues, and [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Registered Report redesigning feed algorithms to correct norm misperception on Bluesky, both suggest that the comparative election-studies line is now extending from *detecting* manipulation to *designing* and *testing* interventions against it — a natural next phase for a research program that began by asking who controls the narrative and is increasingly asking who controls the algorithm.
