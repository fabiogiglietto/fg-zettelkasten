# Sociocybernetics & Social Systems Theory

## Systems Theory as a Structural Prior: From Internet Architecture to Agentic AI

The four papers gathered here trace a consistent methodological commitment: social phenomena — whether Internet infrastructure, misinformation cascades, machine agency, or artificial agent populations — cannot be understood through technical or individualist frames alone. They require systems-theoretic and cybernetic concepts that treat emergence, observation, and social attribution as primary explanatory resources rather than afterthoughts. Read together, they show sociocybernetics migrating across three decades of technological objects, from Web 2.0 prosumerism to large language model agents, while preserving a core theoretical grammar inherited from Luhmann, von Foerster, and Bateson.

### The Internet as a Social-Technical System

[[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] opens the arc with an explicitly interdisciplinary provocation: Internet architecture, long treated as a purely technical design problem, needs to be re-conceived through social capital theory (Bourdieu, Coleman, Putnam). Its critique of the mismatch between networking notions of centrality (degree, betweenness, closeness) and their social capital counterparts is an early instance of a recurring theme in this cluster — that formal/structural properties of a network do not automatically map onto their social meaning, and that collapsing the two produces analytically impoverished models. The paper's insistence that the "prosumer" and "network prosumer" destabilize the consumer/producer binary anticipates, in a modest architectural register, the later concern with distributed, co-evolving agency that becomes central to the AI-focused papers in this set.

### Second-Order Observation and the Propagation of Falsity

[[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] radicalizes the systems-theoretic commitment by importing second-order cybernetics directly into the study of misinformation. Its central move — relocating analytical attention from the intending creator of "fake news" to the propagator's judgment — is a Luhmannian gesture: information is not a fixed property of a message but "a difference that makes a difference," constituted anew at each point of observation and communication. The micro/meso/macro model (individual judgment, dyadic injector–propagator combinations, emergent global cascades) operationalizes the classic sociocybernetic problem of how micro-level communicative operations aggregate into macro-level, seemingly autonomous patterns without any centralized authorship. This paper's rejection of a single authoritative typology of falsity, and its insistence that coexisting incompatible truth-claims are the normal condition of the hybrid media system, exemplifies the anti-foundationalist stance that runs through the entire structure note: social order does not require consensus, only recursively stabilized communication.

### Agency as Social Status, Not Technical Property

[[Anicker2024-vp]] pushes this constructivist logic to its most direct confrontation with contemporary AI. Where much of the AI ethics and alignment literature asks whether a system "has" agency as an intrinsic capacity, Anicker reframes the question in properly sociological terms: agency is a socially granted and monitored license, attributed through practices rather than discovered through inspection. This paper functions as the conceptual hinge of the cluster — it takes the observer-dependence emphasized in [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] and applies it not to information but to the ontological status of the acting entity itself. The "matrix of AI agency" it proposes is less a technical classification than a demarcation device rooted in social attribution, directly continuous with Luhmann-adjacent thinking about how systems constitute their own boundaries and licenses to act.

### Social Theory as Structural Prior for Multi-Agent Systems

[[Ng2026-og]] arrives as the most formally ambitious extension of this lineage, and arguably the payoff toward which the earlier three papers gesture. It explicitly argues that agentic AI research has inherited an individualist, single-task paradigm that ignores exactly the kind of systems-theoretic insight the other three papers supply from different angles. Its Multi-Agent Social Systems (MASS) framework — formalized as a triple of information exchange, influence dynamics, and interaction network — can be read as an attempt to give Luhmannian/cybernetic intuitions (emergent macro-order from micro-communication, network-constrained dependence, co-evolutionary coupling, distributional instability) a tractable mathematical shell, tested against a genuinely large-scale empirical AI social network (2.1M posts, 39,700 agents). Its four structural priors recapitulate, almost point for point, themes seeded earlier: distributional instability and co-evolution echo the emergent, non-reducible cascades of [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]; network-constrained dependence revisits the centrality/bridging concerns of [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]]; and the paper's insistence that alignment research addresses a "dyadic user-operator" problem distinct from population-level governance implicitly extends Anicker's point that agency and its regulation are social-relational matters, not properties intrinsic to a system in isolation.

### An Arc from Infrastructure to Artificial Sociality

Taken as a sequence, these four papers narrate an expanding domain of application for the same theoretical toolkit associated with ISA RC51: from the architecture of networks ([[Sofia2012-802a21dc]]), to the epistemics of communication and truth within those networks ([[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]), to the status of the entities that communicate ([[Anicker2024-vp]]), to the formal modeling of populations of such entities interacting at scale ([[Ng2026-og]]). What holds the arc together is a shared refusal of technological or individualist reductionism: none of these papers is content to explain Internet dynamics, misinformation, machine agency, or agentic AI behavior by appeal to individual properties (a device's capabilities, a message's origin, a system's technical design) without situating them within the relational, observer-dependent, and emergent structures that social systems theory and cybernetics were built to describe. The most recent paper's explicit call for social theory as a "structural prior" for AI system design can thus be read as a contemporary restatement of the sociocybernetic program this whole structure note traces — a program whose vocabulary has proved remarkably portable across three very different generations of networked technology.
