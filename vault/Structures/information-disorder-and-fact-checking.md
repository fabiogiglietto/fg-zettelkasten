# Information Disorder & Fact-Checking

# Information Disorder & Fact-Checking

## From "Fake News" to Cultural Narrative

The field's conceptual foundations run through a persistent unease with the term "fake news" itself. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] opens the corpus with a foundational provocation: creator-centric typologies of false information fail because they ignore *propagation*—the cascading, observer-dependent judgments that determine what a claim becomes as it circulates through the hybrid media system. This systems-level reframing anticipates a broader arc across the collection away from binary true/false taxonomies and toward narrative, cultural, and rhetorical accounts. [[Marwick2026-qd]] pushes furthest in this direction, arguing disinformation is best understood as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *stories*—with characters, catchphrases, and moral arcs—rather than discrete false facts, a claim echoed and extended in [[Sadler2025-vu]]'s hermeneutic-realist critique of loose narrative talk in disinformation studies, [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]]'s survey evidence that cumulative narrative exposure (not isolated claims) drives belief, and the CSCW-rooted sensemaking work of [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]], which show how "deep stories" about election fraud are collaboratively performed by influencers and audiences through framing actions layered atop largely accurate evidence. [[Goel2025-iq]] operationalizes this insight computationally, showing mainstream news is routinely repurposed into misleading narratives—a phenomenon invisible to domain-blocklist methods—while [[Waight2025-al]] offers a methodological advance (LLM-based narrative-similarity measurement) that makes such narrative diffusion tractable at scale, applied to Russian "biolabs" claims. Meanwhile [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] document the term's rhetorical *weaponization*: "fake news" as an empty, delegitimizing label deployed by hyperpartisan outlets and political actors rather than a description of fabricated content, with [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] showing how Meta's own Newsroom discourse similarly externalizes blame onto "inauthentic" outsiders.

## Measuring the Problem: Prevalence, Reach, and Its Limits

A substantial empirical strand asks how much misinformation actually circulates and who is exposed. Findings are notably mixed and often counter alarmist assumptions. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] track rising misinformation *shares* on TikTok and in European Facebook feeds respectively, yet [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] finds *views* of untrustworthy content remain stable—a dissociation between supply and exposure. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] sharpens this with platform-wide 2020 US data: exposure is low on average, concentrated among a small minority, and experimentally reducing it produces no attitudinal effects—directly complicating narratives of mass persuasion. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] adds diffusion-tree evidence on how misinformation virality is structured on Facebook, while [[Mosleh2024-op]] extends measurement beyond Twitter/Facebook to seven platforms, finding divergent engagement patterns that undercut single-platform generalizations. Methodological infrastructure papers interrogate the *instruments* behind these findings: [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] shows Meta's 100-share threshold artifact confounds cross-country comparison in the URL Shares Dataset, and [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard itself, cautioning that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can distort findings even as continuous scores have stabilized. [[Scalco2026-bd]] inverts the prevalence question entirely, operationalizing "information voids"—demand outstripping credible supply—as a mechanistic precursor to misinformation uptake. Cross-nationally, [[Nenno2025-xa]] and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] press the field to move beyond WEIRD contexts, finding only modest, unevenly generalizable news-value differences in flagged content across 24 countries. At the everyday-experience level, [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds ordinary users flag mainstream/alternative news outlets, not fringe sources, and mostly text-based clickbait—a picture at odds with platform-centric, audiovisual-threat framings.

## Who Spreads It, and Why: Elites, Populism, and Platforms

A second cluster asks about supply-side dynamics. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] provides the most systematic elite-level answer: across 26 countries, neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation sharing, but their *interaction*—radical-right populism—is the strongest determinant, reframing misinformation as strategic party politics rather than generic platform pathology. [[Tai2026-qk]] complements this with evidence that institutional role and ideology function as *checks* on US elected officials' sharing behavior. [[Renault2025-uh]] intervenes directly in the "biased fact-checkers" debate, showing Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under X's crowdsourced Community Notes, undermining claims that professional fact-checking alone explains partisan asymmetries. State and coordinated-actor dynamics are treated by [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s labor-market analysis of the Internet Research Agency, [[Copland2025-em]]'s account of Sky News Australia as transnational network propaganda, [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s study of TikTok's sonic propaganda around Ukraine, and [[Graham2025-gp]]'s case study of how #IStandWithPutin exploits, rather than evades, platforms' "infrastructure of truth." [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a unifying construct spanning state propaganda, gambling networks, and poorly moderated adult-content groups, arguing platform enforcement categories (CIB) are too narrow to catch structurally similar manipulation. Theoretical consolidation of this space is offered by [[Thiele2025-ol]] (a rational-choice attribution model for coordinated manipulation) and [[Mannocci2026-kc]] (a survey unifying coordinated-behavior detection across authenticity, harm, orchestration, and time). [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extends detection to emerging decentralized platforms like Bluesky. [[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Marwick2025-vx]] shift register toward audience-side production, showing how ConspiracyTok creators and "redpilled" far-right adherents construct legitimacy through populist, evidence-mimicking epistemic performances rather than passive exposure.

## Effects: Beliefs, Trust, Polarization, and Real-World Harm

Downstream-effects papers increasingly move past belief-change outcomes toward institutional and behavioral consequences. [[Adam2026-tz]] models the "marriage" of media exposure and predispositions in COVID conspiracy formation, finding both contagion and mitigation pathways. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes furthest toward causal, real-world harm, linking geolocated antivaccine tweets to a modeled 14,000 vaccine refusals and measurable excess COVID deaths. Election-integrity work spans multiple democracies: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] show Brazilian electoral misinformation belief predicts both rising political intolerance and eroded trust in results, with mainstream news use acting as a resilience factor; [[Ventura2025-sw]] isolates WhatsApp's distinct causal role via a deactivation experiment; [[Wack2026-bt]] shows deepfake impact in Kenya is "socially negotiated" through surrounding comments rather than fixed at production. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] examine second-order effects of *discourse about* misinformation itself: media narratives on election interference asymmetrically heighten perceived division along partisan lines, while a novel "hostile misinformation effect" shows citizens perceive their in-group as disproportionately targeted—paralleling the classic hostile media effect. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance to European voters' disinformation concerns, and [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s long-run global analysis contextualizes all of this within a half-century, non-universal decline in trust in news that predates the internet.

## Modality, AI, and the Shifting Ground of Verification

A fast-growing subset confronts generative AI's disruption of both disinformation production and fact-checking practice. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds visual/AI disinformation does *not* uniformly outperform text—effects are issue-bound, tempering alarm about synthetic media's unique power—while [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies a modality-congruent carryover effect in deepfake authentication difficulty. [[Emilio2026-ik]] theorizes the endpoint of this trajectory: "synthetic realities" that induce a rational, society-wide discounting of digital evidence, an "epistemic tax" requiring defense-in-depth mitigation. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] responds epistemologically, proposing "emergent facts" as a fourth category beyond evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based facts to capture GenAI's probabilistic opacity. [[Mattis2026-gu]] finds all task-specific AI disclosures in journalism reduce trust, with fact-checking disclosure penalized most heavily—a finding with direct implications for [[DeVerna2025-dl]]'s demonstration that LLMs are poor political fact-checkers unless paired with curated retrieval, dramatically improving on web search or reasoning alone. Applied fact-checking innovation is represented by [[Ramos2026-qo]]'s argument-checking/narrative-monitoring hybrid (developed by Maldita.es around the Valencia floods) and [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s "post-truth space" mapping technique for locating influential unverified sources, part of the vera.ai project explicitly aimed at tools like those the topic references.

## The Institutional and Political Economy of Fact-Checking

A final cluster interrogates fact-checking's own legitimacy and dependencies. [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform funding through differentiation and transcendence, while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically dismantles Zuckerberg's censorship framing, showing removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases and fact-checkers broadly favor transparency over deletion, expressing skepticism toward Community Notes—directly corroborated by [[Holt2026-zq]]'s finding that user "False News" reports mostly flag politically contested content users disagree with, not falsity per se. [[Heiss2026-qv]] and [[Peters2026-mo]] extend this institutional lens to the research infrastructure itself, arguing platform data access (DSA Article 40) is politically contested and vulnerable to industry capture akin to tobacco or pharma research. [[Xue2025-bp]] examines fact-checking's own rhetorical choices, finding emotionality is prevalent despite objectivity norms. Zooming out, [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes "misinformation-at-scale" as a structural feature of engagement-driven platform business models rather than a bug, a claim that dovetails with the collection's most explicitly synthetic, agenda-setting contributions: [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts (rating platform governance and journalism reform above individual-level fact-checking and literacy interventions), [[Frischlich2025-vn]]'s critique of single-analogy (virus/warfare/pollution) framings in favor of multilevel complexity thinking, and [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s call to import climate psychology's lessons on collective action, vested-interest denial, and researcher organization into misinformation science. Together these pieces mark the field's maturation from adjudicating individual claims toward reckoning with the institutional, economic, and epistemic structures that make information disorder a durable feature of the hybrid media system.
