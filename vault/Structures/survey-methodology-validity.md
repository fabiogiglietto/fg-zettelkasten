# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The comprehension problem at the root

The most fundamental challenge to survey-based inference is also the most neglected: we rarely know whether respondents understood a question the way we meant it. [[Hinck2026-yj]] names this gap directly, arguing that survey research routinely proceeds without any evidence of shared meaning between researcher and respondent, and calls for comprehension testing to become a standard part of survey methodology rather than an occasional add-on from cognitive interviewing. This is a bracing frame for the rest of the cluster: every representativeness statistic, every attention check, and every clever recruitment pipeline presupposes that the underlying item is being read correctly in the first place. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] offers an unintentional illustration of the stakes. Its Italian AI-attitudes data show a persistent gap between self-reported exposure to AI and self-reported understanding of it—most respondents say they feel only "a little" informed even as their tool use rises sharply—which is precisely the kind of discrepancy between surface response and underlying comprehension that Hinck's critique would push researchers to interrogate rather than take at face value.

## Who answers, and does it matter?

A second line of inquiry asks not whether respondents understand questions but whether the people answering them are the right people at all. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] provides the most systematic treatment here, benchmarking nine opt-in online panels against each other on response validity, representativeness, and respondent "professionalism." Its central finding—that these dimensions trade off against one another, with demographically-quota-matched samples (Lucid, Forthright) being more representative but less attentive, partly because of heavier mobile use—undercuts any assumption that "online panel" is a single, interchangeable category. Crucially, Stagnaro et al. show that representativeness matters most for detecting heterogeneous treatment effects, which survive only in the more representative samples, while main effects and cognitively driven experimental effects replicate almost everywhere; post-stratification weighting can patch the latter but not the former. This paper, more than any other in the set, supplies the empirical vocabulary (validity tiers, attention-check filtering, demographic quota effects) that the rest of the cluster implicitly relies on when discussing sample quality.

[[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] approaches the same representativeness question from the recruitment side rather than the panel-vendor side, asking whether a novel channel—Facebook ads targeted by interest and demographic micro-segment—can reach niche, hard-to-survey populations (in their case, Italian conspiracy-theory sympathizers) efficiently and validly. The efficiency case is strong: using Pixel-based conversion tracking and custom-audience exclusion, they recruit over a thousand valid respondents at under fifty cents each, outperforming prior Facebook-based response-rate benchmarks. But the effectiveness case is more equivocal, echoing Stagnaro's representativeness concerns in a different register—the Facebook-recruited sample does not show significantly elevated conspiracy endorsement relative to a general-population CAWI benchmark, leaving open whether "interest" targeting actually identifies holders of the stigmatized belief or just people adjacent to the topic. Read together, [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] frame representativeness and validity as problems that must be solved twice: once in choosing among established panels, and again when inventing new recruitment channels for populations the established panels cannot reach.

## Extracting meaning from open-ended text

A third thread turns the comprehension problem around: instead of worrying whether respondents understood a closed-ended item, it asks what we might learn if we let them answer in their own words—and how to make that rich but unwieldy data tractable at scale. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes combining LLMs with paired-comparison methods to convert free-text responses into continuous latent scales (of knowledge, ideology, emotion, or policy position), explicitly positioning this as superior both to constraining closed-ended items and to labor-intensive manual coding or cruder LLM classification schemes. The Anthropic report [[UnknownUnknown-db]] is a large-scale applied instance of exactly this logic: Claude-powered classifiers infer occupation, career stage, and productivity/displacement sentiment from ~81,000 open-ended survey responses, linking self-reported experience to independently observed usage patterns. Its findings—a dose-response relationship between occupational AI exposure and displacement concern, a U-shaped relationship between perceived speedup and perceived threat—are substantively about AI and labor, but methodologically they demonstrate both the promise and the comprehension risk this cluster keeps circling back to: classifiers could only infer occupation for 39% of respondents, a reminder that automated coding of open text inherits, rather than resolves, the underlying question of whether free-text answers are legible in the way the researcher assumes.

## Quality judgments as a survey-measurement case study

[[Gilardi2026-hw]] sits at an angle to the other papers but usefully stress-tests several of their concerns in a single experiment. Using a preregistered survey with opt-in Swiss respondents, it finds that readers rate AI-generated, AI-assisted, and human-written news as statistically equivalent in credibility and quality absent disclosure, but that disclosure produces a short-term engagement bump that does not persist into stated future intentions. This is a textbook case of the gap between what a single survey item appears to measure (willingness to read AI news) and what it actually captures (curiosity or an "automation heuristic" in the moment)—exactly the kind of interpretive slippage that [[Hinck2026-yj]] warns is rarely checked. It also depends, like the panels in [[Stagnaro2025-pz]], on an opt-in sample whose generalizability is asserted rather than independently validated, underscoring that even well-designed experimental manipulations inherit the foundational comprehension and sampling uncertainties this structure note traces throughout.

## The arc

Taken together, these seven papers trace a progression from diagnosis to remedy. [[Hinck2026-yj]] diagnoses a comprehension deficit at the heart of survey practice; [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] diagnose a parallel sampling deficit, showing that who answers and how they were recruited shapes what inferences are even possible; [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] and [[UnknownUnknown-db]] offer a technological remedy—LLM-assisted analysis of open-ended text—that promises richer, less constrained measurement but imports its own comprehension and classification uncertainties; and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gilardi2026-hw]] serve as applied illustrations, in the specific domain of public attitudes toward AI, of how self-reported understanding, demographic heterogeneity, and disclosure-driven response shifts can all distort the signal a survey is meant to capture. The throughline is a call for epistemic humility: good survey research requires evidence not just that a sample resembles the population of interest, but that respondents understood the question being asked of them.
