# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The comprehension problem at the root

The topic's throughline is best summarized by [[Hinck2026-yj]]'s blunt diagnostic: survey researchers routinely assume shared meaning between question and respondent without ever testing it. This is not a niche complaint but a foundational one — every claim about representativeness, sample quality, or recruitment efficiency downstream of a survey instrument presupposes that respondents interpreted the instrument as intended. The paper's call for explicit comprehension evidence functions as a kind of accountability standard against which the rest of this literature can be read: do these methods actually verify what they claim to measure, or do they merely optimize proxies (completion rates, attention-check pass rates, targeting precision) that stand in for validity without demonstrating it?

## Sourcing respondents: opt-in panels and platform-based targeting

[[Stagnaro2025-pz]] operationalizes exactly this kind of accountability for the opt-in panel ecosystem, benchmarking nine platforms on response validity, representativeness, and "professionalism" rather than treating "opt-in online sample" as a monolithic category. Its central trade-off — that demographically-quota-matched samples (Lucid, Forthright) are more representative but less attentive, largely via heavier mobile use — offers a concrete illustration of Hinck's abstract worry: attentiveness and comprehension are not guaranteed by demographic fidelity, and vice versa. Its finding that simple front-end attention checks can substantially raise validity without sacrificing representativeness, and that weighting recovers main effects but not heterogeneous ones, gives practical teeth to what would otherwise be a purely conceptual concern about unverified respondent quality.

[[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] extends the sample-sourcing question to novel recruitment infrastructure: rather than choosing among existing opt-in panels, it builds one from scratch via Facebook ad targeting, using Pixel-based tracking to construct a genuine conversion-rate metric for a hard-to-reach population (Italian conspiracy-theory sympathizers). The paper demonstrates that platform advertising can be efficient and affordable, but its inconclusive comparison against an ITANES benchmark — the Facebook sample did not show significantly stronger conspiracy endorsement than the general population — echoes the same evidentiary gap Hinck identifies: efficient recruitment does not, by itself, establish that the platform's interest categories capture the underlying construct the researcher intends to measure. Facebook's targeting taxonomy is treated as a proxy for offline attitudes in exactly the unverified way survey question wording is treated as a proxy for comprehension.

## Measurement choices as a second axis of researcher degrees of freedom

[[Schemer2026-mh]] shifts the lens from *who answers* to *how answers are scored*, using specification curve analysis across 504 operationalizations of media-partisanship and polarization measures. Its key finding — that method choice swings coefficient magnitude dramatically while never flipping sign — complements Stagnaro's demonstration that heterogeneous effects are sample-dependent even when main effects are robust. Together these papers suggest a layered fragility in survey-based inference: results can be sensitive both to *whom you sample* and to *how you code what they said*, and audience-based versus content-based measures can inflate or dampen the same underlying relationship by a factor of several. This resonates with Hinck's broader point that survey researchers often under-document the assumptions baked into their instruments and coding schemes alike.

## Scaling toward richer, better-validated measurement

Two papers gesture toward computational responses to the comprehension and measurement-choice problems. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes combining LLMs with paired-comparison techniques to convert open-ended text into continuous latent scales, explicitly motivated by the limits of closed-ended items (which constrain what respondents can say) and traditional manual coding (which cannot scale). The Anthropic report [[UnknownUnknown-db]] offers a large, applied instance of this logic: Claude-powered classifiers infer occupation, career stage, and productivity/displacement attitudes from ~81,000 free-text responses, linking self-report to independently observed usage data. Both papers implicitly wager that LLM-assisted coding of open text can get closer to what respondents actually mean than fixed-choice items can — a direct, if untested, response to Hinck's comprehension critique. But neither paper interrogates whether LLM classification itself introduces new, unverified assumptions about meaning, leaving open exactly the kind of validation gap that Stagnaro's and Schemer's empirical benchmarking exercises exist to close.

## Megastudies, replication, and infrastructure for trustworthy inference

[[Voelkel2026-lc]] models an institutional rather than technical response to quality concerns: a registered-report megastudy (preceded by five replication pilots) testing the ten most-cited climate messages at N=13,544. Its findings — several widely cited strategies fail to replicate, effects that do emerge are small and largely non-heterogeneous by party, and attitude shifts do not translate into costly behavior (donations) — serve as a case study in why single, underpowered studies proliferate over-optimistic claims, paralleling Stagnaro's argument that a single quick study on a single opt-in platform cannot be trusted to reveal heterogeneous effects. Both papers advocate scale and cross-validation (multiple samples, multiple pilots) as the corrective infrastructure for a field prone to overclaiming from thin evidence.

## The same problem beyond self-report

Finally, [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that the comprehension/measurement-validity problem is not unique to self-report surveys. Comparing in-situ to ex-situ web-scraped news content, it finds that the collection *environment* itself — not the emphasized time delay — is the dominant source of measurement distortion, and that these errors are systematically, not randomly, distributed across content categories. This is structurally the same warning as Hinck's: researchers have long assumed that scraped or trace-based data faithfully represents user exposure, much as survey researchers assume respondents understand questions as intended, and in both cases the assumption goes empirically unchecked until someone builds the comparison. Read together, these eight papers trace a single arc — from the foundational admission that survey researchers lack comprehension evidence, through empirical audits of sample and measurement validity, novel recruitment channels, and computational tools for extracting meaning, to a shared conclusion that no stage of the data pipeline, self-report or trace-based, should be taken on faith without direct methodological interrogation.
