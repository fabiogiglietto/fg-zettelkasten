# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The opt-in sample problem

The empirical core of this topic is the demonstration, most systematically in [[Stagnaro2025-pz]], that "online sample" is not a single category. Comparing nine opt-in panels side by side, the paper documents a genuine trade-off: samples built on demographic quotas (Lucid, Forthright) are more representative even on attitudinal and non-demographic dimensions, but purchase that representativeness at the cost of response validity — more inattentive, speeding, mobile-heavy respondents. Crucially, the paper shows that heterogeneous treatment effects (the kind political scientists most want to detect) only surface in the more representative samples, and that post-stratification weighting can rescue average effects but not subgroup effects. This is a sobering methodological lesson that reverberates through the rest of the collection: a "significant" finding in an unrepresentative sample may simply be invisible to a more representative one, not because the treatment doesn't generalize but because the moderating population isn't there to be sampled.

[[Voelkel2026-lc]] supplies a large-scale illustration of exactly this dynamic from the other end — a registered-report megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messages, run on a scale (N=13,544) that only opt-in online recruitment makes affordable. Its finding of small effects, minimal partisan heterogeneity, and a stark attitude–behavior gap could itself be read through Stagnaro's lens: absent evidence about the representativeness of its own sample, it's unclear whether the null heterogeneity reflects a real absence of audience-specific persuasion or a sample not diverse enough to reveal it. Read together, the two papers argue for treating sample composition as a first-order methodological variable, not an afterthought to be waved away by weighting.

## Does anyone understand the question?

[[Hinck2026-yj]] reframes the representativeness debate around a prior and more foundational problem: even a perfectly representative sample tells us little if respondents don't interpret the question as the researcher intends. Its polemical claim — that survey research routinely proceeds without any evidence of shared meaning between researcher and respondent — sits upstream of everything else in this set. It implicitly indicts standard practice across the other papers here: attention checks (as in [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]) verify effort, not comprehension; large-N megastudies (as in [[Voelkel2026-lc]]) achieve statistical power without necessarily achieving semantic validity.

[[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] can be read as a partial technical response to this comprehension gap. By pairing LLMs with paired-comparison scaling of open-ended responses, it aims to recover the richer, less constrained meaning that closed-ended items foreclose — precisely the kind of evidence Hinck argues is missing — while avoiding the prohibitive labor costs of manual open-ended coding. [[Dubey2026-bl]] touches the same nerve from the construct-validity side: its two studies of a "balanced news chatbot" show that self-reported trust and perceived usefulness (measured via an adapted Technology Acceptance Model) predict attitudes and intentions almost perfectly, a suspiciously clean result that itself raises the comprehension question — are respondents distinguishing these constructs, or are they collapsing them into a single evaluative judgment that the survey instrument then over-fits?

## Beyond surveys: auditing the instruments of digital social science

Several papers extend the "is our measurement instrument valid?" question beyond the survey response itself to the infrastructures researchers increasingly rely on as substitutes or complements for self-report. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard, the source-trustworthiness database that has become a default measurement tool in misinformation research, showing that while continuous scores have stabilized since 2022, the binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs long used in published studies can flip substantive conclusions depending on which database snapshot a lone major outlet happens to cross. This is structurally the same lesson as Stagnaro's: a measurement convenience (a single cutoff, a single opt-in panel) can silently determine the finding.

[[Ulloa2024-jm]] makes an analogous case for web-tracking data, showing that ex-situ scraping — the standard way researchers reconstruct browsing content after the fact — introduces roughly 34% content distortion from the collection environment itself, dwarfing the temporal-delay effects the field had previously worried about, and that this error is non-randomly distributed across news categories in ways that bias downstream conclusions about media diets. [[Brown2025-jk]] performs a similar audit for LLM-based text annotation, the increasingly common substitute for human coding in subjective classification tasks: it finds that demographic "bias" in LLM annotation is small and dataset-specific, and that item difficulty (human annotator disagreement) is a far stronger predictor of LLM-human divergence than any demographic variable — a finding that reframes annotation validity as a property of the item, not primarily of the coder (human or machine). Together, these three papers argue that the representativeness-and-validity scrutiny long applied to survey samples needs to be applied with equal rigor to the newer measurement pipelines — trustworthiness databases, scraped content, LLM annotations — that are rapidly being treated as ground truth.

## Recruiting hidden populations

[[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Lyons2026-ca]] both address the practical problem of finding respondents who are rare or hard to reach through conventional panels. Iannelli's Facebook ad-based recruitment procedure, tested on Italian conspiracy-theory sympathizers, demonstrates that Pixel-based conversion tracking and custom-audience exclusion can make platform advertising into a controllable, low-cost recruitment channel — achieving a 3.28% conversion rate at €0.46 per respondent — even as its central substantive question (whether interest-based targeting actually surfaces more conspiracist respondents than a general-population benchmark) remains inconclusive. This ambiguity is itself instructive: platform-based recruitment can be efficient without yet being demonstrably effective at capturing the niche attitudinal population it targets, echoing Stagnaro's caution against conflating operational convenience with substantive validity.

[[Lyons2026-ca]] tackles a structurally similar hard-to-reach population — the small subset of people who consume the bulk of low-credibility health content online — not through platform-based recruitment but by linking a national survey to passive web- and YouTube-tracking data. Its key methodological move, using behavioral trace data to validate and extend self-reported constructs (discernment, conspiracist worldview), exemplifies one direction in which comprehension and validity concerns can be triangulated rather than resolved by better wording alone. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] extends this same logic to a genuinely novel and hard-to-reach population: Claude.ai users surveyed at N≈81,000, whose open-ended responses are classified via LLM-powered inference (recovering usable occupation data for only 39% of respondents) rather than closed-ended coding — a direct real-world application of the LLM-assisted open-response scaling that [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes in the abstract.

## When method choice becomes the finding

[[Schemer2026-mh]] closes the loop by making researcher degrees of freedom the explicit object of study. Running a specification curve of 504 combinations of media-slant and polarization operationalizations on the same panel data, it shows that measurement choices — audience-based versus content-based partisan slant scores, feeling thermometers versus trust versus misperception measures — swing coefficient magnitudes nearly sevenfold without flipping their sign, and that difference-score conventions mask real asymmetries between conservative and liberal media effects. This is the most direct demonstration in the collection of the meta-claim implicit in nearly every other paper here: from opt-in sample choice ([[Stagnaro2025-pz]]) to database snapshot ([[Luhring2025-od]]) to scraping environment ([[Ulloa2024-jm]]) to slant-measurement operationalization ([[Schemer2026-mh]]), the "finding" in social science is frequently inseparable from an underdetermined bundle of measurement decisions — and the collection's shared argument is that these decisions deserve the same scrutiny, evidence, and reporting standards that [[Hinck2026-yj]] demands for the humbler question of whether respondents understood the question at all.
