# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## Comprehension as the unexamined foundation

The most basic question a survey can raise — do respondents understand the question the way the researcher intends? — turns out to be the one methodologists ask least often. [[Hinck2026-yj]] makes this absence explicit, arguing that survey research routinely proceeds without any evidence that comprehension matches intent, leaving inference about attitudes and behaviors resting on an unverified assumption of shared meaning. This is a deliberately foundational claim, and it sets the terms for everything else filed under this topic: before asking whether a sample is representative or a recruitment method efficient, we should ask whether the instrument itself is measuring what it claims to measure. Several of the other papers here can be read as *de facto* responses to this challenge — not explicit comprehension tests, but adjacent efforts to check whether survey-based or trace-based measures correspond to the underlying constructs researchers care about.

## Sample quality and representativeness trade-offs

[[Stagnaro2025-pz]] provides the topic's empirical centerpiece, directly interrogating the "opt-in online sample" as a methodological category. Comparing nine platforms along validity, representativeness, and professionalism, it documents a structural trade-off: samples built with demographic quotas (Lucid, Forthright) better match population benchmarks but show lower response validity, partly because they recruit more mobile-phone respondents; curated panels (Prolific, CloudResearch) show the reverse pattern. Critically, the paper shows that simple front-end attention checks can substantially raise validity without eroding representativeness — a low-cost fix with real consequences, since heterogeneous treatment effects (by partisanship, race, age) only surface reliably in the more representative, quota-based samples, and weighting cannot recover them in the less representative ones. This finding complicates any simple validity-representativeness hierarchy: researchers studying moderation effects need representativeness that simple attention filters cannot substitute for, while researchers needing clean "main effect" signal may prefer the high-validity, low-representativeness tier. Open MTurk is flagged as uniformly poor, a useful negative benchmark.

## Reaching hard-to-survey populations through platform advertising

[[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] extends the sampling question to populations that standard panels struggle to reach at all — in this case, conspiracy-theory sympathizers in Italy — by building a Facebook ad-based recruitment pipeline using Pixel conversion tracking and custom-audience exclusion to measure a true "conversion rate" rather than a crude click-through rate. The procedure is shown to be cheap and efficient (€0.46 per valid respondent, a 3.28% conversion rate exceeding prior benchmarks), demonstrating that platform advertising can be an operationally viable alternative to panel-based CAWI or snowball sampling for niche populations. But its *effectiveness* — whether interest-based targeting actually captures the ideologically distinctive population it aims for — is inconclusive: the Facebook-recruited sample did not differ significantly from a general-population benchmark in conspiracy endorsement. Read alongside [[Stagnaro2025-pz]], this suggests that efficiency gains from novel recruitment channels do not automatically translate into validity gains; the representativeness problem simply resurfaces in a new guise, this time as uncertainty about what platform "interest" targeting actually selects for.

## When the measurement, not the sample, is the problem

A second cluster of papers shifts the locus of concern from *who* is recruited to *how* the resulting data are measured and operationalized, extending the comprehension-and-validity logic beyond self-report into digital-trace and source-rating methodologies. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that web-tracking studies — often treated as a gold-standard alternative to self-reported media exposure — carry their own severe measurement distortions: ex-situ scraped content diverges from in-situ captured content by roughly 34%, with errors concentrated non-randomly in paywalled or interaction-gated categories, biasing substantive conclusions about news diets in ways that simple post-hoc corrections cannot fix. [[Lyons2026-ca]] uses cleaner linked survey-and-trace data to show that exposure to low-credibility health content is rare but concentrated among older, lower-discernment, more conspiracist-minded users, and that this exposure travels through referral chains among low-credibility sites rather than through search or social media — a finding only possible because the trace data could be validated against survey-measured discernment and beliefs, illustrating why linking self-report to behavioral data (rather than trusting either alone) matters for inferential validity. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs an analogous audit at the level of the measurement *instrument* itself, showing that NewsGuard's source-trustworthiness database has stabilized since 2022 but that binary "trustworthy/untrustworthy" classifications can radically distort downstream findings compared to continuous scores — a direct illustration of Hinck's broader worry that researchers rarely interrogate whether their operationalizations capture what they intend.

[[Schemer2026-mh]] generalizes this concern into a systematic test: applying specification-curve analysis across 504 combinations of media-slant and polarization measures, it finds that methodological choices change the *magnitude* (though not the direction) of the partisan-media/polarization relationship, with audience-based slant measures inflating estimates relative to content-based ones. This paper essentially operationalizes the comprehension-and-validity worry as a researcher-degrees-of-freedom problem, showing empirically how much published "findings" about polarization are artifacts of measurement choice.

## New instruments, old questions

A final cluster asks how emerging computational tools — chiefly LLMs — reshape, and potentially reproduce, these validity and comprehension problems rather than resolving them. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes LLM-paired comparisons as a way to scale open-ended survey responses into continuous latent measures, directly addressing the trade-off between the depth of open-ended items and the labor cost of hand-coding — a question of what, exactly, open-ended text is a valid signal of. [[Brown2025-jk]] interrogates the reliability of LLMs as annotators of subjective, contentious text, finding that demographic "bias" in LLM-human disagreement is small and dataset-specific, while item difficulty (human annotator disagreement itself) is by far the dominant predictor of agreement — a result that reframes annotation validity less as a model-bias problem and more as a measurement-difficulty problem, echoing Stagnaro's point that aggregate validity metrics can mask where the real signal breaks down. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], Anthropic's survey of 81,000 Claude.ai users, shows both the promise and the fragility of this approach at scale: occupation and productivity-gain attributes are inferred from open text via Claude-based classifiers, successfully linking self-report to independently observed usage patterns, but with only 39% of occupations even classifiable — a concrete reminder that LLM-assisted measurement inherits, rather than escapes, the comprehension and coverage problems at the heart of this topic.

Two applied studies round out the picture by showing comprehension and validity concerns playing out in substantive survey design. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] demonstrates that disclosure framing (not underlying content quality) drives short-term engagement with AI-generated news, a reminder that what respondents report "wanting" can reflect question context and novelty rather than stable preference — precisely the kind of gap between intended and actual meaning that [[Hinck2026-yj]] warns about. [[Fattorini2026-bo]], using repeated nationally representative surveys, documents a persistent gap between self-reported *exposure* to AI information and self-reported *understanding* of it, empirically illustrating the exposure–comprehension disjunction that motivates this entire topic area: more contact with a survey's subject matter does not guarantee that respondents grasp it as researchers assume.

## Synthesis

Read together, these papers trace a single arc: survey-based inference depends on layered, largely unverified assumptions — that respondents understand questions as intended ([[Hinck2026-yj]]), that samples represent the populations they claim to ([[Stagnaro2025-pz]]), that novel recruitment channels reach who they target ([[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]]), that trace and source-level measures capture real exposure rather than artifacts of collection method ([[Ulloa2024-jm]], [[Lyons2026-ca]], [[Luhring2025-od]]), that operationalization choices don't drive substantive conclusions ([[Schemer2026-mh]]), and now that LLM-based coding and simulation preserve rather than launder these same uncertainties ([[Brown2025-jk]], [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]], [[UnknownUnknown-db]]). The applied cases ([[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Fattorini2026-bo]]) show these abstractions mattering in practice: engagement measures track disclosure framing, not quality; exposure measures track media contact, not comprehension. The cumulative message is that "data quality" in survey and survey-adjacent research is not a single property to be checked once, but a chain of validity claims — comprehension, sampling, recruitment, measurement, operationalization, and now AI-mediated coding — each of which needs its own evidence rather than inherited trust.
