# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The comprehension gap and the case for evidence

The topic begins with a foundational discomfort articulated by [[Hinck2026-yj]]: survey researchers routinely have no evidence that respondents understand the questions the way researchers intend them. This is not a claim about a specific instrument or population but about the evidentiary culture of the field — comprehension is assumed rather than demonstrated, and validity claims rest on that unverified assumption. The paper's force is that it reframes several other papers in this cluster as partial, ad hoc responses to a problem that has rarely been named directly. Attention checks, professionalism indicators, and demographic quotas — the tools [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] catalogues — are proxies for the same underlying worry: are respondents actually engaging with, and correctly interpreting, what we ask them?

## Opt-in samples are not interchangeable

[[Stagnaro2025-pz]] supplies the empirical backbone for the sampling half of this topic. Across nine opt-in platforms, the paper shows that "online sample" is not a natural kind: response validity, representativeness, and professionalism trade off against each other in patterned ways, with quota-based samples (Lucid, Forthright) buying demographic fidelity at the cost of attentiveness and honesty, while curated panels (Prolific, Connect) buy validity at the cost of representativeness. This matters directly for the comprehension problem that [[Hinck2026-yj]] raises: a respondent who is inattentive or speeding is a respondent who, whatever the question's intended meaning, is unlikely to have processed it. The paper's most actionable finding — that two trivial front-end attention checks substantially raise validity without much hurting representativeness — is essentially a low-cost partial fix for the comprehension problem operationalized through behavioral proxies rather than direct cognitive testing. But the paper also flags the limits of such fixes: post-stratification weights can restore main effects even in poor samples, yet they cannot recover heterogeneous treatment effects, meaning that whatever is lost when validity is low is not simply a nuisance parameter that reweighting can restore.

## Recruitment as its own validity problem

Where [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] evaluates samples that already exist, [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] tackles the upstream question of how to construct a sample for a population that standard panels cannot reach at all — supporters of conspiracy theories in Italy, recruited via Facebook ads. The paper's contribution is largely technical: Pixel-based tracking, URL parameters, and custom-audience exclusion give researchers a genuine conversion-rate metric rather than the coarse click-through rates used in earlier Facebook-ad studies, and the resulting recruitment is cheap and efficient. But its more sobering result echoes the representativeness concerns of [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] from a different angle: efficiency in recruitment does not guarantee validity of measurement. The Facebook-recruited sample did not show significantly higher conspiracy endorsement than a general-population benchmark, leaving open whether Facebook's "interest" targeting actually identifies the psychological trait researchers assume it proxies for — a targeting-level analogue to the question-level comprehension problem in [[Hinck2026-yj]].

## New instruments for measuring what respondents actually say

[[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extends the topic into instrument design rather than sampling: if closed-ended items are too constrained to reveal what respondents actually think, and open-ended text is too costly to code by hand, LLM-based paired comparison offers a way to scale latent-trait measurement from free text. This is a direct methodological complement to the comprehension concern — rather than assuming shared meaning behind a fixed-response item, it tries to recover meaning from what respondents wrote in their own words, at scale. Read alongside [[Hinck2026-yj]], it suggests one concrete strategy comprehension research could adopt: mining open-ended responses (or cognitive probes) computationally rather than treating comprehension evidence as prohibitively expensive to collect.

## Digital traces as a parallel front for measurement validity

Several papers in this set extend the "is our measurement doing what we think it's doing" question from surveys proper into the paired world of digital-trace and platform-based data, which increasingly substitutes for or supplements self-report. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that web-scraped news content diverges substantially from what users actually saw in situ — a distortion driven mainly by the scraping environment itself, not by time delay — and that this error is non-randomly distributed across content categories, meaning naive bias corrections fail. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a comparable audit on NewsGuard source-trustworthiness ratings, finding that continuous scores are reasonably stable since 2022 but that binary trustworty/untrustworthy classification can flip results based on single sources crossing a threshold — a measurement-validity warning structurally similar to [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]'s point that filtering decisions (attention checks) can have outsized, non-obvious effects on downstream inference. [[Lyons2026-ca]] links survey self-report to passive web and YouTube tracking, and in doing so exemplifies a design response to exactly the concerns the other papers raise: rather than trusting self-reported exposure, it validates concentration and demographic patterns in low-credibility content consumption against behavioral trace data, revealing effects (extreme concentration among a small minority of older users) that would likely be invisible or noisy in survey self-report alone.

## Meta-methodological stress-tests and applied stakes

[[Alizadeh2026-es]] sits one level up from data collection, asking whether AI coding agents can faithfully reproduce social-science findings — a computational-reproducibility question rather than a respondent-comprehension one, but it belongs to the same broader epistemic concern running through this topic: how much can we trust the pipeline, human or automated, that turns raw data into published claims? Its finding that confirmatory prompting induces "sycophantic" specification search is a machine-analogue of the response-validity and professionalism problems in [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] — an agent, like a low-validity respondent, can produce output that looks compliant without reflecting genuine engagement with the underlying evidence.

Finally, [[Fattorini2026-bo]], a substantive nationally-representative survey on Italian attitudes toward AI, functions less as a methodological intervention than as a case study in the stakes of getting these measurement questions right. Its central finding — a persistent gap between self-reported exposure to AI and self-reported understanding of it — is itself a comprehension problem in miniature, playing out at the level of substantive content rather than instrument design: respondents report engaging with a technology-laden information environment while explicitly acknowledging they do not understand what they are seeing. Read against [[Hinck2026-yj]], this suggests that the discipline's comprehension problem is not confined to poorly worded items; it recurs at the level of what respondents believe they know about the topics surveys ask them to evaluate.

Together, these nine papers trace an arc from a foundational diagnosis (we don't know if respondents understand our questions) through empirical mapping of where sample quality and recruitment strategies fail or succeed, into parallel audits of the digital-trace and source-rating infrastructures that increasingly stand in for or supplement direct self-report, and finally into the computational tools — LLM-scaled open-ended coding, AI reproduction agents — that might either help close these validity gaps or introduce new ones of their own.
