# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The comprehension problem as a foundation

Before asking whether a sample is representative, one must first ask whether respondents understood what was asked of them. [[Hinck2026-yj]] stakes out this foundational position, arguing that survey researchers routinely publish inferences from closed-ended items without ever demonstrating that respondents interpreted the questions as intended. This is less a specific empirical finding than a provocation aimed at the field's evidentiary standards: shared meaning between researcher and respondent is assumed, not tested. Read as the opening move of this structure, it reframes everything that follows—sample quality, recruitment innovation, measurement scaling—as downstream of a more basic and largely unaddressed validity threat.

## Sample validity, representativeness, and the cost of reaching niche populations

[[Stagnaro2025-pz]] operationalizes a version of this skepticism at the level of *samples* rather than individual items, auditing nine opt-in online panels along response validity, representativeness, and professionalism. Its central finding—a trade-off in which demographically-quota'd samples (Lucid, Forthright) are more representative but less attentive/honest, while curated panels (Prolific, Connect) are the reverse—directly complicates any simple recommendation about "which platform to use." Critically, the paper shows that representativeness matters most for detecting *heterogeneous* treatment effects and that post-stratification weighting cannot substitute for a genuinely representative base sample. This nuances the comprehension worry raised by [[Hinck2026-yj]]: even if every respondent understood the question, a sample skewed toward professional, non-representative respondents will still misestimate who the effect holds for.

[[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] tackles the recruitment side of the same problem: how to reach populations—here, Italian conspiracy-theory sympathizers—that standard panels struggle to sample at all. By exploiting Facebook's Pixel-based conversion tracking and custom-audience exclusion, the study achieves an efficient, low-cost recruitment pipeline with a favorable conversion rate relative to prior ad-based survey work. Yet its own results echo the caution running through [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]: efficiency in reaching a niche population does not guarantee that platform-inferred "interest" targeting actually selects for the attitudes researchers assume it does—the Facebook-recruited sample did not show significantly stronger conspiracy endorsement than a general-population benchmark. Together these two papers frame recruitment and sample evaluation as two faces of the same validity question: getting people into the sample is not the same as getting the *right* people, defined either demographically or attitudinally.

## Extracting richer measures once respondents are in

A second strand asks what can be learned once data are collected, particularly from respondents' own words rather than forced-choice categories. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes pairing LLMs with paired-comparison scaling to convert open-ended text into continuous latent measures, explicitly motivated by the limits of closed-ended items (constrained variance) and traditional manual coding (unscalable). This is, in effect, a technical answer to part of the comprehension problem raised by [[Hinck2026-yj]]: if respondents' free text can be scaled reliably, researchers gain a window onto how people actually construe a question rather than forcing them into pre-set categories whose fit is unverified.

[[UnknownUnknown-db]] offers a large-scale applied instance of this same logic outside traditional academic survey research: Anthropic's analysis of ~81,000 Claude.ai users uses LLM classifiers to infer occupation, career stage, and productivity/displacement perceptions from open-ended self-reports, then links these inferred attributes to independently observed usage patterns. The convergence between self-reported displacement concern and observed occupational AI exposure is notable precisely because it triangulates survey self-report against behavioral trace data—a validity check in the spirit of [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]'s representativeness checks, but performed on inferred rather than directly asked attributes, and enabled by the same LLM-based extraction strategy that [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] formalizes methodologically.

## When measurement choices, not just samples, drive conclusions

[[Schemer2026-mh]] extends the "does method choice matter" question from sampling to *operationalization*, running a specification-curve analysis across 504 combinations of media-slant and polarization measures. Its key result—that the direction of the partisan-media–polarization relationship is robust but its magnitude and significance swing enormously depending on measurement choices, with audience-based scores inflating effects relative to content-based ones—is a direct structural analogue to [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]'s finding that main effects replicate across samples while heterogeneous effects do not. Both papers converge on a shared methodological moral: aggregate/main-effect conclusions are comparatively robust to design choices, but nuanced, disaggregated, or asymmetric patterns are exactly where researcher degrees of freedom (in sample selection or in variable construction) can quietly reshape the substantive story.

## Parallel measurement-validity concerns beyond the survey instrument itself

Several papers extend this cluster's core concerns—stability, comprehension, and validity of measurement—into adjacent, non-survey data infrastructures that increasingly substitute for or complement self-report. [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of the NewsGuard database shows that binary trust classifications (rather than continuous scores) can distort downstream misinformation research, a coverage-driven artifact strikingly similar to Stagnaro's warning that categorical filtering decisions (e.g., attention-check cutoffs) can nontrivially reshape conclusions even when they seem methodologically innocuous. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] makes an analogous point for web-tracking data: the *collection environment* itself (in-situ vs. ex-situ scraping), not just elapsed time, is the dominant source of measurement error, and this error is systematically—not randomly—distributed across content categories, directly paralleling how sample-validity issues in [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] are concentrated in specific subgroups (mobile users, certain demographics) rather than spread evenly.

Finally, [[Gilardi2026-hw]] returns to a properly survey-experimental design, but one that itself illustrates the comprehension and disclosure issues motivating [[Hinck2026-yj]]: readers rate AI-authored and human-authored news as equivalent in quality until authorship is disclosed, after which engagement intentions shift in ways the authors attribute to curiosity rather than genuine belief revision. This is a useful cautionary case for the whole topic—self-reported "willingness to engage" and "quality perception" are themselves interpretive constructs whose meaning to respondents may shift with contextual framing, reinforcing the broader argument that comprehension, sample composition, recruitment channel, and measurement operationalization are not separable methodological footnotes but jointly determine what survey-based (and survey-adjacent) research can credibly claim.
