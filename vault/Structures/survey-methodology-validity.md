# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The comprehension problem as a starting premise

The through-line connecting these papers is a simple but corrosive question: do we actually know what our respondents mean, and do we know who our respondents are? [[Hinck2026-yj]] states the first problem starkly, arguing that survey research as a field has almost no systematic evidence that respondents interpret questions the way researchers intend. This is not a call for better wording guides but for an evidentiary standard—comprehension should be tested and documented, not assumed. Read this way, the paper functions as the topic's conceptual anchor: every downstream methodological fix (sample selection, attention checks, LLM-assisted coding) is only as good as the shared meaning it presupposes between question and answer.

## Who answers, and how well: the opt-in sample landscape

[[Stagnaro2025-pz]] takes the second half of the "who are our respondents" question and answers it empirically at scale, benchmarking nine opt-in online panels on response validity, representativeness, and professionalism. Its central finding—a trade-off in which demographically-quota-driven samples (Lucid, Forthright) are more representative but less attentive, largely because of heavier mobile usage—reframes "online sample quality" as a multidimensional design choice rather than a single quality score. Crucially, the paper shows that cheap front-end attention checks recover much of the lost validity without eroding representativeness, but that post-stratification weighting cannot rescue heterogeneous treatment effects the way it can main effects. This nuance matters directly for [[Hinck2026-yj]]'s comprehension critique: attentiveness filters address whether respondents are *engaging* with items, but say nothing about whether they *understand* them as intended—two distinct failure modes that the field tends to conflate.

## Reaching the hard-to-reach: platform-based recruitment as an alternative

If [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] evaluates existing opt-in panels, [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] tests an alternative sampling infrastructure altogether—Facebook's ad platform—as a way to recruit niche, stigmatized populations (conspiracy-theory sympathizers) that standard panels struggle to reach. The paper's contribution is largely operational: using Pixel tracking, URL parameters, and custom-audience exclusion, the authors achieve a genuine conversion-rate metric and very low per-respondent cost. Yet its substantive payoff is more cautionary than triumphant: the Facebook-recruited sample did not show significantly higher conspiracy endorsement than a general-population benchmark, casting doubt on whether platform "interest" targeting actually identifies people holding the beliefs advertisers assume. This echoes the representativeness-validity tension in [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]—efficient, affordable recruitment does not guarantee that the resulting sample is *substantively* the population researchers think they're purchasing.

## Researcher degrees of freedom as a hidden layer of sample/measurement fragility

[[Schemer2026-mh]] extends the sample-quality argument into measurement-choice territory. Using specification curve analysis across 504 operationalizations of media partisanship and polarization, it shows that methodological decisions—which slant score, which dependent variable—shift the *magnitude* of documented relationships dramatically even while preserving their direction. This is a structural analogue to [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]'s sample comparison: just as no single opt-in panel is "the" answer, no single operationalization of a contested construct is neutral, and both papers converge on the same prescription—report sensitivity across plausible specifications/samples rather than a single point estimate.

## Digital-trace measurement as the sample-quality problem's sibling

Three papers extend the validity concern from survey respondents to the digital traces increasingly used to study public opinion. [[Oswald2025-km]] argues that the production-consumption gap on social media—a small vocal minority generating the discourse a silent majority merely consumes—systematically misleads both citizens and researchers who treat visible online content as a proxy for public opinion, a distortion structurally analogous to unrepresentative opt-in panels distorting substantive inference. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] and [[Luhring2025-od]] locate parallel validity threats further downstream in the data pipeline: web-scraped news content diverges substantially from what users actually saw in situ (driven mainly by paywalls and interactive elements, not time delay), and NewsGuard's widely used trustworthiness ratings, while largely stable since 2022, can produce sharply different conclusions depending on whether continuous or binarized cutoffs are used. Together these three papers generalize the topic's core lesson beyond traditional survey sampling: any data source treated as a stand-in for "what people think" or "what people are exposed to" carries its own selection and measurement artifacts that require explicit auditing before substantive claims can be trusted.

## New computational tools, old comprehension questions

Finally, two papers gesture toward LLM-based methods that promise to loosen the trade-off between rich, open-ended measurement and scalability—while implicitly reopening the comprehension question from a new angle. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes pairing LLMs with paired-comparison techniques to convert free-text survey responses into continuous latent scales, arguing this captures nuance that closed-ended items and manual coding cannot. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] operationalizes a version of this at large scale, using Claude-based classifiers to infer occupation, career stage, and productivity perceptions from ~81,000 open-ended responses to a Claude.ai user survey, linking self-report to observed usage patterns. Both papers implicitly bet that LLMs can recover intended meaning from unconstrained text more faithfully than closed-ended instruments—yet neither directly confronts [[Hinck2026-yj]]'s challenge of demonstrating that respondents and models share an interpretive frame. As LLM-mediated coding becomes a default tool for exactly the kind of large opt-in and platform-recruited samples discussed above, the comprehension-evidence gap the topic opens with becomes, if anything, more urgent rather than resolved.
