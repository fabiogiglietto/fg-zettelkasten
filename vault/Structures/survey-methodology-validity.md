# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The comprehension gap at the foundation

Any survey-based inference presupposes that a respondent interprets a question the way the researcher intends. [[Hinck2026-yj]] makes the case that this assumption is rarely, if ever, tested: the field has developed sophisticated tools for sampling and weighting but has left the semantic link between question and answer largely unverified. This is a foundational rather than incremental critique, and it frames the rest of this collection's concerns—about who answers surveys and how carefully—as downstream of a more basic, largely unaddressed problem of whether "answering" means the same thing to researcher and respondent.

## Who answers, and how carefully: evaluating opt-in samples

[[Stagnaro2025-pz]] takes up the empirical half of the data-quality problem, benchmarking nine opt-in online panels (Lucid, Prolific, Forthright, Connect, MTurk, and others) on validity, representativeness, and professionalism. Its central finding—a trade-off in which demographically-quoted samples (Lucid, Forthright) are more representative but less attentive, largely because of heavier mobile use—complicates any simple hierarchy of "good" versus "bad" panels and instead argues for matching platform to research purpose. Notably, the paper shows that lightweight front-end attention checks can substantially improve validity without much cost to representativeness, and that weighting fixes main effects but not heterogeneous treatment effects, meaning inattentive or unrepresentative respondents don't just add noise—they can silently erase theoretically important subgroup effects. This finding gives empirical teeth to Hinck's abstract worry: even when comprehension itself isn't tested, careless or misrepresentative respondents can produce answers that look valid in aggregate while masking exactly the heterogeneity researchers care about.

## Recruiting beyond the panel: hard-to-reach populations

Where [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] audits existing opt-in vendors, [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] tests a newer recruitment channel—Facebook ads—as a way to reach populations that panels struggle to find, using Italian conspiracy-theory believers as a hard case. The paper's contribution is largely infrastructural: it shows how Pixel-based conversion tracking, URL parameters, and custom-audience exclusion let researchers measure a true conversion rate and screen out duplicate or shared submissions, yielding a cheap, efficient, and comparatively well-instrumented recruitment pipeline. But its substantive payoff is more sobering: despite efficient targeting, the Facebook-recruited sample did not differ significantly from a general-population benchmark in conspiracy endorsement, suggesting that interest-based ad targeting may not reliably surface the ideologically distinctive respondents it promises. Read alongside [[Stagnaro2025-pz]], this reinforces a theme running through the topic: novel or non-probability recruitment methods can be efficient and well-instrumented on the sampling side while still leaving open questions about what kind of respondent, and what kind of answer, they actually deliver.

## Measurement choices propagate into substantive conclusions

[[Schemer2026-mh]] extends the quality-of-inference concern from *who* answers to *how the resulting variables are constructed*. Using specification curve analysis across 504 operationalizations of media partisanship and polarization, it shows that the strength (though not the direction) of a now-canonical finding—that partisan media use predicts polarization—is highly sensitive to seemingly small coding decisions, with audience-based slant measures inflating effects relative to content-based ones and bipolar difference scores masking real asymmetries between conservative- and liberal-leaning respondents. This is, in effect, a demonstration of researcher degrees of freedom operating on the measurement side of survey research, complementing Stagnaro's demonstration of sample-side degrees of freedom: both papers show that conclusions researchers treat as robust findings can be artifacts of specific, under-scrutinized methodological choices.

## Extending validity concerns beyond self-report

[[Ulloa2024-jm]] pushes the same logic of unexamined measurement error into a domain adjacent to but distinct from self-report survey items: behavioral trace data used as a survey substitute. Its finding that ex-situ web scraping introduces large, systematic (not random) distortions in measured news content—far larger than the temporal-delay effects previous work emphasized—shows that the same underlying worry animating [[Hinck2026-yj]] and [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] (does the measurement actually capture what we think it captures?) applies just as forcefully to "objective" digital-trace measures as to subjective self-reports, and that naive bias-correction strategies fail to fix it.

## New tools, same old question

The final two papers gesture toward where measurement is heading. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes using LLMs paired with comparison methods to convert open-ended survey text into continuous latent scales, explicitly aiming to recover the richness lost when researchers default to closed-ended items—an implicit rebuttal to the comprehension worries in [[Hinck2026-yj]], since open text at least reveals something of how respondents actually understood a prompt. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] offers a large-scale applied instance of this approach, using Claude-based classifiers to infer occupation, career stage, and displacement concern from ~81,000 open-ended responses and linking these inferred attributes to independently observed usage patterns. Together they suggest a possible resolution to several threads in this topic at once: LLM-assisted coding of open-ended responses could make it more feasible to check comprehension at scale, to extract signal from qualitative self-report without prohibitive labor costs, and to validate self-report against behavioral benchmarks—though, as with Facebook-ad targeting and web-scraped trace data, the crucial unresolved question is whether these AI-mediated measurements introduce their own new, and so far uncharacterized, sources of systematic error.
