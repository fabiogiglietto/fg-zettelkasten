# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## Does the Respondent Understand the Question? The Comprehension Gap

The most basic precondition for any survey-based inference is that respondents interpret questions the way researchers intend. [[Hinck2026-yj]] argues that this precondition is rarely tested: survey research as a field has normalized skipping evidence of comprehension, leaving causal and descriptive claims resting on an unverified assumption of shared meaning. This is a foundational critique that frames everything else in this topic — representativeness, sample quality, and recruitment innovations are all downstream concerns that presuppose comprehension has already been secured. [[Schemer2026-mh]] offers a concrete illustration of how fragile that assumption can be even when comprehension is not in doubt: using specification curve analysis across 504 operationalizations of media partisan slant and polarization, the authors show that *how* a construct is measured — audience-based versus content-based partisanship scores, bipolar difference scores versus disaggregated components — dramatically changes the magnitude (though not the direction) of substantive findings. Together these two papers establish that measurement validity is not a single checkbox but a multidimensional risk running from question wording through variable construction to analytic choices.

## Sample Quality and Representativeness in Opt-In Panels

If comprehension and construct validity are threats internal to any single respondent's answer, representativeness and response validity are threats at the level of the sample. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] provides the most systematic empirical treatment here, benchmarking nine opt-in online panels (Prolific, Lucid, MTurk, Forthright, CloudResearch products, etc.) on validity, representativeness, and professionalism simultaneously. Its central finding — a trade-off whereby demographically-quota-matched samples (e.g., Lucid, Forthright) are more representative but less attentive and more mobile-skewed, while curated panels (Prolific, CloudResearch) are cleaner but less representative — gives researchers a practical taxonomy rather than a single "best" platform. Crucially, the paper shows that simple front-end attention checks can substantially improve validity without costing representativeness, but that post-stratification weighting, while able to recover *main* effects even in poor-quality samples, cannot recover *heterogeneous* treatment effects — a limitation with direct implications for any researcher relying on reweighted convenience samples to study subgroup differences. This finding dovetails with Hinck's broader point: even a well-weighted sample does not guarantee that the underlying question-response process was valid for all subgroups equally.

## Platform-Based Recruitment as a Methodological Frontier

A parallel thread asks whether new recruitment channels — rather than legacy panels — can solve representativeness and hard-to-reach-population problems directly. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] develops and stress-tests a Facebook-ad recruitment pipeline using Pixel-based conversion tracking and audience exclusion to recruit a stigmatized, niche population (conspiracy-theory sympathizers) cheaply and efficiently. The procedure demonstrates clear *efficiency* gains — low cost per valid respondent, precise conversion metrics superseding blunter click-through measures — but the paper is appropriately cautious about *effectiveness*: the Facebook-recruited sample did not show stronger conspiracy endorsement than a general-population benchmark, leaving open whether platform "interest" targeting truly isolates the attitudinal population of interest, or merely approximates it. [[Allcott2025-jb]] extends the platform-methodology discussion in a different direction, using a large-scale randomized field experiment embedded directly in Facebook and Instagram's ad delivery infrastructure to estimate the causal effect of political advertising exposure on attitudes and behavior. While substantively about political advertising rather than survey recruitment per se, it exemplifies the same infrastructural turn — platforms as both the site of intervention and the object of measurement — and shares Iannelli's central methodological tension: null or inconclusive effects may reflect either a true absence of effect or a genuinely underpowered design, a distinction that is itself a data-quality problem.

## Computational Tools for Extracting Richer Measures

A newer strand addresses data quality not through sampling but through measurement technology: how to extract valid, scalable signal from open-ended or unstructured responses. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes pairing LLMs with paired-comparison methods to convert free-text survey responses into continuous latent scales, explicitly positioning this as a corrective to both the narrowness of closed-ended items and the labor cost of traditional open-text coding. The Anthropic report [[UnknownUnknown-db]] offers a large-scale applied instance of this logic, using Claude-powered classifiers to infer occupation, career stage, and displacement concern from ~81,000 open-ended responses and linking these inferred attributes to independently observed usage data. Read alongside Hinck's comprehension critique, these papers raise a second-order version of the same question: if LLM classifiers are now inferring what respondents *meant*, researchers need comparable evidence that the LLM's inferred construct matches the respondent's actual intent — the comprehension problem displaced from human coder to machine coder.

## Data Quality Beyond the Survey Instrument

Finally, [[Ulloa2024-jm]] extends the data-quality lens past self-report entirely, showing that web-scraped behavioral measures of news consumption — increasingly used as "objective" alternatives to self-reported media exposure — are themselves subject to severe, systematically distributed measurement error depending on collection environment (in-situ vs. ex-situ) rather than the time delay previously assumed to be the main culprit. This matters directly for papers like [[Schemer2026-mh]], which depends on valid media-exposure measures to operationalize partisan media diets; if such measures are partly constructed via scraping, Ulloa's findings suggest an additional, underappreciated source of the very specification variance Schemer documents. [[Fattorini2026-bo]], by contrast, offers a comparatively well-behaved substantive case study — a nationally representative, repeated cross-sectional survey of Italian AI attitudes — that illustrates what a representativeness-conscious, multi-wave design focused on comprehension gaps ("exposure" vs. "understanding") can look like in practice, serving as a positive counterpoint to the methodological risks catalogued elsewhere in this set.

## Synthesis

Across these nine papers, a coherent arc emerges: comprehension is the most basic and least verified assumption in survey research ([[Hinck2026-yj]]); even once comprehension is assumed, operationalization choices can swing substantive conclusions ([[Schemer2026-mh]]); sample source and composition introduce a second, well-documented layer of validity-representativeness trade-offs ([[Stagnaro2025-pz]]); novel platform-based recruitment offers a potential way to reach populations outside traditional panels, but its "effectiveness" relative to its clear "efficiency" remains unresolved ([[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]], [[Allcott2025-jb]]); and emerging computational tools promise richer measurement from open-ended or behavioral data while introducing their own unverified inferential assumptions ([[DiGiuseppe2025-es]], [[UnknownUnknown-db]], [[Ulloa2024-jm]]). [[Fattorini2026-bo]] stands as a reminder that well-designed representative survey work remains a viable, if imperfect, benchmark against which all of these newer methods must ultimately be judged.
