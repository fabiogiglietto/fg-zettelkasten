# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The comprehension problem: do respondents even understand the question?

The foundational challenge for this cluster of papers is articulated most starkly by [[Hinck2026-yj]]: survey researchers routinely publish inferences from closed- and open-ended items without ever demonstrating that respondents interpreted the question as intended. This is not a minor methodological footnote but a claim that shared meaning between researcher and respondent is usually *assumed*, not *evidenced*. Read against the rest of this topic, Hinck's argument functions as the upstream diagnosis for problems that the other papers try to detect, measure, or route around downstream — attention failures, low-effort responding, misclassification of respondent attributes — all of which can be understood as symptoms of an unverified comprehension chain.

[[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] can be read as a partial, technologically-mediated answer to Hinck's challenge. By pairing LLMs with paired-comparison techniques to scale open-ended responses into continuous latent measures, the paper implicitly argues that free text — precisely because it does not force respondents into researcher-imposed categories — offers a route to checking (or at least enriching) what respondents actually mean, without the prohibitive coding costs of traditional content analysis. It sits in productive tension with Hinck: rather than validating comprehension of a fixed-response item, it tries to let respondents speak in their own terms and infer structure after the fact.

## Sample quality and representativeness: not all opt-in samples are equal

[[Stagnaro2025-pz]] is the empirical center of gravity for the "response validity vs. representativeness" question. Its comparison of nine opt-in panels overturns the temptation to treat "online convenience sample" as a single methodological category: platforms with demographic quotas (Lucid, Forthright) are more representative on attitudinal and demographic benchmarks but show systematically lower response validity, an effect substantially explained by heavier mobile-phone usage. The paper's most actionable finding — that two trivial front-end attention checks meaningfully raise validity without eroding representativeness — offers a concrete, low-cost fix that resonates with Hinck's call for baked-in evidentiary checks, even though attention checks test engagement rather than comprehension per se. Just as important is Stagnaro et al.'s caution that post-stratification weighting can restore main effects but not heterogeneous treatment effects, meaning the choice of sample source has consequences that reweighting cannot paper over — a caution that echoes, from a different methodological register, the demonstration in [[Schemer2026-mh]] that operationalization choices (there, of media-slant and polarization measures) can swing coefficient magnitudes by nearly sevenfold even while leaving the sign of the relationship untouched. Together these papers make the case that "the data source" and "the measure" are not neutral scaffolding around a research question but active determinants of what gets found.

## Recruitment: reaching populations standard samples miss

[[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] extends the representativeness conversation from *which panel to buy* to *how to build a sample from scratch* for populations that standard panels struggle to reach. Using Facebook's advertising infrastructure (Pixel tracking, URL parameters, custom-audience exclusion) to recruit Italian conspiracy-theory sympathizers, the paper demonstrates that platform-based ads can be efficient and inexpensive (€0.46 per valid respondent, a 3.28% conversion rate exceeding most prior benchmarks) while leaving open whether such targeting actually captures a *distinctively* opinionated population — the Facebook sample did not differ significantly from a general-population CAWI benchmark in conspiracy endorsement. This null result is instructive alongside Stagnaro et al.'s tiered validity map: efficient recruitment and demonstrable representativeness are separate achievements, and a procedure can succeed on cost and reach while remaining agnostic on whether it captures the "hard-to-reach" attitudes it was designed to target.

## Beyond the survey instrument: representativeness of what gets observed at all

Two papers push the representativeness question outside the survey response itself, toward what data are even available to researchers claiming to describe "public opinion." [[Oswald2025-km]] argues that the visible content researchers scrape or observe on social media is generated overwhelmingly by a small, unrepresentative minority of highly active users, meaning that *any* inference — survey-adjacent or otherwise — built on "observed" online discourse inherits a production-consumption skew analogous to a badly unrepresentative sample. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] documents a parallel but more technical distortion in digital-trace research: ex-situ web scraping of news content diverges substantially from in-situ, user-experienced content, with errors concentrated non-randomly across content categories (especially paywalled and login-gated material). Neither paper is about survey questionnaires directly, but both extend the topic's core concern — does the collected data faithfully represent the population and experience it purports to measure — into adjacent data-generating processes that increasingly substitute for or supplement traditional survey recruitment.

## Stakes: why this matters for substantive inference

The applied AI-attitude surveys in this file ([[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]], and the Anthropic study [[UnknownUnknown-db]]) are not methodological papers, but they usefully dramatize the stakes. Each relies on exactly the kind of unverified inferential chain that Hinck warns about — self-reported "understanding" of AI, self-reported productivity gains, self-reported concern about displacement — filtered in the Anthropic case through LLM-based classification of open-ended text reminiscent of [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]]'s scaling approach, and in the Pew and Observa cases through nationally fielded but still self-report-dependent instruments. Read against the top of this structure note, they illustrate why the comprehension and representativeness questions are not academic: large public claims about how "Americans" or "Italians" understand and are affected by AI rest on survey architectures whose comprehension validity is asserted rather than demonstrated, and whose sample and measurement choices — as [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] and [[Schemer2026-mh]] show for other domains — could materially change the substantive picture.

## The arc

Taken together, the papers trace an arc from foundational skepticism ([[Hinck2026-yj]]) through empirical sample diagnostics ([[Stagnaro2025-pz]]), to innovations in recruitment ([[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]]) and analysis ([[DiGiuseppe2025-es]]), out to adjacent data sources that inherit the same representativeness anxieties ([[Oswald2025-km]], [[Ulloa2024-jm]]), and finally to a methodological reflexivity check ([[Schemer2026-mh]]) showing how much measurement choice alone can move substantive conclusions — with the applied AI surveys standing as case studies of what is at risk when these safeguards are skipped.
