# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## Foundational doubts: do we know what respondents mean, and who they are?

The topic's starting premise is unsettling: much of what survey researchers claim to measure rests on assumptions that have rarely been tested. [[Hinck2026-yj]] makes this explicit at the level of individual questions, arguing that the field has normalized publishing survey-based inferences without ever demonstrating that respondents interpret items as researchers intend—a gap that, if taken seriously, undercuts validity claims regardless of how the sample was drawn. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] takes the complementary tack of interrogating the *sample* rather than the *item*, running nine opt-in online panels through parallel batteries of attentiveness, honesty, and demographic/attitudinal benchmarking. Together these two papers frame the field's central tension: even a perfectly representative sample does not guarantee valid inference if respondents misread the question, and even perfectly comprehended questions yield distorted conclusions if the sample is skewed or inattentive. Stagnaro2025-pz's key empirical contribution—that representativeness and response validity trade off against each other (quota-based panels like Lucid and Forthright track ANES benchmarks well but show more inattentiveness and speeding, partly via heavier mobile use) and that cheap front-end attention checks can substantially repair validity without eroding representativeness—offers a partial, practical answer to the kind of evidentiary demand Hinck2026-yj is making, while also showing its limits: filtering recovers main effects even in poor samples (e.g., via post-stratification) but cannot recover heterogeneous treatment effects, which only emerge in the more representative panels. This suggests comprehension and representativeness problems are not interchangeable and must be diagnosed and remedied separately.

## Platform-based recruitment as a third axis

Where [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] treats existing opt-in panels as given objects to be audited, [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] asks whether researchers can *construct* a sample for populations no panel reaches well, using Facebook's ad infrastructure (Pixel tracking, custom-audience exclusion, URL-parameter passing) to recruit conspiracy-theory sympathizers in Italy. The paper's efficiency findings are striking on their own methodological terms—a 3.28% conversion rate and €0.46 per valid respondent, beating prior Facebook-ad CTRs—but its effectiveness result is more sobering: the Facebook-recruited sample did not show reliably stronger conspiracy endorsement than a general-population CAWI benchmark, leaving open whether interest-based ad targeting actually isolates the attitudinal niche it claims to. This null/inconclusive result is a useful counterweight to Stagnaro2025-pz's demonstration that representativeness can sometimes be engineered via quotas: Iannelli2018-ebd918b7 shows that for niche, stigmatized populations, the targeting mechanism itself (platform "interest" categories as proxies for real attitudes) is an unverified assumption of exactly the kind Hinck2026-yj warns against—here applied not to question wording but to audience definition.

## Augmenting and reinterpreting self-report with LLMs

A second cluster addresses not who answers or whether they understand, but how richly their answers can be extracted and scaled. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes pairing LLMs with paired-comparison methods to convert open-ended text into continuous latent-trait scales, explicitly positioning this as a way to recover the depth that closed-ended items lose while avoiding the cost of manual coding—an implicit rejoinder to the comprehension problem Hinck2026-yj raises, since open-ended responses at least give researchers raw material to check interpretation against, if that material can be processed at scale. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], Anthropic's report on ~81,000 Claude.ai users, operationalizes a version of this idea in the wild: Claude-powered classifiers infer occupation, productivity gains, and displacement concern from open-ended survey responses and link these to independently observed usage patterns. The convergence between inferred self-report (e.g., occupational exposure correlating with displacement concern) and the Economic Index's behavioral data is itself a validity check of the kind survey methodologists call for—though the fact that occupation could only be inferred for 39% of respondents is a reminder that LLM-based inference brings its own coverage and misclassification risks, echoing the comprehension-and-representativeness tensions found elsewhere in this set.

## Validity threats beyond the questionnaire: digital trace and discourse data

Two papers extend the volume's concerns to data sources that are not surveys at all but are increasingly used as proxies for public opinion. [[Oswald2025-km]] documents the production-consumption gap on social media—a small, vocal minority generates most visible political content while the majority remains silent—arguing that both citizens and researchers who treat visible online discourse as public opinion are systematically misled. This is a representativeness problem transposed from panels to platforms: just as Stagnaro2025-pz shows that opt-in survey samples are not interchangeable proxies for the population, Oswald2025-km shows that social media traces are an even more skewed proxy, with an unknown and compounding bias layered on top of self-selection. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] pushes the same logic into web-tracking methodology, showing empirically that ex-situ web scraping (the dominant method for reconstructing news exposure from browsing histories) distorts measured content by ~34–40% relative to in-situ capture, with errors concentrated non-randomly in paywalled and interactive content categories. Read alongside Stagnaro2025-pz's attention-check remedy and Iannelli2018-ebd918b7's platform-based recruitment, Ulloa2024-jm reinforces a shared methodological moral across very different data types: measurement error from recruitment or collection infrastructure is rarely uniform, and naive corrections (post-stratification weights, raising classifier thresholds) tend to repair aggregate point estimates while leaving distributional or subgroup-level distortions intact.

## When methodological choices, not just data quality, drive conclusions

A final pair of papers shows that even with reasonably collected data, the *choices* made in operationalizing constructs can determine substantive findings as much as sampling or comprehension does. [[Schemer2026-mh]] applies specification curve analysis to 504 combinations of media-slant and polarization measures in U.S. panel data, finding that while directional conclusions are stable, coefficient magnitude and significance swing considerably depending on whether partisan media exposure is scored from audience behavior or content analysis, and whether polarization is measured via feeling thermometers, trust, or misperceptions. This is a sobering companion to Stagnaro2025-pz's finding that heterogeneous treatment effects depend on sample choice: both papers show that "the same" relationship can look stronger, weaker, or undetectable purely as a function of researcher degrees of freedom, independent of any flaw in the underlying data collection. [[Wan2026-ai]], combining survey measures of political efficacy with actual Google Discover usage logs among Dutch users, sits at the intersection of self-report and behavioral trace data that this whole topic circles around—using linked survey-plus-log data to ask whether a subjective disposition (efficacy) conditions objectively observed selection behavior. Its design implicitly argues for exactly the kind of triangulation Hinck2026-yj calls for in the abstract: validating self-reported constructs against independently observed behavior rather than treating survey responses as self-certifying.

Taken together, the nine papers trace an arc from first principles (do respondents understand the question? is the sample representative and attentive?) through emerging recruitment and measurement technologies (Facebook ads, LLM-scaled open text) to the recognition that even well-collected data remain vulnerable to compounding, non-random distortions—whether in scraped web content, visible social-media discourse, or the researcher's own operationalization choices—that demand explicit testing rather than assumption.
