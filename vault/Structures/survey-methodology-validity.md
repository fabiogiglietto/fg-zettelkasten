# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The quality of opt-in samples

The empirical core of this topic is [[Stagnaro2025-pz]], a systematic comparison of nine opt-in online panels that treats "non-probability sample" as a category requiring dissection rather than a single methodological black box. Its central finding — that quota-based samples (Lucid, Forthright) buy representativeness at the cost of response validity, largely because they rely heavily on mobile respondents — establishes a trade-off that recurs throughout this literature: platforms differ not just in who they reach, but in how honestly and attentively those respondents answer. Crucially, the paper shows that simple front-end attention checks can substantially repair validity without eroding representativeness, but that post-stratification weighting only rescues main effects, not the heterogeneous treatment effects that are often the point of running an experiment on a diverse sample in the first place. This distinction between "getting an average right" and "getting the right people to detect it" is a methodological warning that echoes into questions of recruitment discussed below.

## Comprehension as a prior question

[[Hinck2026-yj]] steps back from sample composition to ask a more foundational question: even with a representative, attentive sample, do respondents understand the questions as researchers intend? The paper argues that survey research as a field has largely stopped demanding evidence of comprehension, treating shared meaning as an assumption rather than something to be tested. This reframes the validity concerns in [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] — attentiveness and effort are necessary but not sufficient if the underlying construct is misread. Read together, these two papers suggest a layered validity problem: first, are respondents paying attention and answering honestly (validity of *engagement*); second, are they answering the question that was actually asked (validity of *interpretation*). Most quality-assurance practices in the field, including attention checks, address only the former.

## Recruitment innovation for hard-to-reach populations

[[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] tackles a different piece of the same puzzle: not how to evaluate existing opt-in panels, but how to build new sampling frames for populations too niche for standard panels to reach efficiently — in this case, Italian supporters of conspiracy theories. Using Facebook's ad infrastructure (Pixel tracking, custom-audience exclusion, URL parameters), the study demonstrates that platform-based recruitment can be cheap, fast, and methodologically transparent about response rates in ways that beat prior click-through-rate benchmarks. Yet its own results underline the representativeness/validity tension from [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]: efficient recruitment did not clearly translate into a sample that was more ideologically distinctive than a general-population benchmark, leaving open whether Facebook's interest-based targeting actually captures the stigmatized attitudes it is meant to surface, or merely a differently-biased slice of the population. This is a caution against assuming that a novel recruitment channel solves the validity problem simply because it solves the reach problem.

## Extracting richer measures from open-ended text

A second cluster addresses how much information researchers can extract once respondents are recruited and have answered in their own words. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] argues that closed-ended items artificially constrain the depth of measured constructs, and proposes combining LLMs with paired-comparison methods to convert open-ended text into continuous latent scales, aiming to outperform both manual coding and cruder LLM-classification approaches. The Anthropic case study, [[UnknownUnknown-db]], is a real-world instance of this general strategy: it uses Claude-powered classifiers to infer occupation, career stage, and displacement concerns from free-text responses across ~81,000 users, linking self-reported experience to independently observed usage patterns. Read against [[Hinck2026-yj]]'s call for comprehension evidence, this LLM-based turn is double-edged: it promises richer, more naturalistic measurement of what respondents actually mean, but it also introduces a new inferential layer (model-inferred attributes) whose own validity — does the classifier interpret free text the way a human coder or the respondent themselves would — is exactly the kind of comprehension question the field has historically neglected to test explicitly.

## Measurement choices as a source of divergent findings

[[Schemer2026-mh]] extends this validity concern from the response side to the researcher's side of the measurement pipeline. Using specification curve analysis across 504 combinations of media-slant and polarization operationalizations, it shows that substantive conclusions about partisan media's effect on polarization are highly sensitive to arbitrary-seeming coding decisions (audience-based vs. content-based slant scores, bipolar vs. disaggregated indices), even though the *direction* of effects remains stable. This complements [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]'s demonstration that weighting recovers main effects but not heterogeneity: both papers show that a single "significant effect" can mask enormous variability once the researcher's degrees of freedom in measurement are made explicit, and both argue for disaggregation (by sample, by subgroup, by political valence) as a diagnostic against false consensus in findings.

## A wider methodological throughline

[[Ulloa2024-jm]], though centered on web-tracking rather than self-report, belongs to the same broader argument about measurement fragility: it shows that a plausible-seeming methodological convenience (scraping article content after the fact rather than in-situ) introduces large, *systematically* — not randomly — distributed errors that bias downstream substantive conclusions about news categories and exposure. This mirrors the logic running through the survey papers: measurement error is rarely just noise to be averaged away; it is patterned, correlated with substantive variables of interest (partisanship, device type, content category), and capable of silently reshaping conclusions about heterogeneity, effect size, or direction. Together, these seven papers trace an arc from sample-level diagnostics ([[Stagnaro2025-pz]]), to recruitment innovation and its unresolved validity payoff ([[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]]), to the comprehension gap underlying all self-report ([[Hinck2026-yj]]), to new computational tools for extracting meaning from open text and their own untested validity ([[DiGiuseppe2025-es]], [[UnknownUnknown-db]]), and finally to the demonstration that even downstream analytic choices about how to operationalize constructs can rival sampling and recruitment as sources of divergent empirical conclusions ([[Schemer2026-mh]], [[Ulloa2024-jm]]).
