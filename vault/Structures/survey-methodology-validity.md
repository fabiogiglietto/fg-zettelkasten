# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## Foundations: Do We Know What Respondents Think They're Answering?

The most basic question in survey methodology—whether respondents interpret questions the way researchers intend—turns out to be the least empirically grounded. [[Hinck2026-yj]] makes this gap explicit, arguing that the field routinely publishes survey-based inferences without ever testing comprehension, leaving "shared meaning" between researcher and respondent as an unverified assumption rather than a demonstrated fact. This is a deliberately foundational provocation: before asking whether a sample is representative or a recruitment channel is efficient, we should ask whether the instrument itself is doing what we think it does. It sets the terms for everything else in this collection, which largely concerns downstream threats to validity (sample composition, platform effects, measurement technology) while leaving this upstream threat—comprehension—comparatively unaddressed.

## The Shifting Ground of Opt-In Samples

If comprehension is one unexamined assumption, the equivalence of "online samples" is another. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] dismantles the practice of treating opt-in panels as interchangeable, benchmarking nine platforms on response validity, representativeness, and respondent "professionalism." Its central finding—a trade-off whereby demographically-quoted samples (Lucid, Forthright) are more representative but less attentive, partly because of heavier mobile use—gives empirical teeth to the kind of skepticism [[Hinck2026-yj]] raises at the level of individual items: even when a question is well understood, the sample answering it may not resemble the population being described, or may be answering carelessly. Usefully, the paper doesn't just diagnose the problem; it shows that two simple front-end attention checks recover much of the lost validity without meaningfully distorting representativeness—a rare case in this literature where a practical fix is both cheap and well-evidenced. It also delivers a methodologically important caveat for applied researchers: post-stratification weights can restore average treatment effects in unrepresentative samples, but they cannot manufacture the heterogeneous effects that only emerge in demographically rich, representative data. This matters directly for recruitment-innovation work elsewhere in the topic, since any new sampling channel (e.g., Facebook ads) inherits the same representativeness/validity trade-off this paper formalizes.

## Recruiting the Hard-to-Reach: Platform-Based Sampling as an Alternative

Where [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] audits existing opt-in vendors, [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] tests a new acquisition channel altogether: Facebook advertising, aimed at a population—Italian conspiracy-theory sympathizers—that is both niche and reluctant to self-identify through conventional panels. Using Pixel-based conversion tracking and custom-audience exclusion, the study offers a methodologically careful demonstration that Facebook ads can recruit at low cost (€0.46/respondent) and with conversion rates that beat prior benchmarks in this literature. Yet its most important contribution is arguably its epistemic honesty: despite efficient recruitment, the procedure's *effectiveness*—whether "interest" targeting actually surfaces ideologically distinctive respondents—is inconclusive, since the Facebook-recruited sample didn't differ significantly from a general-population CAWI benchmark on conspiracy endorsement. This echoes the representativeness/validity tension in [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]: a channel can be operationally efficient (high conversion, low cost, clean deduplication) while leaving open whether it reaches who researchers think it reaches. Together, these two papers suggest that evaluating a sampling or recruitment strategy requires at least two independent axes—operational efficiency and substantive validity—and that success on one says little about the other.

## New Instruments for Old Measurement Problems

A second cluster turns to computational tools that reshape what counts as a "survey response" in the first place. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] targets the open-ended/closed-ended trade-off that has long constrained survey design: closed items are tractable but shallow, open-ended items are rich but costly to code. By pairing LLMs with paired-comparison scaling, the paper proposes recovering continuous latent measures (ideology, knowledge, emotion) from free text without the labor bottleneck of manual coding—directly relevant to the comprehension concerns in [[Hinck2026-yj]], since open-ended responses are one of the few ways researchers can actually observe how respondents understood a question rather than merely assuming it. The Anthropic report on 81,000 Claude.ai users ([[UnknownUnknown-db]]) is a large-scale instance of this same logic in practice: Claude-powered classifiers infer occupation, career stage, and productivity perceptions from open-ended self-reports, linking qualitative survey data to independently observed usage patterns. Its finding that self-reported displacement concern tracks observed occupational AI exposure is notable methodologically as much as substantively—it is a validity check achieved by triangulating survey self-report against behavioral trace data, a strategy that resonates across several papers in this set.

## Trace Data as Validity Check—and as Its Own Validity Problem

That triangulation strategy is explored most directly, and most skeptically, in the final pair of papers. [[Guess2019-ym]] shows the method's promise: by linking panel survey responses to respondents' actual Facebook sharing histories, the authors move beyond self-reported behavior (notoriously unreliable for stigmatized or rare acts like sharing fake news) to observe it directly, revealing that age—not just ideology—is the most robust predictor of fake-news dissemination. This is precisely the kind of behavioral corroboration that survey-only designs cannot offer, and it demonstrates why marrying trace data to self-report can resolve ambiguities that neither source alone could settle. But [[Ulloa2024-jm]] complicates any assumption that trace data is a clean ground truth. Its comparison of in-situ versus ex-situ web-scraped content shows that the measurement environment itself—not just the oft-cited problem of time delay—introduces substantial, non-randomly distributed error into digital trace records, with cascading effects on downstream classification and substantive inference. Read alongside [[Guess2019-ym]], this is a cautionary complement: behavioral trace data is only as trustworthy as its collection method, and researchers who treat it as an unproblematic benchmark against which to validate self-report risk importing a new, under-examined source of bias in place of the old one.

## Synthesis

Across these seven papers runs a single throughline: survey-based inference rests on a chain of assumptions—that respondents understand the question ([[Hinck2026-yj]]), that the sample answering it resembles the population of interest ([[Stagnaro2025-pz]]), that novel recruitment channels reach who they claim to reach ([[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]]), and, increasingly, that computational tools used to process or corroborate responses (LLM scaling, trace-data linkage) introduce their own interpretable and controllable error ([[DiGiuseppe2025-es]]; [[UnknownUnknown-db]]; [[Guess2019-ym]]; [[Ulloa2024-jm]]). None of these assumptions can be taken for granted, and the collection's overall arc is toward making each one empirically testable rather than methodologically assumed—whether through attention checks, conversion-rate audits, in-situ data capture, or direct comprehension testing.
