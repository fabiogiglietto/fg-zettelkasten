# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The comprehension problem beneath the data-quality problem

The most basic question in survey research — do respondents understand what we are asking them? — is also the most neglected. [[Hinck2026-yj]] makes this explicit: survey-based inference routinely proceeds without any evidence that respondents interpret questions the way researchers intend, leaving a foundational gap in an evidentiary chain that political and social science treats as settled. This is a useful anchor for the rest of the topic, because every subsequent paper in this cluster is, in one way or another, an attempt to either detect or route around failures of shared meaning — whether by filtering out inattentive respondents, choosing better recruitment channels, or replacing closed-ended items with richer open-ended measures whose meaning can be reconstructed after the fact.

## Who answers, and how well: auditing opt-in samples

If comprehension is one half of validity, *who is answering* and *how carefully* is the other. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] provides the most systematic empirical answer among these papers, benchmarking nine opt-in online panels (Lucid, Prolific, MTurk, Forthright, CloudResearch products, etc.) on response validity, representativeness, and respondent "professionalism." Its central finding — a trade-off in which demographically-quoted samples look more representative but show lower attentiveness, disproportionately via mobile respondents — reframes "data quality" as multidimensional rather than a single pass/fail criterion. Crucially, the paper shows that a cheap fix (two front-end attention checks) meaningfully raises validity without eroding representativeness, but that post-stratification weighting cannot rescue heterogeneous treatment effects the way it can rescue main effects. This matters directly for [[Voelkel2026-lc]], a megastudy of climate messaging that depends on large, heterogeneous opt-in samples to detect small effects and partisan moderation; its findings — small, largely non-heterogeneous attitude effects and a stark attitude–behavior gap on donations — are only interpretable against a background assumption (largely unexamined in that paper, but directly interrogated by Stagnaro et al.) that the sample's variation in attention and demographics is not swamping the true heterogeneity the design is built to detect. Read together, the two papers suggest that megastudy-scale designs are as much a solution to sample noise as to statistical power.

## Reaching populations panels can't: platform-based recruitment

Where Stagnaro et al. audit existing panels, [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] asks whether entirely new recruitment infrastructures can extend survey research to populations panels struggle to reach. Using Facebook's ad-targeting and Pixel-based conversion tracking to recruit Italian conspiracy-theory sympathizers, the paper offers a methodologically careful demonstration that ad-based recruitment can be efficient and auditable (a real conversion-rate metric, deduplication via Pixel) — but its comparison against an ITANES probability benchmark leaves *effectiveness* unresolved: the Facebook sample was not reliably more conspiracist than the general population, undercutting the assumption that interest-based targeting reaches ideologically distinctive respondents. This is a cautionary complement to Stagnaro et al.'s broader lesson that a sampling frame's efficiency (cost, speed, control) and its representativeness/validity for a given construct are separable properties that must be demonstrated, not assumed — precisely the standard of evidence [[Hinck2026-yj]] calls for at the level of individual questions.

## Measurement choices as a hidden source of instability

A parallel strand of concern is that even with a "good" sample, researcher decisions about *how to operationalize* constructs can drive substantive conclusions as much as the underlying phenomenon does. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates this with a specification-curve analysis of 504 ways to measure partisan media slant and polarization, showing that while the sign of the media–polarization relationship is stable, its magnitude — and the visibility of asymmetries between conservative and liberal media diets — depends heavily on operationalization choices (audience-based vs. content-based slant scores, bipolar vs. disaggregated polarization measures). This is a different flavor of the validity problem than sample composition: it shows that "data quality" failures can originate downstream, in coding and aggregation decisions, even when the underlying survey responses themselves are unproblematic. It is a natural bridge to the comprehension question raised by [[Hinck2026-yj]], since many of these operationalization choices are themselves attempts to infer a latent construct (media diet, polarization) from responses whose intended meaning was never independently verified.

## New instruments for extracting meaning from unstructured responses

Two papers point toward a possible resolution: using large language models to recover richer, more valid measures from what respondents actually say rather than constraining them to closed-ended categories. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes pairing LLMs with paired-comparison methods to scale open-ended survey text into continuous latent measures of ideology, knowledge, or emotion, explicitly framing this as a corrective to both the impoverishment of closed-ended items and the unscalability of manual open-ended coding. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] offers a large-scale applied instance of this logic outside the traditional survey pipeline: an ~81,000-respondent survey of Claude.ai users in which Claude itself infers occupation, career stage, and displacement concern from free text, linked back to platform usage data. Together these two works suggest an emerging paradigm in which the comprehension and coding problems flagged by [[Hinck2026-yj]] and [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] are addressed not by better instructions or attention checks alone, but by instruments capable of interpreting open, less-constrained respondent language — with the important caveat, visible in the Anthropic study's own limitations (occupation recoverable for only 39% of respondents), that LLM inference introduces its own opaque source of measurement error requiring the same kind of validation Hinck calls for.

## A cautionary parallel from outside self-report

[[Ulloa2024-jm]] does not concern surveys directly, but it belongs in this cluster as a structural analogue: it shows that a widely used substitute for self-report — scraped web-tracking data — suffers from large, systematically (not randomly) distributed measurement error driven by the collection environment itself, not the time lag researchers had worried about. The parallel to the opt-in-sample literature is close: just as Stagnaro et al. show that convenience samples cannot be treated as a homogeneous category, Ulloa et al. show that "digital trace" data cannot be treated as a transparent stand-in for lived exposure, and that the errors introduced are non-random enough to bias substantive conclusions about polarization and news diets — the very outcomes at stake in [[Schemer2026-mh]]. The throughline across this whole topic, then, is a shared insistence that every stage of the online-data pipeline — comprehension, sampling, recruitment, operationalization, and now automated measurement — carries validity assumptions that must be tested empirically rather than taken on faith.
