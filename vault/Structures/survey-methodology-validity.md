# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The comprehension problem at the root

The most fundamental question in this cluster is asked almost provocatively by [[Hinck2026-yj]]: do we have any actual evidence that respondents understand survey questions the way researchers intend? The paper's claim is less an empirical finding than a diagnosis of absence — survey-based inference across the social sciences rests on an unverified assumption of shared meaning between researcher and respondent. This sets the terms for everything else filed here: before asking whether a *sample* is good, or whether a *recruitment channel* is efficient, one has to ask whether the instrument itself is eliciting the construct it claims to measure. Framed this way, [[Hinck2026-yj]] functions as the conceptual anchor of the topic, and the other papers can be read as implicitly supplying (or failing to supply) exactly the kind of evidence it says is missing.

## Who answers, and how well: sample quality as a multidimensional problem

[[Stagnaro2025-pz]] takes the comprehension question and generalizes it to a broader audit of *who* is answering and under what conditions. By benchmarking nine opt-in online samples on response validity, representativeness, and "professionalism," it shows that these properties trade off against each other rather than co-vary: quota-based samples (Lucid, Forthright) are demographically closer to the population but are also where inattentive, speeding, and dishonest responding concentrates, partly tracking mobile-device use. This directly operationalizes the worry in [[Hinck2026-yj]] — a demographically representative sample is no guarantee that respondents are engaging with questions as intended — and offers a partial fix (simple front-end attention checks) that improves validity without much cost to representativeness. Importantly, the paper also shows that weighting can patch main effects but not heterogeneous treatment effects, meaning the choice of sample source has consequences that travel downstream into substantive conclusions, not just into surface-level demographic match.

## Recruitment as a design problem

If sample composition is partly a consequence of *where* respondents are recruited from, [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] pushes the inquiry upstream to the recruitment mechanism itself. Using Facebook's ad infrastructure (Pixel tracking, URL parameters, custom-audience exclusion) to reach a niche, hard-to-survey population — Italian conspiracy-theory sympathizers — the paper demonstrates that platform-based recruitment can be cheap, fast, and well-instrumented for measuring true response rates rather than proxy click-through rates. Yet its central finding complicates the optimism: the Facebook-recruited sample was not reliably more ideologically distinctive than a general-population CAWI benchmark, meaning that interest-based targeting does not guarantee access to the attitudinal population a researcher thinks they are reaching. This is a recruitment-side echo of the representativeness/validity tension in [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] — efficient, low-cost channels for reaching "hard" populations do not automatically solve the deeper problem of knowing who, substantively, has been recruited.

## Rethinking what a survey response even is

A second thread asks whether the closed-ended question format itself is an adequate measurement instrument, pulling the topic toward computational remedies. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] argues that closed-ended items artificially constrain the depth of what can be measured, and proposes pairing LLMs with paired-comparison methods to turn open-ended text into continuous latent scales — an attempt to recover richer signal without the prohibitive cost of manual coding. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], Anthropic's large-scale survey of Claude users, operationalizes a version of this idea at scale: LLM classifiers infer occupation, career stage, and productivity/displacement attitudes directly from free text, then connect these self-reports to independently observed usage patterns. Read alongside [[Hinck2026-yj]], this body of work is double-edged: LLM-based scaling could, in principle, generate the comprehension evidence that traditional closed-ended surveys lack (since open text reveals how respondents actually frame a question), but it also introduces a new, largely unaudited measurement layer — the classifier's own interpretive assumptions — that itself needs validation. Notably, occupation could only be inferred for 39% of Anthropic's respondents, a reminder that LLM-mediated measurement has its own non-random missingness and interpretive slippage, not unlike attention-check failures or API indexing gaps elsewhere in this set.

## The same crisis of inference beyond the survey instrument

Two papers extend this methodological skepticism to data sources adjacent to, but not literally, open-ended survey text. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that web-tracking research measuring "online news consumption" is itself vulnerable to a comprehension-like validity gap: content scraped outside the moment of user interaction diverges substantially (≈34%) from what the user actually saw, with errors concentrated non-randomly in paywalled and interaction-gated categories. [[Rieder2025-ju]] makes a structurally similar point about YouTube's search API, which systematically "forgets" older content and returns inconsistent results across identical queries, undermining any downstream claim about what content population a researcher has actually sampled. Neither paper is about survey respondents, but both are about the same underlying failure mode that animates [[Hinck2026-yj]] and [[Stagnaro2025-pz]]: researchers routinely treat a convenient data-generating process (a scrape, an API call, an opt-in panel) as if it transparently reflected the target construct, without auditing the gap between the two.

## Measurement choices propagate into substantive conclusions

Finally, [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates concretely why all of this matters for inference, not just for description. Running a specification-curve analysis across 504 operationalizations of media partisanship and polarization, the study finds that measurement choices — audience-based versus content-based slant scores, bipolar versus disaggregated polarization indices — swing coefficient magnitudes by nearly sevenfold, and can mask real asymmetries (e.g., between conservative- and liberal-leaning media diets) when researchers default to a single convenient operationalization. This is the natural endpoint of the arc traced by this topic: comprehension gaps ([[Hinck2026-yj]]), sample-source heterogeneity ([[Stagnaro2025-pz]]), recruitment-channel opacity ([[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]]), and instrument/pipeline distortions ([[Ulloa2024-jm]], [[Rieder2025-ju]], and the LLM-based measurement innovations in [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] and [[UnknownUnknown-db]]) are not independent nuisances but compounding sources of researcher degrees of freedom. The through-line is a call for explicit validation at every stage of the survey and digital-trace pipeline — who is asked, how they are reached, what their answers mean, and how those answers are operationalized — rather than treating any single stage as self-evidently valid.
