# Survey Data Quality, Comprehension, and Recruitment

## The question behind the question: do respondents understand us?

The foundational claim organizing this topic is deceptively simple: survey inference assumes shared meaning between researcher and respondent, and that assumption is rarely tested. [[Hinck2026-yj]] makes this gap explicit, arguing that the field has built decades of inference on an unverified premise—that respondents interpret questions as intended—and calls for comprehension testing to become a standard part of survey methodology rather than an afterthought relegated to cognitive interviewing specialists. This is a quietly radical reframing: it suggests that even perfectly representative, perfectly "valid" samples (in the sense of attentiveness and honesty) could still produce invalid inferences if the question-response mapping is broken. [[Schemer2026-mh]] offers an empirical demonstration of a structurally similar problem one level up the analysis pipeline: using specification curve analysis across 504 operationalizations of media slant and polarization, it shows that researcher degrees of freedom in *measurement construction*—not just respondent comprehension—can swing effect magnitudes nearly sevenfold while leaving direction intact. Read together, these two papers bracket the measurement-validity problem from both ends: Hinck interrogates whether respondents understand the stimulus, Schemer interrogates whether researchers' own downstream coding choices are arbitrary enough to manufacture apparent findings. Both argue that "the result" is partly an artifact of unexamined methodological discretion.

## Who answers, and how well: sample quality across platforms

If comprehension is one half of validity, sample composition and response quality is the other, and [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] is the anchor empirical paper here. Its nine-sample comparison operationalizes a trade-off that recurs throughout this literature: demographic-quota samples (Lucid, Forthright) look more representative but show weaker response validity, partly driven by heavier mobile usage, while curated panels (Prolific, Connect) show the reverse pattern. Crucially, the paper shows this isn't a fixed trade-off—two trivial front-end attention checks substantially improve validity without much representativeness cost—but also shows real limits: post-stratification weights recover main effects but not heterogeneous treatment effects, meaning that subgroup-level claims (the kind increasingly central to social science and policy debates) are exactly where sample choice matters most and where quick fixes don't help. This finding dovetails with Hinck's broader worry: a sample can pass every validity check Stagnaro proposes and still misrepresent subgroup effects if researchers over-trust weighting as a cure-all.

[[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] extends the sample-quality question to recruitment strategy itself, testing whether Facebook's ad infrastructure (Pixel tracking, custom audience exclusion, URL parameters) can efficiently and validly reach a hard-to-survey niche population—conspiracy-theory sympathizers in Italy. The efficiency case is clear (sub-€0.50 per respondent, conversion rates beating prior CTR benchmarks), but the effectiveness case is not: the Facebook-recruited sample's conspiracy endorsement didn't differ significantly from a general-population CAWI benchmark, raising the same question Stagnaro poses about demographic quota samples—does a targeting mechanism that looks precise on paper (interest-based ad targeting; demographic quotas) actually deliver the distinctive population it promises? Both papers converge on a methodological lesson: novel recruitment or sampling technology must be validated against external benchmarks, not just assessed on cost and response-rate efficiency.

## New measurement technologies: LLMs as a bridge and a risk

A newer thread concerns whether large language models can rescue the open-ended response—traditionally rich but uncodable at scale—without reintroducing comprehension or validity problems through a different door. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes LLM-paired comparisons as a way to convert free text into continuous latent-trait scales, directly addressing the tension between closed-ended items (easy to code, but constraining what respondents can say) and open-ended items (expressive, but labor-intensive to analyze). This matters for the Hinck critique: open-ended responses are arguably the best available evidence of what respondents actually understood by a question, so LLM-based scaling could become a practical mechanism for generating the comprehension evidence Hinck says is missing — provided the LLM coding itself doesn't introduce new, unvalidated interpretive assumptions.

The Anthropic report on 81,000 Claude.ai users ([[UnknownUnknown-db]]) is a real-world instance of this approach at scale, using Claude-powered classifiers to infer occupation, career stage, and displacement concerns from open-ended survey text. It is useful here less as a labor-economics finding and more as a methodological specimen: occupation could be inferred for only 39% of respondents, a reminder that LLM-based inference from free text, however scalable, carries its own substantial missingness and classification uncertainty—an echo of Hinck's comprehension-evidence problem displaced onto the inference pipeline rather than the question-wording stage. Similarly, [[Fattorini2026-bo]], though primarily a substantive study of Italian AI attitudes, illustrates why comprehension evidence matters domain-by-domain: its finding of a persistent "exposure-understanding gap"—rising AI visibility without corresponding self-assessed knowledge—is itself a population-level manifestation of the comprehension concern Hinck raises about individual survey items, here concerning an entire technology rather than a single question.

## An adjacent cautionary tale: measurement validity beyond surveys

[[Ulloa2024-jm]], on web-scraping distortions in news-consumption measurement, sits slightly outside the survey-instrument core of this topic but reinforces its central moral from an entirely different methodological tradition. Just as Stagnaro shows that opt-in samples are not interchangeable and must be benchmarked empirically, Ulloa shows that scraped "digital trace" data—often treated as a superior, bias-free alternative to self-report—carries its own large, systematically distributed measurement error (dominated by collection environment rather than the time-delay factor prior work emphasized). The shared lesson across this entire structure note is that no data-generation method is self-validating: whether the instrument is a survey question, a Facebook ad, an LLM classifier, or a scraper, researchers must empirically demonstrate—not assume—that the method captures what they intend it to measure.
