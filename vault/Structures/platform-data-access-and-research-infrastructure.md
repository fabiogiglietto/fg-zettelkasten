# Platform Data Access and Research Infrastructure

## Structure Note: Platform Data Access and Research Infrastructure

### The arc: from open APIs to walled gardens to regulated (but contested) access

The papers filed here trace a consistent historical arc that several pieces narrate explicitly and in parallel. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes nearly two decades of platform data policy — prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, "limited options," and academic cooperation — while [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] retells the same story for Twitter/X specifically as a move from "Wild West" to "Golden Age" (Academic API) to post-Musk "walled garden," and Bastos2025-ya offers a eulogy for the suite of APIs that made "Twitter studies" possible. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] supplies the bibliometric backbone for this narrative, showing 25% median annual growth in Twitter-based publications through 2022 followed by stagnation and a 13% decline by 2024 — direct evidence that infrastructural closure translates into lost scholarly capacity. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review generalizes this pattern across social science disciplines: social-media-based empirical research tripled from 2010 but plateaued and declined in 2022–2024, with Twitter/X and Facebook usage dropping specifically after API restrictions, even as researchers begin drifting toward YouTube, TikTok, and donation-based methods. Collectively these pieces establish the empirical stakes of what [[Freelon2024-sc]] and others call the "post-API age."

Into this vacuum steps the EU's Digital Services Act, and a cluster of papers assesses whether Article 40 actually delivers on its promise of researcher access as a legal right rather than platform philanthropy. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frame the DSA optimistically as a genuine, if turbulent, shift in the balance of informational power — arguing that platform resistance is itself evidence the regulation is biting rather than failing. [[Pierri2025-hm]], drawing on an 18-month ERC pilot with DG-CONNECT, is more circumspect, cataloguing misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, and a "circular resource problem" disadvantaging under-funded researchers, and flagging LLMs as a looming regulatory blind spot. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] documents the unevenness concretely: response times from 2 to 100+ days, boilerplate denials, and EU proceedings against X, TikTok, and Meta for breaching Article 40 obligations — producing a bifurcated "data abyss" that excludes Global South and early-career researchers. [[Peters2026-mo]] zooms in on one underexamined dimension of this regulatory process — data *quality* — showing it was nearly absent from official DSA text but pushed into the final Delegated Regulation by academic and NGO advocacy, against platform (especially Meta's) strategic inversion of the argument.

### Auditing the tools themselves

A substantial cluster moves from policy-level assessment to hands-on technical audits of the access mechanisms platforms have actually built. [[Rieder2025-ju]] finds YouTube's Data API search endpoint "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay in retrievability that undermines DSA-era systemic-risk research. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] reaches a parallel verdict on TikTok's Research API, finding persistent limitations despite its 2023 DSA-driven expansion. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds constructively by building TubeStats and TokStats, tools that exploit platforms' ID-space structure to generate genuinely random samples where official APIs cannot, addressing the "denominator problem" that opportunistic, keyword-driven samples systematically distort. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] extend the critique to Meta's evolving infrastructure: Cullen's "seeing like an API" framework shows how CrowdTangle's technical design and governance quietly shaped what researchers could even ask, while Bruns dissects the "clean room" model of the Meta Content Library that succeeded it, arguing that mandating access (per DSA) is meaningless if the mode of access — Jupyter-only, code-skewed, non-longitudinal, cross-platform-incompatible — structurally excludes qualitative researchers and Majority World institutions. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] provides useful pre-history here, showing how even earlier tools like the Facebook Ad Library lacked the granularity needed for meaningful exposure research well before the current crisis. [[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] apply similar audit logics to adjacent DSA transparency obligations — YouTube's publisher-labeling and removal communication, and cross-platform moderator language allocation, respectively — both concluding that current transparency reporting falls short of what independent, systemic-risk research requires. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys this same DSA-era tool landscape at a higher level, and [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes the whole terrain via a "price, proficiency, or permission" taxonomy of 72 access tools, showing production data vastly outnumbers consumption data and platform-permission tools remain inherently ephemeral.

### Distortion, not just closure: methodological hazards of platform-mediated data

Several papers show that even where data access exists, it can mislead. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] finds a hidden algorithmic artifact in Meta's URL Shares Dataset — a 100-share anonymization threshold interacting with feed-ranking changes to produce spuriously similar cross-country trends, a cautionary tale for any comparative or longitudinal study built on platform-curated data. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that web-scraping itself, independent of time delay, introduces systematic (non-random) distortion in news-consumption measurement, arguing for in-situ tracking over ex-situ scraping. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard, a widely used third-party trust-rating infrastructure, finding that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications (rather than continuous scores) can flip substantive findings depending on database snapshot. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] push in the opposite direction methodologically — showing that even "black box" systems (Facebook's News Feed, X's recommender) leave detectable, auditable traces (lagged engagement effects; a linear ideology-encoding direction in embedding space) that argue for the *feasibility* of systemic algorithmic audits under DSA Article 40, if access were adequate. [[Oswald2025-km]] adds a more foundational warning: the production-consumption gap on social media means that even perfect data access risks measuring a skewed, unrepresentative "tip of the iceberg" of public opinion — a problem no API fix alone can solve. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] echo this from the news-sharing literature, critiquing the field's habitual reliance on Twitter/Facebook and unidimensional partisan measures, and calling for multi-platform, multi-level approaches.

### Multi-platform infrastructure as the emerging frontier

As single-platform access collapses or becomes unreliable, researchers increasingly frame the challenge as one of *infrastructure* rather than any single tool. [[Schulte2026-df]] makes this explicit, using the 2025 German election to show that the core bottleneck is not a lack of studies but the absence of reusable, standardized multi-platform observability — parties strategically differ in platform choice, and TikTok's weak cross-modal correlations (text/voice/face) demand new multimodal methods that current infrastructure doesn't support. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] examine election research specifically, the former surveying new DSA-mandated portals for studying online campaigning, the latter showing how, absent such infrastructure, volunteer browser-plugin auditing (in Brazil, 2018) filled the gap — a precursor to today's "platform-independent" methods. [[Votta2025-xz]] extends this to ad-delivery algorithms shaping political ad reach and cost, another domain where opacity persists despite nominal transparency tools. [[Allen2025-ot]] situates LLM-powered browser-extension experiments (reviewing Piccardi et al.) as a genuinely platform-independent alternative methodology, born of necessity as API and collaboration routes close off — directly complementing the toolbuilding orientation of vera.ai/PROMPT-style infrastructure work.

### Industry entanglement and the limits of collaboration as a solution

A final thread interrogates whether *collaborating* with platforms — rather than merely accessing their data — can substitute for independent infrastructure. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers the sharpest verdict via a meta-critique of the Meta2020 partnership: even at its most methodologically ambitious, platform-mediated research suffers from low "temporal validity" (platforms change too fast to replicate) and "poetic validity" problems (inconsistent operationalization of concepts like "echo chamber"), leading him to conclude that proactive regulation, not reactive academic study, is the only viable path. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] generalize this into a systematic critique of industry influence on the field: roughly half of high-profile social media research has disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, concentrated among a small repeatedly-engaged set of scientists who also populate editorial and review roles — pushing "industrial saturation" toward 80% once authors, editors, and reviewers are combined. These pieces draw explicit parallels to tobacco, pharma, and food science, and argue that open-science norms like preregistration are insufficient safeguards when platforms control both data and framing. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines an adjacent dependency — fact-checkers' financial reliance on the very platforms they help police — showing how professional apologia rhetoric manages this tension without resolving it. Together with [[Bechmann2026-dr]]'s call to move beyond narrow causal-effects paradigms toward treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure, and [[Moran2025-qn]]'s account of the destabilization of the trust-and-safety profession itself, this cluster insists that data access debates cannot be separated from questions of institutional independence, funding structures, and whose incentives shape the research agenda.

### Governance beyond the DSA, and the broader threat landscape

Rounding out the topic, [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the DSA within a comparative typology of content-moderation regimes (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation) across the EU and US, showing that "transparency" itself functions as a security-bureaucratic technology rather than a neutral accountability tool — a useful corrective to treating DSA access mandates as self-evidently benign. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] and [[Graham2025-gp]] extend the governance lens to adjacent harms (deepfake fraud, propaganda exploiting "infrastructures of truth") that current data-access and moderation regimes struggle to address, while [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Wang2026-ub]] supply empirical snapshots of platform-level phenomena (TikTok mis/disinformation prevalence; the failed academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration) that themselves depend on — and are constrained by — the very access infrastructures this topic interrogates. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] offers an early, adjacent example of platform-ad-based methodology (Facebook Pixel-based survey recruitment) illustrating how researchers have long had to route around formal API access to reach hard-to-study populations, prefiguring the improvisational toolbuilding ethos now central to the vera.ai/PROMPT program and its peers.
