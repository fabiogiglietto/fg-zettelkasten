# Platform Data Access & Research Governance

## The arc: from open APIs to walled gardens to regulated access

The papers filed here trace a single historical arc in three acts. First came a "Golden Age" of comparatively open platform data — Twitter's REST/Streaming/Academic APIs, Facebook's early data philanthropy — that made possible an entire empirical subfield of "Twitter studies" (Bastos2025-ya) and underwrote roughly two decades of growth in social-media-based social science ([[Yang2026-tq]], [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]]). Second came the "APIcalypse": Cambridge Analytica, the 2023 Twitter/X paywall, Reddit's Pushshift shutdown, and the CrowdTangle closure collapsed this infrastructure almost overnight, a process [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes definitively (prehistory → laissez-faire → authentication → limited options → academic cooperation → post-API) and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] narrates as a move "from the Wild West to the Walled Garden." Third is the still-unsettled present: the EU's Digital Services Act, and specifically Article 40's vetted-researcher provisions, attempts to convert what had been platform philanthropy into a legal entitlement — the pivot this whole topic cluster is organized around.

Quantitative eulogies anchor the empirical stakes of this transition. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] tabulates 33,306 Twitter-based studies and documents a 13% publication decline in 2024 following the API shutdown; [[Yang2026-tq]]'s broader systematic review shows social-media-based social science tripling from 2010 through the early 2020s before plateauing and declining in 2022-2024, with Twitter/X and Facebook usage cratering specifically. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] shows even before the shutdown that platform-provided data (Meta's URL Shares Dataset) carried hidden algorithmic artifacts — a caution that anticipates later concerns about data *quality*, not just data *access*.

## CrowdTangle's afterlife and the clean-room problem

CrowdTangle occupies a special place as the totemic casualty of this transition. [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "Seeing like an API" study of 20 CrowdTangle users shows empirically how the tool's technical design and governance rules shaped what researchers could even conceive of asking, and [[Lukito2026-nb]] catalogues CrowdTangle's August 2024 shutdown as disrupting election research on the very eve of the US election. Its replacement, the Meta Content Library, becomes the paradigm case for [[Bruns2026-pn]]'s critique of "clean room" environments: mandatory data deletion cycles that block longitudinal work, Jupyter-only interfaces that exclude qualitative and non-coding researchers, and isolation between platform datasets that forecloses cross-platform comparison. Together these two papers argue that mandating access (per DSA) is necessary but insufficient — the *manner* of access determines its research utility, a theme echoed in [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]]'s survey of the post-CrowdTangle tooling landscape and in [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats project, built explicitly to supply the representative "denominators" that neither official research APIs nor clean rooms provide.

## Article 40 in practice: promise, friction, and audit

A cluster of papers assesses DSA Article 40 as it meets platform reality, and the researcher's own EU expert role sits squarely inside this conversation. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and the companion Forum piece by [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frame the current turbulence — platform resistance, procedural opacity, inconsistent timelines — not as regulatory failure but as evidence the regulation is finally biting on real asymmetries of power; [[Pierri2025-hm]], drawing on an 18-month ERC-DG CONNECT pilot, catalogues concrete implementation bottlenecks (circular resource problems disadvantaging early-career researchers, platform threats of legal action, a coming wave of first access decisions) and flags large language models as an unaddressed regulatory blind spot. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows via consultation-document analysis that "data quality" — central to whether Article 40 access is actually usable — was almost entirely absent from official EU drafting and had to be pushed in by academic and NGO stakeholders against platform resistance (Meta notably inverted the argument to claim access was futile). [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] documents the resulting bifurcated regime empirically: response times from 2 to over 100 days, boilerplate denials, and formal Commission proceedings against X, TikTok, and Meta for Article 40(12) breaches — precisely the systemic-risk data-access terrain in which the researcher's own expert role is situated.

Platform-specific audits give this abstract friction empirical teeth. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's DSA-mandated Research API riddled with unexplained limitations; [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," with findable results decaying sharply within 20-60 days — a direct threat to any systemic-risk research that relies on retrospective reconstruction of events; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data itself to expose stark linguistic inequities in human moderation staffing across six VLOPs; and [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's election-period source-ranking and removal communications, calling current API and Transparency Database access "insufficient for robust... auditing" and reframing transparency as "observability." [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] both apply this lens to election research specifically, cataloguing the proliferating but unevenly implemented DSA-mandated data portals and the "price, proficiency, or permission" barriers that continue to structure who can use them — echoed empirically in [[Schulte2026-df]]'s account of scraping bottlenecks in studying the 2025 German election across X and TikTok.

## Beyond access: quality, methodology, and the politics of measurement

A parallel strand interrogates what "good" platform data would even mean once access exists. [[McNally2025-dn]] demonstrates that Facebook's News Feed is not the inscrutable "black box" it is often assumed to be — algorithmic interventions leave detectable, lagged fingerprints in engagement data — and explicitly proposes this kind of longitudinal detection as a template for DSA Article 40(4) systemic audits. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] complicates web-scraping-based measurement more generally, showing that ex-situ collection environments (not merely time delay) introduce large, systematically biased distortions in news-consumption research. [[Allen2025-ot]] surveys the spectrum of platform-independent experimental methods (browser extensions, LLM-based rerankers) that have emerged precisely because platform cooperation is now unreliable, situating this innovation against the largest platform-sanctioned experiment on record.

That experiment — the Meta 2020 Facebook/Instagram Election Study — recurs as the field's central cautionary tale. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the sharpest methodological verdict: even at its most ambitious, platform-collaborative research suffers from low "temporal" and "poetic" validity, and platforms change faster than social science can characterize them, which is itself an argument for proactive regulatory disclosure requirements rather than reactive academic study. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] generalize this into a structural critique of industry capture: the latter's bibliometric audit finds roughly half of high-profile social media research in top journals has undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small elite of repeatedly-engaged scholars who also dominate editorial gatekeeping — evidence that voluntary "independence by permission" collaborations (Pierri's term) cannot substitute for regulatory guarantees of access.

## Governance logics and conceptual reframing

Zooming out, [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the DSA within a comparative EU/US legislative landscape, arguing that content-moderation law across jurisdictions converges on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" — transparency, auditing, and reporting as instruments of governance-at-a-distance — which helps explain why Article 40 access unfolds through such procedural, audit-heavy machinery rather than direct mandate. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes for a conceptual reframing to match this new regulatory moment, arguing that DSA-era platform research should move beyond narrow causal-effects designs toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure. [[Ahuja2025-ku]]'s dark-patterns framework, while focused on Article 25 rather than Article 40, exemplifies the same broader move this literature is making: translating abstract regulatory categories (autonomy violation, systemic risk, data quality) into operational, auditable design and access criteria. Read together, these papers suggest the DSA era's central task is not simply winning access but building the shared infrastructure, standards, and independent institutions — the themes uniting [[Schulte2026-df]], [[Bruns2026-pn]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] — needed to make that access durable and genuinely useful, an unresolved marathon rather than a settled achievement.
