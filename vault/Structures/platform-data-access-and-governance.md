# Platform Data Access & Governance

## The arc: from open API to walled garden, and the stakes of that closure

The corpus assembles a coherent historical narrative, even though no single paper tells it end to end. [[Freelon2024-sc]] provides the master periodization — API prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation, and finally a fractured "post-API age" of four official regimes plus unofficial workarounds — and nearly every other paper in this collection can be located somewhere along that timeline. Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] eulogize the Twitter API specifically, the latter quantifying the loss with bibliometric precision: a 25% median annual growth in Twitter-based scholarship through 2022, arrested and then reversed (–13% in 2024) once Enterprise pricing hit $42,000/month. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] generalizes this trajectory into three epochs — "Wild West," a brief "Golden Age" of the Academic API, and the post-Musk "walled garden" — arguing that the pattern (also visible in CrowdTangle's shutdown, Reddit's Pushshift closure, TikTok's restrictions) is structural rather than idiosyncratic to any one platform or owner. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review supplies the aggregate empirical confirmation: the share of social-science papers using social media data tripled over 2010–2022 before plateauing and declining in 2022–2024, with Twitter/X and Facebook — the two most-studied platforms — driving the fall.

Two entangled historical drivers recur across these accounts: the Cambridge Analytica scandal, which triggered the first great closure of Facebook/Instagram APIs ([[Freelon2024-sc]], [[Bruns2026-pn]]), and Musk's 2022 acquisition of Twitter, which dismantled the Academic API and pushed access toward "pay-to-play" ([[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], [[Wang2026-ub]]). [[Wang2026-ub]]'s study of academic Mastodon migration shows the human cost of that second rupture directly: even a coordinated, motivated cohort of scholars failed to sustain a durable exit from Twitter, with retention hinging on niche server communities and cross-server engagement rather than sheer commitment — evidence that platform lock-in outlasts even acute crises of trust.

## The DSA as regulatory answer — and its uneven, contested implementation

Against this backdrop of platform discretion, the EU's Digital Services Act, and Article 40 in particular, emerges as the corpus's central regulatory hope, though the papers disagree sharply about how much has actually changed. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[Pierri2025-hm]] frame Article 40 as a genuine — if fragile — turning point, converting data access from platform philanthropy into a legal entitlement, while cataloguing implementation bottlenecks: misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, and a "circular resource problem" that disadvantages early-career and under-resourced researchers. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] offers the most explicitly normative framing, arguing that the current turbulence — platform stonewalling, procedural friction, geopolitical contestation — is not evidence of regulatory failure but proof the DSA is finally constraining entrenched platform power, and that the correct posture is a "marathon" of sustained enforcement rather than retreat. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] complicate this optimism empirically: response times to Article 40 requests range from days to over a hundred, denials are often boilerplate, and the European Commission has already opened proceedings against X, TikTok, and Meta for non-compliance — with preliminary findings that TikTok and Meta breached their obligations. [[Peters2026-mo]]'s discourse analysis of the Delegated Regulation's drafting shows this contestation playing out at the level of concepts themselves: "data quality" was almost absent from official EU text until academic and NGO stakeholders forced its partial inclusion, while platforms (notably Meta) sometimes inverted researcher arguments to claim access was futile precisely because platform data was low quality.

A cluster of papers audits what the DSA-mandated tools actually deliver in practice, and the verdict is consistently disappointing. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained inconsistencies; [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API to be "forgetful by design," with a severe temporal decay effect that makes historical retrieval unreliable and undermines any claim to support systemic-risk research; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data itself to expose stark disparities in human moderator allocation across languages, with Global South languages receiving as little as 7.5% of English's moderation investment on X. [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this critique to YouTube's election-period content moderation, finding inconsistent application of publisher labels and vague removal communications, and calls for reconceiving transparency as "observability" — attention to the infrastructural conditions that make platform knowledge possible at all. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] push this furthest by examining the *form* of access rather than its mere existence: the Meta Content Library's "clean room" model, inherited from CrowdTangle's closure, structurally privileges quantitative, code-fluent, well-resourced Global North researchers while foreclosing qualitative, cross-platform, and longitudinal work — echoing Cullen's "Seeing like an API" framework, which shows how CrowdTangle's technical design and governance rules actively shaped what researchers could see, ask, and conclude even before its shutdown. [[Lukito2026-nb]] synthesizes this into a "price, proficiency, or permission" typology of access barriers, finding that of 72 election-research data tools surveyed, only six address consumption/demand-side data at all — a profound asymmetry given how much scholarship (rightly, per [[Oswald2025-km]]'s account of the production-consumption gap) depends on knowing who is exposed to content, not just who produces it.

## Platform-mediated research as compromised evidence

A second major thread interrogates not access itself but the epistemic reliability of what access, even when granted, actually yields — and here the collection turns sharply critical of platform-cooperative research designs. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s reading of the Meta2020 partnership is exemplary: despite unprecedented methodological ambition, the studies suffer from low "temporal validity" (findings decay before they can be generalized) and low "poetic validity" (inconsistent operationalization of concepts like "echo chamber"), leading him to conclude that reactive social science is structurally incapable of keeping pace with platforms — necessitating proactive regulation requiring platforms to self-characterize their effects. [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s null-effects study of political ad removal and [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s earlier finding that individual choice suppresses cross-cutting exposure more than Facebook's algorithm are both cited elsewhere in the corpus as cases whose null or reassuring findings must be read against the conditions of their production. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] supply the most systematic version of this suspicion: quantitative audits showing that roughly half of high-profile social media research in top journals has disclosable industry ties, mostly undisclosed, concentrated among a small repeatedly-engaged clique of scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping — yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the literature once authors, editors, and reviewers are combined, and skewing research topically toward user-focused questions (misinformation sharing) rather than platform-scale dynamics. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this as a variant of tobacco/pharma-style industry capture, uniquely acute in platform research because platforms exclusively control the infrastructure needed to study them. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] provides a granular technical instance of the same worry: the 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset produces near-identical cross-country trend artifacts that can masquerade as substantive findings about, e.g., COVID-19 misinformation — a caution about mistaking platform engineering choices for social signal. [[Allen2025-ot]] offers a partial way out, endorsing "platform-independent" experimental methods (browser extensions plus LLM reranking) as a productive middle path once platform cooperation becomes unreliable, while [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats tools operationalize a similar unpermissioned, denominator-providing logic for YouTube and TikTok, explicitly framed as a post-API-age necessity.

## Policy changes as objects of study in themselves

Beyond questions of access, several papers use policy shifts as natural experiments revealing platform power directly. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X's algorithmic feed shows causal, asymmetric, persistent conservative attitude shifts — reconciling earlier Meta null findings by showing that algorithms reshape *whom users follow*, not just what they passively see. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], both built on the Meta Content Library, demonstrate Meta's active, temporally volatile curatorial hand: the former finds Italy's political-content-reduction rollout began roughly ten months before Meta's announced global timeline and produced a 72% reach collapse for MPs (with extremist accounts compensating via posting volume), exposing a transparency deficit even under DSA reporting; the latter shows partisan and quality-based reach effects fluctuating sharply around known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures. [[McNally2025-dn]] complements this with a longitudinal audit of Facebook's News Feed treatment of Guardian content, arguing algorithmic interventions leave detectable, laggy statistical traces that make the "black box" framing untenable and bolster the case for DSA-based systemic audits. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] examines Meta's 2025 abandonment of third-party fact-checking, finding the "censorship" narrative empirically thin (removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases) while documenting fact-checkers' anxious pivot toward defending their platform partnerships — a defensiveness that [[Farkas2026-lr]] analyzes rhetorically as an apologia genre negotiating dependence on the very companies fact-checkers must appear independent from. [[Ahuja2025-ku]], [[Bouchaud2026-lr]], and [[Votta2025-xz]] extend policy-facing scrutiny to design and algorithmic mechanics (dark patterns under Article 25, inadvertent ideological profiling in X's recommender embeddings, ad-delivery cost/reach dynamics), each suggesting regulatory frameworks must reach beneath stated policy into architecture itself.

## Democratic stakes and the shape of a response

Several pieces step back to state the normative stakes explicitly. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Moran2025-qn]] frame platform retrenchment — including the collapse of trust-and-safety infrastructure — as directly implicated in democratic backsliding. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts converges on platform governance and journalism ecosystems as the top-rated intervention priorities, above individual-level literacy fixes, echoing the structural (rather than behavioral) diagnosis running through this whole topic. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes furthest conceptually, urging researchers to abandon narrow causal-effects paradigms in favor of treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure — a framing [[Crosset2026-mq]] complements from political theory, showing how EU and US legislation converges on "security bureaucratisation" (transparency, auditing, reporting) as the mechanism through which states now govern platforms as quasi-public infrastructures, even while enacting three distinct circulation regimes (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation). [[Helmond2026-ll]] closes the loop by suggesting platformization itself is entering a new AI-centered phase — the retirement of the Like button and the shift to space-based AI infrastructure signal that the very object of governance is mutating again, likely outrunning whatever accountability regime the DSA era manages to construct. [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] ground these debates in concrete election-research practice, arguing that multi-platform observability must be built as reusable infrastructure rather than assembled ad hoc for each election cycle — a practical coda to the collection's overarching argument: that data access, once a matter of convenience, has become a matter of democratic accountability, and that neither market discretion nor voluntary platform goodwill can be trusted to sustain it.
