# Computational Social Science Methods & Research Ethics

## Foundations: Social Media as Object and Instrument of Social Research

The corpus's deep background is the moment when behavioral traces from platforms became a primary evidentiary base for social science. [[Barbera2015-je]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]] exemplify this founding move: following networks and retweet behavior are treated as costly signals from which latent ideology can be scaled, producing claims about echo chambers, elite-mass polarization, and asymmetric cross-ideological engagement that still anchor debates decades later. [[Bail2018-fk]]'s bot-exposure field experiment pushed this instrumental use further, showing that the normative hope encoded in such designs—that cross-cutting exposure depolarizes—can backfire, while [[Lazer2018-mm]] situated the resulting "fake news" panic as a problem requiring interdisciplinary, platform-collaborative science. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] complicates the conceptual ground beneath all of this by arguing that "fake news" research has mis-located its object: intent-centered typologies of creators cannot capture how falsity actually cycles through propagators in a hybrid media system. Together these pieces establish both the promise of social-media-as-data and the conceptual fragility already visible in its earliest applications.

## The Post-API Age: Rise and Fall of Platform Data Access

A central narrative arc running through the collection is the rise and collapse of open API access. [[Freelon2018-ao]] coins the "post-API age" at the moment Facebook's Pages API closed, arguing that API-dependent training is structurally fragile and that scraping and legal literacy must become core competencies. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] extend this into fuller periodizations—moving from laissez-faire "Wild West" access through a brief "Golden Age" of academic APIs to today's commercialized, walled-garden regimes—while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya document the Twitter case concretely: a scientometric "eulogy" showing a 13% drop in Twitter-based publications after the 2023 paywall, and a retrospective on how "Twitter studies" became a subfield now facing extinction. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review of 2010–2024 social science publishing corroborates this at the discipline level, showing Twitter/X and Facebook dominance plateauing and declining exactly as API restrictions bite, with research only slowly reorienting toward YouTube, TikTok, and donated data. [[Wang2026-ub]] supplies a vivid coda: even a highly motivated cohort of academic early adopters failed to sustain a migration to Mastodon, showing that platform collapse does not simply displace a research community elsewhere—it can disperse and attrit it.

## Regulatory Repair: The Digital Services Act and Its Discontents

A second arc tracks the EU's attempt to re-found data access as a legal right rather than platform philanthropy. [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] frame DSA Article 40 as a milestone shadowed by resource asymmetries, slow vetting, and platform obstruction, while [[Peters2026-mo]] shows empirically that "data quality" was nearly absent from official DSA texts and had to be pushed in by academic and NGO stakeholders against platform resistance—evidence that even technical standards are politically negotiated. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] extend the critique to the lived experience of access: clean-room environments and CrowdTangle's now-defunct API are shown to embed a "seeing like an API" politics that privileges quantitative, code-based, Global-North research while excluding qualitative and cross-platform work. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] turns DSA-mandated transparency data itself into a research instrument, revealing systematic underinvestment in moderation for Global South languages—showing both the promise of regulatory transparency and how much further it needs to go. [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s audit of YouTube's search API documents a parallel, non-EU-specific infrastructural failure—a "forgetful by design" endpoint whose temporal decay undermines DSA systemic-risk research—while [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds pragmatically by building independent, unpermissioned infrastructure (TubeStats, TokStats) to supply the representative denominators platforms refuse to guarantee.

## Industry Influence and the Politics of Evidence

Running parallel to the infrastructural story is a sustained critique of who controls the evidence base once access exists. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document, with tobacco- and pharma-industry parallels, how undisclosed funding, collaboration, and editorial ties concentrate industry influence in high-profile platform research, biasing topics toward user-level phenomena (misinformation sharing) and away from platform-level dynamics. [[Heiss2026-qv]] generalizes this into a structural argument: because platforms uniquely control the data needed to study them, academic–industry entanglement is harder to escape here than in other contested-science domains. [[Munger2025-cz]] supplies the paradigmatic case study, arguing that the Meta2020 partnership—despite its methodological ambition—produced narrow, quickly decaying estimates and inconsistent operationalizations, exposing the limits of one-off collaborative science on infrastructures that change faster than publication cycles. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]] show how this influence can be invisible and purely technical: Meta's 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in the Social Science One URL dataset systematically inflates fake-news prevalence estimates and produces spurious cross-country convergence, demonstrating that privacy-engineering choices are themselves a site of epistemic distortion.

## The Risks and Responsibilities of Studying Platforms

A smaller but pointed cluster addresses what it costs individuals to do this work and how science itself should be organized in response. [[Park2026-tr]] offers the first systematic account of researchers' lived experience of legal risk under overbroad computer-crime and IP law, documenting chilling effects, vulnerability "stockpiling," and the protective but unevenly available role of personal legal counsel. [[Moran2025-qn]] shows the mirror-image institutional erosion on the platform side, as the trust-and-safety profession that researchers often depend on for moderation data and collaboration is itself destabilized by layoffs and political pressure. [[Matias2025-px]] proposes a constructive corrective, arguing that public and lived-experience involvement is not a political concession but a way of improving the rigor of AI and platform evaluation at the stages of equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation. [[Balluff2026-if]] closes this loop with a call for reflexivity closer to home, cataloguing the epistemic, environmental, and ethical trade-offs of unreflective LLM adoption in communication research itself.

## Measurement Matters: Operationalization, Specification, and the Fragility of Findings

A large share of the corpus is a sustained argument that disagreements in computational social science often trace back to measurement choices rather than to the underlying social reality. [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis of partisan media slant and polarization measures shows that methodological choice drives the magnitude (though not the direction) of findings about media effects. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits the NewsGuard database underlying much misinformation research, finding that continuous scores are reasonably stable but that binary thresholds can flip substantive conclusions. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] demonstrates that ex-situ web scraping—not time delay—is the dominant source of distortion in news-exposure measurement, with errors concentrated non-randomly across content categories. [[Hartmann2025-px]] and [[van-Eck2026-xg]] perform structurally similar diagnoses for adjacent literatures: echo-chamber research disagreements stem from heterogeneous conceptualizations and operationalizations, while depolarization recommendations are shown to be systematically disconnected from the evidence scholars themselves produce, resting instead on implicit deliberative-democratic ideals. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] extends this critique to coordinated-behavior research, proposing a unifying framework (actors, synergic actions, intent) to replace the fragmented, platform-contingent definitions currently in use, and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] show how the same problem plays out for engagement signals (Facebook Reactions) and for news-sharing ideology measures, both calling for richer, multi-level operationalizations. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] generalize the diagnosis to the field's conceptual and geographic scope, arguing respectively that single analogies (virus, warfare, pollution) cannot capture misinformation's multilevel complexity and that polarization scholarship is fragmented across disciplines and skewed toward Western contexts. [[Oswald2025-km]] adds a further wrinkle: because a small minority of highly active users produce most visible content, the very traces researchers and citizens rely on may misrepresent the silent majority, compounding measurement problems with a representativeness problem baked into the platforms themselves.

## New Computational Toolkits: Embeddings, LLMs, and Their Hazards

A forward-looking cluster evaluates the new generation of text- and vision-based tools against this backdrop of measurement skepticism. [[Fan2025-ut]] proposes linear concept erasure (LEACE) as a low-cost fix for a specific, well-defined problem—confounded document embeddings—showing substantial gains in multilingual and multi-source clustering without degrading out-of-distribution performance. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends embedding-based clustering to images, arguing that vision-language models can recover connotative, culturally embedded meaning that CNN pipelines miss, with the added benefit of TF-IDF interpretability. [[Brown2025-jk]] interrogates LLM-based annotation directly, finding that item difficulty (human label entropy) dwarfs demographic bias as a predictor of LLM-human disagreement, a more optimistic but still conditional verdict on LLM annotation validity. [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] push similar hybrid logics into qualitative and framing analysis, combining topic modeling, network methods, and LLM-assisted labeling under human oversight to preserve interpretive depth at scale, while [[Alizadeh2026-es]] tests the frontier of automation itself, showing that coding agents can now reproduce a striking share of published computational findings—though with trade-offs around sycophantic specification search when prompted to confirm results—and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] reports a parallel agentic system for automating peer review verification, flagging similar concerns about scale outpacing scrutiny. [[Fan2026-af]] and [[Bruns2025-fz]] propose complementary methodological reorientations for digital trace data broadly: treating user activity as timestamped sequences rather than aggregated cross-sections, and mapping multimodal interactions via embeddings rather than conventional "hairball" network visualizations. [[Gardam2025-er]] rounds out this cluster by arguing that image-centric platforms like Instagram demand visual-first rather than text-centric discourse methods, a methodological point that dovetails directly with [[Arminio2025-tw]]'s case for connotative image clustering.

## Recruiting, Sampling, and Participating: Alternative Routes to Social Data

A final set of papers addresses how researchers obtain people and content when platform infrastructures are unreliable or when trace data alone cannot answer the question. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] tackle the survey-recruitment side: the former develops and validates a Facebook ad-based procedure for reaching niche, stigmatized populations using Pixel-based conversion tracking, while the latter offers a systematic comparison of nine opt-in online panels, showing a representativeness–validity trade-off that attention checks can partially resolve without harming sample quality. [[Wan2026-ai]] and [[Eady2025-vm]] use linked survey and behavioral data to extend ideology- and efficacy-based measurement into algorithmic feeds and news-sharing networks, continuing the Barberá-style tradition of inferring latent constructs from platform behavior but applying it to new objects (Google Discover, URL-sharing ideology). [[Kim2026-wg]] shows the stakes of direct exposure measurement done well, using a real-time ad-tracking tool to document geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising and its turnout effects—a case where methodological rigor in measuring individual exposure overturns prior null findings based on indirect proxies. Finally, [[Graham2026-fb]] and [[Volpe2026-um]] illustrate the substantive, often ethnographic end of this spectrum: mixed-methods and digital-ethnographic approaches to Reddit "botting" and Instagram/TikTok micro-influencing that destabilize clean analytic categories (human/bot, authentic/branded), reminding the broader methodological literature that not all computational social science reduces to trace-data modeling—qualitative and participatory approaches remain essential complements to the infrastructural and measurement debates that dominate the rest of this collection.
