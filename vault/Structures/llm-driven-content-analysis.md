# LLMs in Computational Content Analysis

## The methodological core: what LLMs can and cannot do for content analysis

At the centre of this collection lies a cluster of papers that directly interrogate the reliability of LLMs as classifiers, annotators, and scalers of political text. [[Bailard2024-pj]] demonstrates a mature, theory-driven use of fine-tuned transformers (DeBERTa) to classify collective-action frames at scale, showing that careful supervised classification can support genuinely novel causal claims about the online–offline nexus of extremist mobilisation. This sits at one methodological pole—fine-tuning a smaller, task-specific model—contrasted by [[Meher2025-qb]], which shows that QLoRA-based fine-tuning of an open Llama model can achieve strong multi-label conflict-event classification on consumer hardware, arguing that efficient adaptation, not raw scale, is what matters for political science applications. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] pushes in yet another direction, showing that simply prompting instruction-tuned LLMs to place texts on ideological scales and averaging many responses yields estimates that correlate strongly with established benchmarks—an appealingly simple alternative to both supervised scaling (Wordscores/Wordfish) and fine-tuning pipelines.

Running alongside these success stories is a strand of sober, sometimes critical, validation work. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] offers a granular reflection on what it actually takes to validate an "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline—fine-tuned classification, embedding-based clustering, and LLM-generated cluster labels—arguing that each stage needs its own tailored validation and that even skilled human coders, not crowdworkers, are needed once LLMs begin outperforming naive annotators. [[Larsson2026-ro]] provides a concrete instance of this hybrid approach (GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment classification validated by humans) applied longitudinally to non-English political Facebook data, showing the method's practical viability while modelling how such validation should be reported. [[Brown2025-jk]] complicates the annotation-bias narrative that motivates much of this validation concern: across four contentious datasets, demographic bias in LLM labels is real but small and dataset-specific, while item difficulty (human label entropy) dominates as a predictor of LLM-human agreement—suggesting that fairness audits of LLM annotation should centre on task difficulty rather than model or prompt choice alone. [[Paci2025-ag]] and [[DeVerna2025-dl]] push the validation question toward the limits of LLM competence: Italian-language pragmatic inference (implicature, presupposition) remains poor even with chain-of-thought prompting, and even reasoning- and search-augmented LLMs cannot reliably fact-check political claims without curated retrieval context—both papers converging on the claim that capability gains alone will not solve these tasks; what is missing is either pragmatic grounding or curated evidence.

## Embeddings, clustering, and the search for coherent semantic structure

A second, tightly linked thread concerns embedding models and clustering as measurement tools. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] directly compares OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against the Italian BERT-family UmBERTo for clustering political news, finding the LLM-based embedding consistently produces more coherent clusters—extending English-language findings about LLM embeddings' superiority to a non-English setting and feeding directly into the pipeline validated in [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]. [[Fan2025-ut]] steps back to interrogate a structural weakness of embeddings generally: they encode confounders like document source or language that bias similarity-based clustering and retrieval; the paper shows that linear concept erasure (LEACE) can strip this signal cheaply without degrading out-of-distribution performance, offering a general-purpose fix that complements model-choice comparisons like Giglietto's. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the clustering conversation to the visual domain, arguing that vision-LLMs should be used to translate images into connotative textual descriptions before embedding and clustering, since CNN pipelines capture only denotative similarity and lack interpretability—directly paralleling the embedding-model-choice logic of the textual clustering papers but for multimodal content. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] gesture toward related unsupervised or multi-modal framing-detection pipelines, though with less empirical detail, while [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes "practice mapping"—embedding network actions themselves—as a way of representing multimodal social media interaction beyond conventional network visualization. Together these papers sketch an emerging embedding-methods literature: which embedding model to use, how to clean it of confounds, and how to extend clustering logics from text to images and interaction traces.

## Applications: measuring discourse, narrative, and polarization

Several papers apply these LLM-based pipelines to substantive questions about political discourse and polarization, each pushing the conceptual vocabulary of "frame," "narrative," or "position" further than older text-as-data tools allowed. [[Elfes2026-jb]] uses an LLM to operationalise Greimas's actantial model at scale, introducing "narrative polarisation" as a construct distinct from opinion- or interaction-based polarisation, and finding a striking divergence between highly polarised video content and more harmonised comment discourse—an empirical nuance invisible to standard sentiment or topic-based polarization metrics. [[Waight2025-al]] tackles a closely related measurement problem—narrative similarity across languages and sources—arguing that lexical overlap, topic modeling, and relational extraction all conflate distinct estimands, and building an LLM-based claim-distillation pipeline that alone detects the diffusion of Russian "biolabs" narratives into mainstream U.S. outlets, a phenomenon invisible to exact-text-reuse methods. Both papers exemplify a broader lesson echoed throughout the topic: estimand-estimator misalignment (per Grimmer and King, invoked explicitly in [[Waight2025-al]]) is a recurring failure mode that LLM-based measurement can address, but only with careful validation against gold-standard cases.

[[Lee2026-je]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] extend this application arc to the inference of latent political traits rather than manifest content. Lee et al. show LLMs can infer political alignment from nonpolitical text via subtle cultural cues, outperforming supervised baselines and raising serious privacy concerns; Bouchaud et al. show, from an entirely different angle, that platform recommender embeddings inadvertently encode a near-perfect linear ideology dimension—both papers converge on the finding that political signal is diffusely embedded in seemingly nonpolitical linguistic and behavioural data, and that this embedded signal is both a measurement opportunity and a normative hazard (profiling, microtargeting). [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] rounds out this concern with direct experimental evidence that LLM persuasiveness is driven by information density and post-training choices rather than personalization, and that increasing persuasiveness trades off against factual accuracy—a finding with direct implications for how LLM-generated content might reshape the very political discourse that computational content analysis studies.

## Infrastructure, bias, and reflexive critique

A final set of papers steps back from specific pipelines to ask what kind of scientific infrastructure and epistemic posture LLM-based content analysis requires. [[Balluff2026-if]] offers the most direct methodological critique in the set, cataloguing epistemic, environmental, and ethical costs of reflexive LLM adoption and urging a "least resource-intensive method sufficient for the task" ethic—directly relevant to the model-comparison findings in [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Meher2025-qb]], which show that smaller, well-adapted models often suffice. [[Waight2026-ts]] complicates the notion of LLMs as neutral analytical instruments by showing that state media control is itself embedded in training data and shapes model outputs—a finding that should give pause to anyone using commercial LLMs as if they were unbiased coders of political content, tying back to [[DeVerna2025-dl]]'s finding of ideological skew in LLM-cited sources. [[Rieder2025-ju]] and [[Zheng2026-bi]] address a parallel infrastructural concern from the data-collection side: platform APIs (YouTube search, and the absence of representative sampling on YouTube/TikTok) are unreliable or systematically biased, meaning that even before LLMs enter a pipeline, the underlying corpora researchers analyze may be non-representative or non-replicable—a foundational validity threat that compounds whatever uncertainty LLM-based classification introduces downstream. [[Allen2025-ot]] extends this infrastructural argument to experimental methodology, framing LLM-powered browser-extension experiments as a necessary adaptation to closing platform APIs.

Two further papers gesture at where this field is heading. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the broader landscape of LLM-based annotation and synthesis, providing a taxonomy that contextualises the more specific validation and application papers above, while [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] extend the "LLMs auditing science" theme one level up the meta-hierarchy—testing whether AI coding agents can reproduce social-science findings and whether agentic review tools can catch errors in scientific manuscripts, respectively—both surfacing the same sycophancy and validity concerns (confirmatory bias, hallucination, metadata leakage) that recur in the annotation-bias literature. [[Minici2024-tf]] and [[Goldberg2026-eb]] point outward from measurement toward application and governance: graph-foundation-model detection of coordinated information operations, and a broader normative vision of how LLM-enabled deliberation technologies might reshape digital public squares. Read together, the thirty papers trace an arc from "can LLMs classify/cluster political text reliably" through "what biases and infrastructural limits condition that reliability" to "what happens when LLM-shaped discourse itself becomes the object, and co-author, of political communication."
