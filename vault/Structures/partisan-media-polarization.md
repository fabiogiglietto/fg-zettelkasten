# Partisan Media & Political Polarization

## The arc of inquiry: from echo chambers to algorithmic curation to institutional capture

The papers filed here trace a shift in how partisan media effects have been studied: from early concerns about selective exposure and "echo chambers" in user choice, through a wave of large-scale platform-collaborative experiments that complicated that story, to a current generation of work locating polarization in algorithmic governance, elite strategy, and cross-platform institutional dynamics. Read together, they form something like a thesis-antithesis-synthesis arc.

### The echo chamber thesis and its early complications

The foundational claim—that homophilous networks and selective exposure segregate partisans into mutually isolated information worlds—is established by [[Barbera2015-je]]'s ideal-point estimation method and [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]]'s percolation model of homogeneous cascades. But even within this founding generation, the picture was never simple. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] showed that friend networks on Facebook contain substantial cross-cutting ties, with individual click choices suppressing diverse exposure more than the News Feed algorithm itself. [[Flaxman2016-lm]] found modest, bidirectional effects of search and social media on segregation. [[Barbera2015-fw]] demonstrated that polarization is topic- and time-dependent, with nonpolitical events producing genuine "national conversation," and crucially identified an *ideological asymmetry*: conservatives engage in less cross-cutting communication than liberals. [[Messing2014-jc]] offered a countervailing mechanism—social endorsement cues can override partisan source cues in selection decisions. This early literature set up two recurring tensions that the rest of the corpus keeps returning to: asymmetry (is polarization symmetric across left/right, or driven disproportionately by one side?) and attribution (is the fragmentation caused by algorithms, by user choice, or by the entangled interaction of both?).

### The null-results turn: platform experiments and their limits

A pivotal cluster of papers—largely emerging from the 2020 Facebook/Instagram election studies—pushes back hard against folk theories of algorithmic radicalization. [[Guess2023-ur]] found that replacing the algorithmic feed with a chronological one altered engagement and content mix dramatically but produced no detectable change in polarization, knowledge, or participation. [[Guess2023-ai]] found the same null pattern for removing reshares, despite reshares driving much of the political-news and misinformation exposure. [[Nyhan2023-gb]] showed like-minded content is prevalent but reducing it by a third moved no attitudinal needle. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] complicates this reassurance from within the same data ecosystem, finding that segregation increases sharply along the "funnel of engagement" and is markedly asymmetric—conservative audiences occupy a uniquely homogeneous corner where most fact-checked misinformation concentrates, echoing [[Benkler2018-lw]]'s earlier argument that the crisis is institutional and asymmetric rather than a generic technological pathology, and [[Yang2023-cg]]'s finding of a 5-8x partisan asymmetry in image-based misinformation. [[Bail2018-fk]]'s field experiment adds a further wrinkle: exposure to opposing views can *backfire*, polarizing Republicans further—undermining any simple remedy based on cross-cutting exposure, a worry later formalized by [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s review showing depolarization recommendations are rarely evidence-based.

More recent work reopens the algorithmic question with sharper causal designs. Gauthier2025—properly [[Gauthier2026-iq]]—finds that X's "For You" algorithm *does* shift attitudes rightward, resolving the apparent contradiction with the Meta null results by identifying a persistence mechanism: the algorithm reshapes who users *follow*, an effect that survives even after the algorithm is switched off. Efstdoulis—[[Efstratiou2025-gs]]—shows that apparent right-leaning algorithmic amplification on Twitter is substantially explained by agitating content and proximity to Musk rather than ideology per se, while documenting a new centralizing force: the platform owner himself. [[Brady2026-ln]] reframes the mechanism again, proposing that engagement-based feeds distort perceived social *norms* about political discourse rather than attitudes directly, and shows a "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this. Together these papers suggest the null results of 2023 may reflect limitations of the chronological-feed manipulation specifically, not of algorithmic curation in general.

### Measurement itself as a site of contestation

A significant methodological strand interrogates how partisan slant and polarization are operationalized, arguing that measurement choices are not neutral. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies argues that disagreement in the field stems largely from heterogeneous operationalizations rather than divergent realities—a diagnosis [[Schemer2026-mh]] substantiates empirically via specification-curve analysis, showing that partisan-media-polarization associations vary enormously in magnitude (though not direction) depending on how media slant is scored. [[Green2025-ap]] extends this critique to the object of measurement itself: domain-level partisan audience scores mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, especially for "moderate" outlets, because networked curation unbundles and rebundles individual stories rather than whole sources—a point Gaisbauer2025-px (filed as Gaisbauer2025-px)... rather, Gaisbauer2025-px is cited elsewhere; the relevant methodological companion here is Gaisbauer2025-px. [[Eady2025-vm]] offers a unifying measurement advance, placing media, politicians, and ordinary users on a common ideological scale via URL-sharing behavior, while [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent ideology estimation to video content via Reddit sharing patterns. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] shows that the very sharing-to-viewership relationship is nonlinear and shaped by active, time-varying platform governance (the 2020 "break the glass" interventions)—meaning Facebook is not a neutral conduit whose curation can be treated as a stable background condition, a point reinforced longitudinally by [[McNally2025-dn]]'s detection of algorithmic ranking effects via time-series anomaly analysis, and by [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]'s finding that Meta's political-content-reduction policy suppressed mainstream politicians' reach while extremist accounts compensated through sheer volume—exposing both asymmetric effects and transparency deficits in platform self-reporting.

### Elite strategy, institutional capture, and the "logic of connective faction"

Running parallel to the platform-centric literature is an institutionalist strand asking how elites, parties, and legacy media interact with partisan digital ecosystems. [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]] furnish the foundational "hybrid media system" framework—old and new media logics interacting through adaptation rather than displacement—which [[Bennett2025-xs]] updates explicitly to explain democratic backsliding, arguing that "digital surrogate organizations" pull conservative parties toward illiberalism in ways that purely cognitive/disinformation accounts miss. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] operationalizes this via the "logic of connective faction," showing how Republican adoption of Critical Race Theory as a talking point tracked ideological extremity and reward from right-wing digital networks. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] offers comparative confirmation across 26 countries: neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation sharing, but their *conjunction*—radical-right populism—does, while [[Darius2026-xl]]'s cross-platform EU study shows PRR parties enjoy disproportionate audience support (especially on TikTok) independent of posting volume, suggesting resonance rather than mere supply. [[Balluff2026-bv]] documents a concrete mechanism of capture—government-advertising-for-coverage exchange in Austria—while [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates assumptions about alternative media's independence by showing substantial entity overlap with legacy outlets. [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Heft2021-ky]] both trace transnational right-wing media infrastructure (Sky News Australia, European/US hyperlink networks), with Heft's counterintuitive finding that these networks' shared reference points are dominated by legacy media, undercutting the "insulated sphere" thesis. [[Iris2026-pg]]'s cross-national study of the 2024 EP elections shows structural, platform-general overrepresentation of Radical Right actors in news coverage itself—suggesting normalization operates upstream of social media curation, in traditional journalistic visibility.

### Affect, rhetoric, and the discursive construction of polarization

A further cluster turns from exposure/structure to the affective and rhetorical textures of partisan media. [[Iyengar2019-jj]] anchors this with the foundational distinction between affective and ideological polarization, showing partisan animus functions as social identity independent of issue extremity—a distinction [[van-Eck2026-xg]] shows is underexploited in depolarization scholarship. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] extends this to misinformation sharing specifically, finding partisan (especially out-party) animus, not ignorance, drives fake-news sharing on Twitter, a finding resonant with [[Vosoughi2018-at]]'s landmark demonstration that falsehood diffuses farther and faster than truth, especially in the political domain, driven by human novelty-seeking rather than bots. Several papers examine the *performative* and *weaponized* use of polarization-adjacent vocabulary: [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] both show "fake news" accusations function as rhetorical weapons of delegitimization rather than descriptions of actual misinformation, while [[Elfes2026-jb]] introduces "narrative polarisation" to show that contestation over actor roles (perpetrator/victim) persists even where surface opinion appears to converge. The affective-affordance literature—[[Kalsnes2025-zb]], [[Anwar2024-34dba628]], [[Marino2026-he]]—shows how platform-specific emotional reactions (Facebook's Love/Angry buttons) are differentially mobilized by populist actors and fluctuate unpredictably over time rather than reflecting stable echo-chamber dynamics, directly complicating [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s Reddit finding of "sedimented" polarization as a structural precondition independent of actual discursive similarity.

### Partisan asymmetry as the recurring empirical crux

Across methods and platforms, one question recurs insistently: is polarization symmetric? The evidence assembled here leans toward documenting right-leaning asymmetries in misinformation and visibility—[[Renault2025-uh]] (Republicans flagged 2.3x more often even under Community Notes' cross-partisan bridging design), [[Bouchaud2026-np]] (showing this bridging design structurally undermoderates polarizing content), [[Yang2023-cg]], [[Guess2021-ym]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]], and [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s striking finding that Brazil's X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet." Yet [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]]'s study of UK scientific sourcing show that perceived and constructed asymmetries can be driven by identity-protective cognition and selective media sourcing rather than objective differences in content—and [[Much2026-gu]]'s podcast study introduces an orthogonal axis of sorting (gendered media spaces) that produces ideological exposure without ideological selection as such.

### Open frontiers: LLMs, methodology, and the call for globalization

The newest layer of this literature turns reflexively on method and scope. [[Lee2026-je]] and [[Paci2025-ag]] probe what large language models can and cannot infer or interpret in partisan discourse—one showing surprising success at covert ideology inference, the other showing persistent failure at pragmatic/implicit meaning, bookending the promise and limits of LLM-based computational political communication exemplified methodologically by [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]]'s unsupervised framing pipelines. Meanwhile [[Falkenberg2026-ka]], [[Oswald2025-km]], and [[Schulz2026-ts]] converge on a structural critique: polarization research is geographically skewed, built on unrepresentative vocal minorities, and subject to "false polarization" via self-selected posting—concerns given comparative texture by [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]]'s European analyses and [[Lukito2026-il]]'s and [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]]'s cross-platform right-wing curation studies. [[Esau2025-tf]] closes the normative loop opened by [[van-Eck2026-xg]], proposing deliberative reciprocity as an antidote—though the corpus as a whole, from [[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]]'s early skepticism about Facebook Likes as electoral signal through [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]'s diagnosis of big data's disruption of political communication scholarship, suggests the central lesson is methodological humility: what counts as "polarization" depends enormously on where, how, and at what level of the information funnel one chooses to look.
