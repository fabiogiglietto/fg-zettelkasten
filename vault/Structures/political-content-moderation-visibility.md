# Political Content Moderation & Visibility

## The shape of the field: from removal to reduction

The organizing move across this literature is a shift in what counts as content moderation. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] supplies the conceptual hinge: platforms increasingly govern speech not by deleting it but by throttling its reach, a mode of control that is harder to see, harder to contest, and harder to study than outright bans. Nearly every paper in this collection can be read as elaborating, testing, or complicating that claim in a specific empirical register — feed algorithms, ad delivery systems, crowdsourced note-ranking, government advertising, deplatforming, and explicit "political content reduction" policies all turn out to be variations on the same underlying mechanism of differential visibility.

## Meta's political content reduction and its consequences

The empirical core of the topic is the discovery and measurement of Meta's multi-year effort to suppress political content in feeds. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] provides the most granular causal evidence: Italian MPs' Facebook reach fell 72% following a policy rollout that began roughly ten months before Meta's own announced timeline, with extremist accounts compensating through sheer posting volume rather than per-post reach — and with the policy itself absent from Meta's DSA transparency filings. This transparency gap is precisely the pathology that [[McNally2025-dn]] sets out to falsify empirically at the level of the News Feed generally: using Guardian engagement data across a decade, it shows that "black box" algorithmic change leaves detectable statistical fingerprints (lagged, but real), making the case that reduction is auditable if researchers have the right longitudinal tools. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends this logic to viewership at scale, showing that partisan-audience content is systematically penalized in views relative to shares while journalistically rigorous content is rewarded, and — crucially — that these penalties and rewards intensify around known governance flashpoints like the 2020 "break the glass" measures, evidence that curation is actively managed rather than a stable structural artifact. Together these three papers reframe "political content reduction" from a single Meta policy announcement into an ongoing, opaque, and unevenly documented practice of visibility governance that long predates and outlives its public framing.

This body of work sits in explicit tension with the earlier Meta-partnered election studies. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the meta-scientific verdict on that earlier tradition: the 2020 Meta collaboration, for all its rigor, produced findings with poor "temporal validity" (already stale) and "poetic validity" (conceptually thin), an argument used to justify proactive regulatory disclosure rather than reactive academic study. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] represent the two poles of that older debate — the former minimizing algorithmic contribution to segregation relative to user choice, the latter finding segregation compounding at each successive stage of curation — and [[Guess2021-ym]] supplies adjacent descriptive infrastructure on news category exposure. The newer wave of DSA-enabled, non-partnered audits (Giglietto's papers, McNally) implicitly argues that the older cooperative paradigm systematically underestimated governance effects because it treated moderation policy as a fixed background condition rather than a moving, strategically timed target.

## Algorithmic amplification beyond Meta: X and YouTube

A parallel strand interrogates X/Twitter's feed and recommendation systems after the Musk acquisition. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Gauthier2026-iq]] both find that algorithmic curation shifts visibility rightward, but they diverge on mechanism: Efstratiou attributes gains largely to "agitating" behavior and proximity to Musk rather than ideology per se, while Gauthier's field experiment isolates a durable, asymmetric persistence effect — switching the algorithm on changes attitudes and following behavior in ways that switching it off does not reverse — which helps explain why the 2020 Meta null-effect findings do not generalize to X. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] pushes the inquiry inward, showing that X's recommender embeds a near-perfect linear encoding of users' ideological position even absent explicit political signals, raising the GDPR/DSA-relevant question of inadvertent profiling; the companion piece [[Bouchaud2026-np]] turns to Community Notes and finds that its bridging-based design structurally undermoderates precisely the polarizing content most consequential for elections, a design flaw rather than a bug. [[Renault2025-uh]] complements this by showing Community Notes flags Republican misinformation at 2.3x the rate of Democratic misinformation — evidence against claims of anti-conservative fact-checker bias, but also evidence that crowdsourced systems do not equalize outcomes across parties in the way their proponents claim. [[Rieder2026-pp]] (Rieder2026-pp) and [[Jurg2025-ur]] extend the visibility-governance lens to YouTube, documenting respectively how deplatforming fails to contain "ambient" ideological ecosystems that recommendation systems keep amplifying, and how "authoritative source" promotion during EU elections is applied so inconsistently (publisher labels missing in seven EU languages) that transparency commitments become nearly unauditable. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] rounds out this cluster with a structural finding underlying all these visibility questions: moderation capacity itself is unevenly allocated across languages, meaning millions of EU users in "Global South" languages have no human oversight at all — a material precondition for any of the algorithmic effects documented above.

## Deplatforming, bans, and their partisan ripple effects

A related set of papers asks what happens when platforms remove rather than merely reduce. [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows that targeted account bans produced network-wide "ripple effects" collapsing the authority structure of a coordinated election-denial community, arguing this was a more effective intervention than the visibility-reduction approach X has since favored — an explicit counterpoint to the reduction-over-removal thesis of [[Gillespie2022-jx]]. [[Ventura2026-yc]] extends the bans literature to the level of an entire platform: Brazil's suspension of X produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users disproportionately circumvented the ban while liberal users exited, a finding that resonates with Gauthier's asymmetric-persistence mechanism and complicates any simple assumption that bans in democracies are politically neutral or reversible. [[Bruns2026-yv]] supplies the historical arc binding these cases together, narrating Twitter's decline from prosocial "digital town square" to Musk-era amplifier of far-right content, and arguing that successor platforms (Bluesky, Threads, Mastodon) have yet to demonstrate that genuine moderation and community governance can be designed in rather than bolted on.

## Alternative design proposals and normative debates

Several papers move from diagnosis to prescription. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment is the most rigorous test of an alternative: a "diversified extremity" algorithm that suppresses influence from extreme partisan users reduces moralized/toxic content and corrects norm misperceptions without denting platform enjoyment, suggesting engagement-based ranking's harms are contingent design choices, not inevitable. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] surveys this design space more broadly — bridging systems, collective dialogue tools, community moderation augmented by LLMs — as a research agenda for LLM-era public squares, explicitly citing Community Notes and Meta's "diverse engagement" ranking as existing but imperfect exemplars, echoing the cautions raised by Renault and Bouchaud about bridging algorithms' partisan blind spots. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] provides the qualitative counterweight from inside the fact-checking profession: fact-checkers themselves largely reject Zuckerberg's censorship framing, favor labeling and counter-speech over removal, and are skeptical that Community Notes can substitute for professional verification — a finding that directly interrogates the legitimacy claims underlying Meta's 2025 pivot. [[Holt2026-zq]] supplies an empirical test of what user-driven reporting (the mechanism Meta says will replace fact-checkers) actually captures, finding that flagged links track polarized disagreement rather than falsity — a serious complication for any Community-Notes-style replacement. [[Moran2025-qn]] contextualizes all of this as professional upheaval: the "trust and safety" field itself is being dismantled by political pressure and layoffs, which is the organizational backdrop against which these design and policy shifts are occurring.

## Adjacent political economy: media capture, advertising, and platform discourse

Two papers connect algorithmic visibility to older forms of media-political entanglement. [[Balluff2026-bv]] shows that government advertising money can cover-financed favorable coverage even in an established Western democracy, evidence that "visibility capture" is not solely an algorithmic-era phenomenon. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Lukito2026-il]] extend the visibility question to strategic communication itself: ad delivery algorithms shape political campaign reach and cost in ways opaque to advertisers, while right-leaning outlets deliberately curate distinct content packages for Twitter versus Truth Social, showing that political actors actively adapt their own visibility strategies to platform-specific governance regimes. [[De2026-ld]] theorizes the platforms' own communicative strategy for managing these shifts — "changecraft" — arguing that ideological pivots (like Meta's fact-checking reversal) are narrated as continuity rather than rupture, which helps explain the transparency deficits documented by Giglietto and others. [[Boyd2026-op]] supplies the broadest framing, arguing that the entire genre of "social media" has become "parasocial media," dominated by algorithmically curated influencer content rather than reciprocal exchange — a diagnosis that helps explain why visibility governance (who gets amplified) has become the central battleground of platform politics rather than peer-to-peer moderation disputes.

## Regulatory infrastructure: the DSA as enabling condition

Underpinning nearly all of the above is the EU's Digital Services Act, which both makes this research possible and constrains it. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] together narrate the DSA's Article 40 as a fragile but real shift from platform "data philanthropy" to legal entitlement, cataloguing platform resistance (delays, boilerplate denials, narrow interpretations) as evidence the regulation is targeting genuine power asymmetries rather than failing outright. [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the DSA within a comparative typology of content-governance regimes (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation), arguing that despite divergent politics, all converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" via transparency and auditing mandates. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] operationalizes this regulatory turn at the interface-design level, mapping dark-pattern taxonomies onto DSA Article 25's autonomy-violation categories and applying the framework to the live X verification case — a reminder that visibility governance operates through interface design as well as ranking algorithms. Finally, methodological infrastructure papers — [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] on LLM-based scaling of political text — indicate how researchers are adapting computational tools to keep pace with the platforms and policies this whole topic tracks, a fitting coda to Munger's warning that empirical social science must not merely react to platforms but build durable, regulation-backed capacity to observe them.
