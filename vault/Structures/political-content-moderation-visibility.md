# Political Content Moderation & Visibility

## The platform as a shifting terrain

Any account of how political content becomes visible—or invisible—has to start from a diagnosis of what platforms have become. [[Boyd2026-op]] sets the terms for the whole topic by arguing that "social media" no longer describes reciprocal sociality but a parasocial regime of scrolling, influencer content, and algorithmic curation; visibility is no longer earned through peer exchange but allocated by systems optimized for engagement and investor return. [[De2026-ld]] complements this with a discourse-analytic account of how platforms narrate their own transformations—"changecraft"—normalizing ideological pivots (such as Meta's retreat from fact-checking) as mere continuity while consolidating governance power. Together they frame the empirical papers below not as isolated policy events but as instances of a broader, ongoing renegotiation of what platforms owe to public discourse, and who gets to decide.

## Algorithms as arbiters of political visibility

A cluster of causal and observational studies asks directly whether ranking algorithms reshape the partisan and journalistic composition of what users see. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] is the foundational reference point, showing that on Facebook, individual click behavior—not the News Feed algorithm—was the primary filter reducing cross-cutting exposure, a finding later studies complicate. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] both audit X/Twitter's algorithmic feed and find it systematically privileges conservative, activist, and "agitating" content while demoting legacy media, with [[Gauthier2026-iq]] documenting a durable, asymmetric conservative shift in attitudes driven by algorithm-induced following behavior, and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] showing that much of this bias is mediated by proximity to Elon Musk and by rhetorical style rather than ideology per se. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] pushes the audit further inward, reverse-engineering X's embedding space to show that ideological position is linearly encoded even absent explicit political signals—turning "inadvertent profiling" into a live GDPR/DSA concern and proposing a projection-based debiasing method. [[McNally2025-dn]] takes a longitudinal, time-series approach to Facebook's treatment of Guardian content, showing that hard news specifically bears the marks of undisclosed algorithmic interventions, countering "black box" narratives. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends this logic across five years of US Facebook data, showing that partisan alignment dampens and journalistic quality amplifies reach, with both effects fluctuating in step with known governance interventions (e.g., 2020's "break the glass" measures)—direct evidence that the platform actively curates rather than merely transmits. [[Brady2026-ln]] closes this cluster with an interventionist registered report: engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized, toxic content and distorts perceived social norms around political hostility, while a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment—demonstrating that visibility governance is a design choice, not a technical inevitability.

## Meta's political content reduction: the case that reached Brussels

At the center of this topic sits [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], the paper that gives the topic its EU-policy salience. Using the Meta Content Library, it shows that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' Facebook reach by up to 72%, that the rollout began roughly ten months before Meta's announced timeline, that the 2025 reversal produced only partial recovery, and—crucially—that extremist accounts compensated for per-post losses by posting far more frequently, ending up with greater aggregate reach than mainstream politicians. This finding reframes "de-amplification" as a policy with asymmetric, potentially self-defeating effects on the actors it targets, and its evidentiary use of DSA-mandated data infrastructure exemplifies the kind of systemic-risk auditing that Article 40 was designed to enable. It resonates with [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]], which examines the same moment in Meta's governance from the fact-checkers' side, showing that Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing for ending third-party fact-checking is empirically unsupported (removal occurred in only ~30% of verified cases) and that fact-checkers themselves favor labeling and reach-reduction over deletion—precisely the kind of intervention [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] shows can misfire when applied crudely across actor types.

## Deplatforming, bans, and their ripple effects

A second empirical strand asks what happens when platforms remove or restrict accounts and audiences outright, rather than merely reweighting feeds. [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows that Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-fraud promoters collapsed the hub-authority structure of the targeted community far beyond the mechanical loss of banned nodes, effectively flipping network dominance to the opposing side—evidence for deplatforming as a "network intervention" rather than simple removal. [[Rieder2026-pp]] complicates this optimism: even after Andrew Tate's channels were removed from YouTube, an "ambient" ecosystem of interviews, remixes, and recommendation-driven amplification sustained—indeed increased—his visibility, showing that moderation aimed at nodes cannot contain circulation driven by recommendation systems. [[Ventura2026-yc]] moves from platform-level content moderation to platform-level access moderation, showing that Brazil's ban on X produced a lasting rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users disproportionately circumvented the ban while liberals exited—demonstrating that state-imposed bans, like algorithmic curation, can have durable, partisan-differentiated visibility effects. [[Lukito2026-il]] offers a related but softer case of platform-conditioned visibility: right-leaning outlets curate distinct content packages for Twitter versus Truth Social, revealing how partisan media actors themselves strategically adapt to platform-specific audiences and moderation regimes.

## From professional fact-checking to the crowd

As platforms retreat from centralized fact-checking, a distinct set of papers interrogates whether crowdsourced alternatives like Community Notes can substitute for it. [[Renault2025-uh]] finds that Republicans are flagged 2.3 times more often than Democrats among "helpful" notes—evidence that partisan asymmetry in misinformation-sharing persists even under a bridging algorithm requiring cross-ideological agreement, undercutting the rationale Musk and Zuckerberg gave for abandoning professional fact-checkers. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] identifies a structural flaw behind this and related patterns: because Community Notes privileges content that attracts cross-partisan agreement, the most polarizing content—arguably that most in need of moderation—is systematically undermoderated by design, with effects that vary by national polarization levels and carry electoral-integrity risks as the system expands globally. [[Holt2026-zq]] examines the complementary mechanism of user flagging itself, showing that Facebook's "False News" reports historically targeted polarized political topics and contextualizing content rather than clearly false claims—suggesting that as Meta shifts weight onto user reports, moderation outcomes may increasingly reflect political disagreement rather than factual accuracy. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] steps back to a more constructive register, surveying LLM-enabled tools (bridging systems, collective dialogue platforms, AI-assisted community moderation) as a potential "second paradigm shift" for digital public squares, explicitly weighing the promise and risks of bridging-based ranking as a design philosophy—situating Community Notes-style mechanisms within a broader research and design agenda rather than treating them as a fait accompli.

## Political economy adjacent to visibility

Two papers extend the visibility question to advertising and media-capture dynamics that structure political actors' reach outside pure content moderation. [[Balluff2026-bv]] documents a "journalism for sale" arrangement in Austria, where government advertising bought increased visibility for Sebastian Kurz and more negative coverage of his rivals in a client tabloid—showing that covert advertiser pressure can produce visibility effects structurally similar to those produced by opaque algorithmic curation. [[Votta2025-xz]] turns to paid political advertising itself, arguing that ad-delivery algorithms on Meta and Google shape reach and cost in ways opaque to campaigners and regulators alike, extending the visibility-governance question from organic to paid political speech.

## Infrastructure, transparency, and the fight over data access

Underpinning nearly all of the above empirical work is a contested infrastructure for studying platforms at all, and several papers turn explicitly to this meta-level. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a skeptical epistemological verdict on the paradigmatic Meta-2020 partnership studies, arguing that rapidly evolving platforms defeat the "temporal validity" of one-off academic-industry experiments and that proactive regulation—not reactive research—is the only viable path to accountability; [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the parallel institutional erosion of the trust-and-safety profession itself. Against this backdrop, the EU's Digital Services Act emerges as the central regulatory lever. [[Pierri2026-ib]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] each assess Article 40's promise and fragility: vetted researcher access is a genuine paradigm shift from platform "philanthropy" to legal right, but implementation is slow, uneven, and contested, producing a bifurcated global research landscape. [[Crosset2026-mq]] theorizes this regulatory moment more abstractly, showing that EU and US content-moderation laws converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" that governs platforms through transparency and audit obligations rather than direct content control. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] operationalizes one such obligation (DSA Article 25) into a design-auditing framework for dark patterns, while [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] use DSA transparency data and mandates to expose concrete visibility failures—linguistic moderation blind spots disadvantaging Global South languages, and inconsistent authoritative-source labeling around the 2024 EU elections, respectively. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] supplies a methodological tool increasingly used across this literature—LLM-based ideological scaling—that underwrites several of the audit studies' ability to locate political actors and texts on partisan dimensions in the first place.

## Synthesis

Read together, these papers trace an arc from diagnosing platforms' structural transformation, through granular audits of how ranking, moderation, and deplatforming reallocate political visibility, to the institutional and regulatory struggle over who gets to know—and govern—these effects at all. The throughline is that visibility is never neutral infrastructure: whether produced by Meta's political content reduction, X's recommender embeddings, Community Notes' bridging algorithm, or a tabloid's advertising ledger, the allocation of political attention is an actively managed, frequently opaque, and often partisan-asymmetric outcome—one that the DSA's data-access provisions have only just begun to make empirically visible.
