# Political Content Moderation & Visibility

## The arc of inquiry

This corpus traces a single sustained problem — how platform architectures and policies govern the visibility of political actors, partisan news, and contested claims — across four decades of platform evolution: from Web 2.0 optimism to algorithmic curation, to Musk-era deregulation and Meta's 2025 retreat from professional fact-checking, to the EU's attempt to build binding transparency infrastructure around all of it. The papers form less a single argument than a set of escalating case studies converging on the same conclusion: platforms are never neutral conduits, their governance choices are consequential, opaque, and asymmetric in partisan effect, and independent verification of these effects is structurally difficult and politically contested.

## Foundational claims: platforms as active curators, not neutral pipes

[[Gillespie2010-as]] supplies the conceptual origin point, showing how the very term "platform" was a rhetorical achievement allowing intermediaries to claim neutrality while making substantive curatorial interventions. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] extends this by naming "reduction" — algorithmic demotion short of removal — as an under-theorized but pervasive governance technique, one deliberately kept less visible than deletion precisely because it draws less political fire. This distinction between removal and reduction structures much of the empirical work that follows: [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] both use Meta-provided data to quantify ideological segregation in Facebook's "funnel of engagement," with the latter directly contradicting the former's relatively sanguine 2015 conclusions by finding much higher segregation and a starkly asymmetric, conservative-dominated corner of the misinformation ecosystem. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] pushes this further, showing that the *relationship* between sharing and viewing itself fluctuates with Meta's undisclosed governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures), providing direct evidence that algorithmic calibration — not just static curation — responds to political crisis.

## Meta's political content reduction policy and its downstream effects

The empirical heart of the topic is the row of studies documenting Meta's 2021–2025 political content reduction policy and its 2025 reversal. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] is the anchor case: a structural-breakpoint analysis of Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach showing the policy began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout, cut re-elected MPs' reach by 72%, and — crucially — left extremist accounts able to compensate through sheer posting volume, a finding that recasts "neutral" visibility reduction as differentially punishing mainstream over fringe actors. This dovetails with [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]]'s finding that Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors is empirically ungrounded (removal was always rare), and with [[Holt2026-zq]], which shows that user "False News" reports mostly flag polarized-but-true content rather than falsehoods, undermining the premise that Community Notes and user reporting can substitute for professional verification. [[Munger2025-cz]] and [[Moran2025-qn]] supply meta-level diagnoses: Munger argues the entire Meta2020 collaborative-science paradigm is structurally unable to keep pace with platform change, while Moran documents the collapse of the "trust and safety" profession itself as political pressure and layoffs hollow out institutional moderation capacity. [[McNally2025-dn]] offers a methodological counterpoint — showing that black-box algorithmic change *can* be detected indirectly through longitudinal engagement time-series — which is itself an argument for the feasibility of the auditing agenda these other papers call for.

## X/Twitter as the paradigm case of ownership-driven visibility change

A parallel cluster uses X's post-2022 transformation as a natural experiment in how ownership and design choices reshape political visibility. Efstratiou2025-gs and [[Gauthier2026-iq]] both find that X's algorithmic feed favors conservative and provocateur content, but differ in mechanism: Efstratiou attributes this largely to behavioral correlates (agitation, proximity to Musk) rather than partisanship per se, while Gauthier's field experiment finds a genuine causal, asymmetric, and persistent conservative shift via follow-graph reshaping — explicitly reconciling the earlier Meta2020 null results with a "persistence mechanism" absent from single-shot designs. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] shows the mechanism can be even more insidious: X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear representation of users' ideology, raising GDPR/DSA profiling concerns, while its companion piece [[Bouchaud2026-np]] argues that Community Notes' bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing content by design — a finding echoed and extended by [[Renault2025-uh]], which shows Republican-authored posts are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrat-authored ones even under crowdsourced, cross-partisan rating, undercutting the "biased fact-checker" rationale for replacing professional moderation. [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s study of Brazil's X ban shows how state-level deplatforming interacts with partisanship to produce a durable rightward "sorting ratchet," while [[Simeone2025-vo]] finds the reverse dynamic at smaller scale: targeted deplatforming of election-fraud amplifiers can structurally collapse a network's authority structure far beyond the removed accounts. [[Rieder2026-pp]] complicates the deplatforming story further, showing that removal of a single figure (Andrew Tate) fails to contain "ambient ideology" once it diffuses into a decentralized ecosystem of remixers and interviewers. [[Lukito2026-il]] and [[Bruns2026-yv]] bookend this cluster with broader diagnoses: Lukito shows right-wing media curate distinct content strategies across Twitter and Truth Social, while Bruns narrates Twitter's arc from prosocial public-debate infrastructure to enshittified fringe platform, skeptically assessing whether Bluesky, Threads, or Mastodon can restore what was lost.

## Governance, discourse, and regulatory response

A third cluster steps back to theorize how platforms *talk about* and are *regulated around* these visibility interventions. [[De2026-ld]] identifies "changecraft" — platforms' discursive strategies for normalizing policy shifts (like Meta's fact-checking pivot) as continuity rather than rupture. [[Boyd2026-op]] offers the widest-lens diagnosis, arguing that the entire genre once called "social media" has become "parasocial media," reframing visibility questions as symptomatic of a deeper structural shift from reciprocal sociality to algorithmically curated consumption. [[Crosset2026-mq]] provides a comparative legal-governmentality account, showing that EU and US content moderation laws converge on "security bureaucratisation" through transparency and auditing regardless of their surface ideology (free circulation vs. patrolling vs. flow optimisation). [[Goldberg2026-eb]] looks forward, surveying LLM-enabled bridging, community-moderation, and deliberation systems as a possible "second paradigm shift" for public squares — directly relevant to the Community Notes debates above.

Finally, a dense cluster addresses the research-infrastructure question directly provoked by the DSA's 2026 policy attention: [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], and [[Pierri2026-ib]] each assess Article 40 vetted-researcher access as a fragile but real shift from platform "data philanthropy" toward binding regulatory obligation, while [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] use DSA-mandated transparency data empirically — the former exposing language-based moderation-workforce disparities that leave millions of EU users without linguistic oversight, the latter auditing YouTube's inconsistent application of authoritative-source labels during the 2024 EU elections. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] rounds out the regulatory-technical bridge, translating DSA Article 25's autonomy-violation categories into an HCI framework for auditing dark patterns, applied directly to the Commission's case against X. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] supply adjacent methodological and empirical infrastructure — LLM-based ideological scaling and ad-delivery-algorithm audits — that support this broader turn toward computational accountability. [[Balluff2026-bv]] closes the loop back to older media-capture logics, showing that even legacy print/TV outlets can be captured via advertising incentives to boost a favored politician's visibility, a reminder that algorithmic platforms did not invent the politics of differential visibility — they intensified and automated it.
