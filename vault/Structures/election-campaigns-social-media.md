# Election Campaigns on Social Media

## From content to actor: detecting coordination across elections

The Italian research group led by Giglietto supplies the backbone of this topic's methodological arc. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] establishes the empirical starting point for the 2018 Italian campaign: populist parties' supporters cluster around insular news sources, and insularity predicts a distinctive Facebook engagement signature (more shares, fewer contesting comments), with the Five Star Movement emerging as an unusually disciplined "cyber party" able to weaponize the attention economy. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] reframes the underlying problem: rather than chasing bad content or bad actors, disinformation research should detect *coordinated collective action*—near-simultaneous, repeated link-sharing across pages, groups, and profiles—a shift that proves empirically powerful (coordinated entities are ~20x more likely to appear on disinformation watchlists) and theoretically portable. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] operationalizes that portability for the 2022 snap election, building a self-updating workflow that surfaces new coordinated assets in near-real time and uncovers not just a hyperpartisan M5S network but click-economy and religious-proselytism operations that content-based detection would have missed entirely. The companion methods papers, [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], extend this infrastructure into the LLM era, validating embedding models and LLM-in-the-loop pipelines for clustering and labeling Italian political news—turning coordination detection into a reusable analytic pipeline rather than a one-off case study.

This Italian lineage travels well beyond Italy. [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapts the "rapid spread network" coordination logic to Brazilian WhatsApp groups during the 2022 election, showing coordinated accounts disseminating misinformation and, crucially, translating online coordination into offline mobilization (doxxing of Supreme Court justices, January 8 protests). [[Oprea2025-lf]] documents a related but distinct phenomenon in Romania's 2024 EU campaign—hyperactive human accounts, not bots, inflating shares across all major parties, exposing a gap between platform policy and enforcement. [[Appel2026-qr]] scales the coordination question up to tens of millions of exposed US users in 2020, showing that deceptive networks' reach is concentrated in a handful of operations and driven mainly by *reshares from non-network accounts*—a structural finding that complicates any simple actor-based accounting of influence. Together these papers trace a throughline: coordination is a behavioral signature that generalizes across platforms (Facebook, WhatsApp, Twitter) and regimes (Italy, Brazil, Romania, the US), but its consequences—clickbait, protest mobilization, election-fraud narratives—are locally contingent.

## Partisan attention, insularity, and the political economy of visibility

A second cluster asks not who coordinates but who gets seen. [[Eady2025-vm]] provides a general measurement instrument—ideology inferred from shared URLs—showing that ideologically extreme legislators dominate the volume and polarization of elite news sharing, and that reduced electoral competition predicts more polarized sharing. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] applies a similar cross-national URL-based logic to 26 countries' parliamentarians and finds that misinformation sharing is not a generic populist or right-wing pathology but specifically a radical-right-populist one, rooted in a shared hostility to liberal-democratic gatekeepers. [[Iris2026-pg]] shifts from politician-generated content to journalistic output, finding the Radical Right systematically overrepresented in mainstream European election coverage relative to polling and results—an attention asymmetry that persists across outlets of varying orientation and intensifies in the campaign's final weeks. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] argues methodologically that this literature has been too quick to flatten "partisan news sharing" into single-dimension outlet-level metrics, proposing a multi-level "political cartography" (story, outlet, content) as a corrective.

Media visibility here is not just measured but manufactured. [[Balluff2026-bv]] documents a covert Austrian case of government-advertising-for-favorable-coverage exchange ("Inseratenaffäre"), showing via difference-in-differences that visibility—not favorability—was the currency purchased, with negative spillover onto the beneficiary's rivals. [[Mosca2026-yh]] complements this by reconceiving "fake news" accusations themselves as a rhetorical weapon of negative campaigning, selectively amplified by aligned media—a lens that dovetails with [[Gaber2022-bk]]'s theorization of "strategic lying" in Brexit and the 2019 UK election, where the lie's rebuttal, not just its utterance, drives salience through media logics that reward repetition over correction. Read together, these papers suggest visibility itself—who is talked about, praised, or accused—has become a contested, purchasable, and strategically engineered resource in campaign communication, echoing [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]'s broader claim that blurred organizational boundaries, microtargeting, and strategic amplification jointly define a "permanent potential influence" condition in contemporary political communication.

## Advertising, targeting, and the limits of persuasion

A distinct thread interrogates political advertising's actual causal weight. [[Allcott2025-jb]], a large randomized field experiment on Facebook/Instagram before the 2020 US election, finds no detectable attitudinal effects from removing political ads—most of which targeted co-partisans rather than persuadable voters—though the study is candid about being underpowered for turnout. [[Kim2026-wg]] pushes back methodologically: average treatment effects mask devastating heterogeneous effects, and geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads in 2016 depressed turnout by up to 17% among the specific minority populations they targeted, a finding invisible to designs (including, implicitly, [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s) that rely on population-average estimates. [[Votta2025-xz]] complicates the picture further upstream, showing that ad *delivery* algorithms—not just advertiser targeting choices—shape reach and cost in ways opaque to campaigners and regulators alike. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] demonstrates a grassroots response to this opacity: volunteer browser-plugin auditing of Facebook ads during the 2018 Brazilian election, flagging irregular electoral advertising that platform transparency tools failed to surface. Across this cluster, a consistent tension emerges between null or small average effects and the possibility that targeting concentrates real harm on specific, structurally vulnerable subpopulations—a tension advertising researchers have only recently learned to design for.

## Narrating crisis, contesting truth: elites as media producers

Several papers track how political elites author and legitimate narratives directly, bypassing or contesting journalistic gatekeeping. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] dissects Brazilian parliamentarians' Twitter behavior during the Manaus COVID collapse, distinguishing objectivity-based "derivative reporting" by opposition figures from authority-based, media-hostile claims by Bolsonaro allies and locally-grounded "brokerage" narratives from Amazonas deputies—three competing epistemic strategies for claiming the right to narrate crisis. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] extends this logic to US election-fraud rumors across 2020 and 2022, showing that meaning migrates from explicit "story specifics" toward allusive cueing that presumes a shared "deep story," such that misinformation's persuasive work increasingly happens at the level of narrative context rather than individual claims—a methodological argument that resonates with [[Gaisbauer2025-by]]'s call for multi-level analysis. [[Rossini2026-mj]] closes the loop empirically for Brazil 2022, showing that belief in electoral misinformation and Bolsonaro support erode trust in results, while mainstream news use—even among Bolsonaro voters—rebuilds it, positioning journalism as a distinct source of democratic resilience against elite-seeded disinformation. Together with [[Gaber2022-bk]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]], this cluster reframes disinformation not as an information-quality failure but as a *strategic communicative practice* wielded by elites contesting who gets to author political reality.

## Platform dramaturgies: formats, emotions, and cross-platform strategy

A recurring empirical concern is that campaign communication is irreducibly platform-specific. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats deploying argumentative securitizing discourse on Facebook/X but shifting to affectively charged, foreign-footage memes on TikTok to court younger voters—the same slogan, three different rhetorical registers. [[Lukito2026-il]] finds a parallel logic among US right-leaning outlets, which curate largely non-overlapping content for Twitter versus Truth Social, evidencing deliberate platform-tailored packaging rather than simple cross-posting. [[Schulte2026-df]] generalizes this into an infrastructural argument for the 2025 German election: parties sort themselves onto different platforms (AfD to TikTok, BSW to X), and TikTok's textual, vocal, and facial channels correlate only weakly with one another, meaning single-modality or single-platform analysis systematically misses how "politainment" is actually constructed. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] operationalizes multimodal analysis methodologically, validating GPT-4o for visual political communication on Instagram and showing German candidates and parties pursue distinct "concentrated visibility" strategies across stories versus posts. Emotional dynamics add another axis of platform specificity: [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] finds that "Angry" reactions—disproportionately earned by Scandinavian populist parties—drive shares while "Love" reactions suppress them, and [[Larsson2026-ro]] traces a decade of Norway's Progress Party showing that negative sentiment (intensified under a new leader) reliably wins shares and comments even as "likes" follow an opposite, more positive logic. These papers jointly argue that engagement metrics are not a unified currency but a bundle of distinct, platform- and emotion-specific economies that campaigns learn to exploit unevenly.

## Infrastructure, access, and the post-API predicament

Underlying nearly every empirical claim above is a meta-problem: what data can researchers actually get? [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys how the EU Digital Services Act has reopened, unevenly, some public data access after years of platform lockdown, while [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes this landscape across 72 tools, diagnosing a "price, proficiency, or permission" triad that structures who can study elections at all, and noting the severe underrepresentation of consumption-side (demand) data relative to production-side (supply) data. [[Schulte2026-df]] (again) makes the case concrete for Germany 2025, cataloguing API rate limits and algorithmic filtering as active constraints on what can be known about TikTok and X discourse. These infrastructural papers reframe much of the topic's other work: findings about coordination, insularity, or advertising exposure are contingent not only on political dynamics but on which platforms happened to grant (or revoke, as with CrowdTangle) research access at a given moment—a methodological humility that [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] anticipated conceptually years earlier.

## Algorithms and generative AI as emergent campaign actors

A final, forward-looking cluster asks how algorithmic curation and generative AI are beginning to reshape the campaign environment itself. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] documents at platform scale how misinformation diffuses distinctively on Facebook during the 2020 US race, disproportionately achieving viral reach relative to other content. [[Brady2026-ln]] intervenes directly on this dynamic with a Bluesky field experiment, showing engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup, moralized, emotional content and distorts perceived social norms—while a "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing platform enjoyment, reframing polarization as a *social-norm-misperception* problem rather than pure attitude change. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] identifies social bots as a genuinely novel fourth agenda-building actor in the 2022 Ohio midterms, exerting outsized influence on campaign sentiment (especially negative tone) while leaving the press comparatively insulated. [[Much2026-gu]] uncovers a subtler algorithmic-cultural mechanism behind the 2024 US youth gender gap: non-political "gendered media spaces" like podcasts incidentally sort audiences into ideologically congruent exposure via masculinity/femininity coding rather than partisanship. Finally, [[Lin2025-xp]] closes the topic on its most speculative frontier, showing brief human-AI dialogues can shift voting preferences beyond the magnitude of traditional advertising—driven mainly by facts and evidence rather than manipulation—while revealing a troubling asymmetry in which AI models advocating right-leaning candidates make systematically more inaccurate claims. Read against the coordination and advertising literatures above, this final cluster suggests the object of study is shifting again: from human-coordinated networks and purchased visibility toward algorithmically- and AI-mediated persuasion infrastructures whose electoral consequences are only beginning to be measured.
