# Election Campaigns on Social Media

## The minimal-effects baseline and its platform-era afterlife

The empirical study of elections and social media still carries the imprint of the 2016 US election, where a cluster of landmark studies converged on a deflationary finding: exposure to fake news and foreign influence operations was real but tiny, concentrated among a small set of already-committed partisans, and dwarfed by mainstream media consumption. [[Grinberg2019-ua]] and [[Guess2019-ym]] showed that "supersharers" of fake news were rare, older, and disproportionately conservative; [[Guess2020-rr]] extended this to browsing data, finding untrustworthy sites made up a sliver of the average information diet; [[Eady2023-xg]] applied the same logic to Russian IRA content, finding exposure concentrated among strong Republicans who needed no persuading. This paradigm was institutionalized in the Meta2020 academic-industry collaboration, whose flagship algorithmic-feed experiments ([[Guess2023-ur]], [[Allcott2025-jb]]) again found large behavioral effects but null attitudinal ones, and whose diffusion analysis ([[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]) and deceptive-network audit ([[Appel2026-qr]]) likewise found that correlational associations between exposure and downstream beliefs evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the collaboration's most pointed internal critique, arguing that one-off, heavily pre-registered experiments on fast-mutating platforms possess neither temporal nor "poetic" validity, and that the null results may be an artifact of design choices that happen to serve Meta's interests.

This consensus has begun to crack as the platform landscape itself has changed. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X under Musk's ownership finds that switching users onto the algorithmic "For You" feed *does* shift attitudes rightward—an asymmetric, persistence-driven effect explained by algorithmically induced changes in who users follow, which helps reconcile the earlier Meta nulls with a platform whose amplification logic has shifted further right. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky registered report similarly finds that engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized, toxic content and distorts perceived social norms (if not directly measurable behavior), while a "diversified extremity" algorithm can partially undo this without sacrificing enjoyment—reframing the debate from direct persuasion to algorithm-mediated misperception of norms. [[Kim2026-wg]]'s re-analysis of 2016 voter-suppression ads makes the adjacent methodological point directly: average treatment effects can mask substantively important heterogeneous effects concentrated in narrowly targeted subpopulations, a critique of the ATE logic underlying much of the "minimal effects" literature. [[Votta2025-xz]] pushes further upstream, arguing that delivery algorithms—not just targeting choices—shape who sees political ads and at what cost, a dynamic largely invisible to advertisers and regulators alike.

## Detecting coordination: an evolving toolkit, from Italy to Serbia

A parallel research program has focused not on exposure effects but on the production side: who manufactures coordinated visibility, and how can it be detected as platforms and tactics evolve? The through-line is Giglietto et al.'s long-running Italian election program. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] first mapped how populist-aligned news sources (M5S, the League) cultivated Twitter "insularity" that translated into distinctive Facebook sharing/commenting asymmetries during the 2018 campaign. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] then reoriented disinformation research around "coordinated link sharing behavior" rather than content veracity, showing coordinated Facebook networks around the 2018 and 2019 Italian elections were far more likely to spread problematic domains. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] solved the resulting staleness problem of static blocklists with an iterative, near-duplicate-detection workflow applied to the 2022 snap election, surfacing a Five Star Movement hyperpartisan network, a religious-page clickbait operation, and a proselytizing bot network in a single pass. The toolkit was then extended methodologically via embedding-model comparisons for Italian political text ([[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]]) and an "LLMs-in-the-loop" validation pipeline ([[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]), and institutionally via an audit of Meta's political-content-reduction policy that found Italian parliamentarians' reach cut by 72% well before Meta's announced global rollout—with extremist accounts compensating via posting volume ([[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]).

This Italian-rooted methodology resonates with, and is complemented by, parallel efforts elsewhere. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] reframes coordination itself as a continuum rather than a binary during the 2019 UK election, showing coordination and automation are orthogonal and that the most tightly coordinated clusters were small activist communities rather than mainstream party machines. [[Keller2019-nk]] supplies rare ground-truth validation from the South Korean NIS astroturfing scandal, showing coordination traces—not bot-like features—best distinguish managed accounts, while also showing that even professionalized, well-resourced campaigns had limited measurable discursive impact. The toolkit travels into encrypted and platform-specific spaces too: [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapts rapid-spread-network detection to 13 million Brazilian WhatsApp messages, tracing coordinated misinformation directly into real-world mobilization around the January 8 attacks; [[Oprea2025-lf]] applies an "Authenticity Matrix" to hyperactive Facebook sharers during Romania's 2024 EU campaign; and [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] argues Serbia's ruling party has moved beyond centralized bot farms toward more mimetic, harder-to-detect grassroots simulation—suggesting detection research must keep adapting to increasingly decentralized manipulation strategies. [[Simeone2025-vo]] closes this loop by showing that targeted deplatforming of a handful of central accounts (the Arizona Election Review network) produced network-wide collapse disproportionate to the accounts removed, evidence that coordination-aware interventions can be more effective than blanket visibility reduction.

## Elites, narratives, and epistemic authority

A complementary strand treats misinformation less as a detectable technical artifact than as a strategic communicative practice of political elites. [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorizes "strategic lying" in the Brexit and 2019 UK campaigns as an evolution, not a rupture, of political spin—a tactic whose power lies precisely in provoking corrective amplification that mainstream and social media then unwittingly distribute. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents a Brazilian variant during the Manaus COVID crisis, where parliamentarians pursued "epistemic authority" through three distinct postures—derivative reporting, authority claims, and locally grounded brokerage—effectively competing with journalists for the right to narrate crises. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] shows a related dynamic operating at the level of intra-party faction: Republicans who adopted the Critical Race Theory talking point were more ideologically extreme and more tightly networked into hyper-partisan media, rewarded with outsized engagement that reinforced radicalization—a "logic of connective faction" that the authors propose travels beyond the US case. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] generalizes this logic cross-nationally, finding across 26 countries and 32 million parliamentary tweets that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing, but their interaction—radical-right populism specifically—does, tying misinformation directly to a legitimacy crisis vis-à-vis liberal institutions rather than treating it as a diffuse platform pathology.

Several studies examine how misleading narratives are collectively assembled and sustained once introduced. [[Starbird2025-jj]] develops an "evidence-frame" framework showing 2022 Arizona election-integrity rumors are produced less by false facts than by partisan framing imposed on accurate evidence, often implicitly, via quote-tweet "call and response." [[Prochaska2025-ef]] extends this into a "deep storytelling" account comparing 2020 and 2022 election denial discourse, showing that by 2022 influencers increasingly relied on audiences to supply pre-established fraud narratives to ambiguous new events, with meaning residing in surrounding narrative context rather than individual posts. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] supplies experimental confirmation that even brief exposure to unsubstantiated elite fraud claims measurably erodes confidence in elections among receptive partisans, and that mainstream fact-checks fail to repair the damage—a finding in tension with [[Renault2025-uh]]'s demonstration that X's crowdsourced Community Notes system, designed to resist accusations of partisan fact-checking bias, nonetheless flags Republican posts as misleading at more than twice the rate of Democratic ones, undercutting claims that institutional fact-checking alone was responsible for partisan asymmetries in sanctioned misinformation.

## Brazil: misinformation, trust, and democratic erosion after Bolsonaro

Brazil anchors the most sustained single-country arc in this collection, tracing a path from pandemic-era crisis communication through the contested 2022 election to the post-inauguration insurrection and its aftermath. Early ad-transparency work by [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] on the 2018 election established crowdsourced auditing as a necessary complement to opaque Facebook advertising, prefiguring later platform-accountability demands. [[Rodarte2026-dk]]'s account of elite narrative contests during the Manaus collapse shows how Bolsonaro-aligned politicians began severing ties with mainstream media years before the 2022 election. That rupture crystallizes in [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]], companion panel studies showing that belief in electoral fraud misinformation both increased political intolerance over the 2022 campaign and eroded trust in the certified results—even as mainstream news use bolstered trust, including among Bolsonaro voters, positioning journalism as a source of democratic resilience against a disinformation order channeled disproportionately through messaging apps rather than open social feeds. [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Kansaon2025-id]] push this messaging-app focus further: a WhatsApp deactivation field experiment and a 13-million-message coordination analysis of WhatsApp groups respectively show that Brazil's dominant communication infrastructure operates by its own logic, largely invisible to feed-based platform research, and was instrumental in mobilizing the doxxing of Supreme Court justices and the January 8 attacks. [[Marino2026-he]]'s three-year longitudinal study of a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network complicates the echo-chamber assumption underlying much of this work, showing affective engagement was highly unstable rather than static, with a marked 2023 inflection—declining emotional intensity and rising internal debate—around Lula's inauguration and the coup attempt, suggesting hyperpartisan communities are porous and event-driven rather than sealed.

## The European 2024 elections and the radical-right visibility problem

A distinct comparative cluster converges on the 2024 European Parliament elections to ask whether platforms and media structurally amplify the radical right beyond its electoral weight. [[Darius2026-xl]]'s cross-platform analysis of 401 parties across all 27 member states finds that populist radical right parties enjoy a modest activity advantage but a dramatic audience-support advantage—most extreme on TikTok—extending even to far-left populists employing similar anti-elitist, emotional registers, implicating communicative style as much as ideology. [[Iris2026-pg]]'s content analysis of news coverage across five countries finds a parallel pattern in legitimate journalism: radical-right actors received disproportionate visibility relative to prior results, polling, and even actual outcomes, intensifying in the campaign's final weeks and cutting across outlets of all political leanings—undermining radical-right claims of media marginalization. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] and [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] examine the supply side of this visibility at the level of campaign content itself: the Sweden Democrats' TikTok-prioritized, meme-driven securitizing visual campaign (often using decontextualized non-EU footage) and far-right deployment of AI-generated multimedia both suggest radical-right campaigns are early and sophisticated adopters of platform-specific, affectively charged visual strategies aimed at younger audiences. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] closes the loop by showing that far-right electoral performance itself shapes voters' subsequent concern about disinformation, while [[Jurg2025-ur]]'s audit of YouTube's content moderation during the same election finds authoritative-source promotion applied inconsistently across languages and broadcasters, and removal communications too opaque to support independent verification of how harmful content is actually handled. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] adds an important caveat from the UK: media narratives *about* interference can themselves produce selective, partisan-asymmetric increases in perceived societal division, independent of whether any interference campaign succeeded—a reminder that the discourse of manipulation can be as consequential as manipulation itself.

## Visual, multimodal, and platform-specific campaigning

Running beneath these country cases is a methodological shift toward multimodal and platform-sensitive analysis. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision pipelines for the 2021 German Instagram campaign, finding it dramatically outperforms purpose-built tools for face recognition and person-counting, and using it to document distinct "concentrated visibility" strategies across candidate versus party accounts and stories versus posts. [[Schulte2026-df]] extends this logic to the 2025 German election across X and TikTok, finding weak correlation between textual, vocal, and facial signals on TikTok (evidence of a "politainment" style pairing critical content with calm delivery) while arguing the central bottleneck is not insight but infrastructure—the absence of standardized, reusable tooling for multi-platform observability. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s earlier study of the 2013 Italian election supplies a useful historical baseline, showing Twitter-as-second-screen practices around televised talk shows were narrow and TV-agenda-dependent even before today's platform fragmentation. [[Much2026-gu]] and [[Lukito2026-il]] extend the platform-specificity argument to the US: podcasts function as "gendered media spaces" sorting audiences on masculinity/femininity rather than ideology, helping explain (if only partially) the 2024 youth gender gap, while right-leaning outlets curate distinctly different content for Twitter versus Truth Social rather than simply cross-posting. [[Divon2026-wx]], [[Gaw2025-ru]], and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] broaden the comparative aperture further still—respectively theorizing influencers as "brokers of the state" on TikTok/Douyin in Israel and China, influence operations as a form of political brokerage in the Philippines, and Facebook emotional-reaction affordances driving share behavior across Norway, Sweden, and Denmark—together suggesting that platform-specific affordances, not a generic "social media effect," are the right unit of comparative analysis. [[Lin2025-xp]] and [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] push this agenda into human-machine territory, showing LLM dialogue can out-persuade traditional ads across four national contexts and that social bots function as measurable "agenda-builders" against campaign messaging in US midterms.

## Research infrastructure: measuring ideology and accessing data under the DSA

A final, reflexive cluster asks how any of this research remains possible given platforms' shifting data regimes. [[Barbera2015-je]] and [[Eady2025-vm]] anchor the methodological toolkit for inferring ideology from behavioral traces—following networks and URL-sharing respectively—showing both those practices and an associated finding (that ordinary users, not politicians, drive the most polarized sharing) remain foundational for the newer studies surveyed above. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] critiques this very toolkit's tendency toward oversimplified, outlet-level, single-country measures of partisan news circulation, arguing for a multi-level "political cartography." But the more urgent constraint, several papers argue, is access itself. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] map the post-API landscape reshaped by the EU Digital Services Act, with the latter identifying a "price, proficiency, or permission" triage that systematically privileges well-resourced, Western researchers and leaves consumption-side data severely underrepresented relative to production-side data. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] demonstrates concretely what is at stake: without DSA-enabled infrastructure like the Meta Content Library, Meta's undisclosed political-content suppression policy—and its asymmetric benefit to extremist accounts—would have remained invisible to researchers and regulators alike. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]], written earlier in this arc, already anticipated the structural problem these later papers confirm: blurred organizational boundaries, opaque micro-targeting, and strategic amplification in a hybrid, attention-scarce media system render traditional self-report and outlet-list methodologies inadequate, a diagnosis that the collection's subsequent turn toward coordination detection, platform-specific multimodal analysis, and regulatory data-access advocacy can be read as a decade-long, still-unfinished response to.
