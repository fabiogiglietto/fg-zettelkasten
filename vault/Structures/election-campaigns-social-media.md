# Election Campaigns on Social Media

## The Empirical Terrain: From Echo Chambers to Coordinated Networks

The oldest layer of this literature asks a foundational question: does social media fragment political attention into ideological silos, or does it sustain a shared national conversation? [[Barbera2015-je]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]] establish the methodological groundwork here, using Twitter's following and retweeting structures to infer ideology at scale, and finding that segregation is topic-contingent rather than universal — political issues echo, nonpolitical events cross ideological lines. This tension between echo-chamber alarm and more modest empirical reality recurs throughout the corpus. [[Eady2025-vm]] extends the ideal-point tradition to news-sharing behavior, showing that ordinary citizens, not politicians, drive the bulk of polarized sharing, while [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] critiques the field's tendency to flatten "political leaning" into a single dimension and calls for multi-level (story/outlet/content) cartographies of circulation.

A second cluster interrogates the "fake news" panic empirically and finds it substantially smaller than feared. [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], and [[Guess2019-ym]] converge on a striking pattern for the 2016 US election: exposure to untrustworthy content was real but narrow, heavily concentrated among older, highly conservative "supersharers," and dwarfed by mainstream news consumption. This "concentrated but marginal" finding is echoed for foreign interference by [[Eady2023-xg]], who shows Russian IRA content reached mostly already-committed Republicans and was overshadowed by domestic political information — and reinforced institutionally by [[Appel2026-qr]], whose Meta-collaborative study of 49 deceptive 2020 networks likewise finds reach concentrated in a handful of operations, indirect resharing as the dominant amplification mechanism, and no robust causal effects once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. Together these papers form a skeptical counter-current to platform-effects alarmism, a position most fully operationalized in the large-scale feed-algorithm experiments of [[Guess2023-ur]] and [[Allcott2025-jb]], both finding that manipulating algorithmic ranking or removing political ads produced large experiential shifts but negligible downstream attitudinal or behavioral change.

Yet this "minimal effects" consensus is not unchallenged. [[Kim2026-wg]] insists that average treatment effects obscure real harm: geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads in 2016 produced substantial turnout depression precisely among the narrowly targeted subpopulation, a methodological rebuke to designs (including several above) that look only at population averages. Similarly, [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment reframes algorithmic harm not as direct attitude change but as *distorted perception of social norms* — engagement-based feeds amplify moralized, emotional content and warp what users believe is normal political discourse, even without shifting their own behavior. And [[Lin2025-xp]]'s multi-country LLM-persuasion experiments demonstrate that a new class of intervention — conversational AI — can produce persuasion effects exceeding those of traditional advertising, reopening the minimal-effects question for the generative-AI era.

## Coordination, Inauthenticity, and Platform Manipulation

A dense methodological strand, anchored in Italian elections, develops tools for detecting coordinated inauthentic behavior once the "bad actor" and "bad content" framings prove insufficient. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] pioneers "coordinated link sharing behavior" analysis across 2018/2019 Italian elections, arguing disinformation research must shift toward ecological, action-based detection; [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends this into a continuously updating workflow applied to the 2022 Italian snap election, surfacing hyperpartisan M5S networks, clickbait operations laundered through religious pages, and even religious-proselytism bot networks. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] complements this with a UK 2019 study insisting coordination is a continuum, not a binary, and that coordination and automation are orthogonal — the most tightly coordinated actors were small activist fringes, not mainstream parties. [[Kansaon2025-id]] transplants this logic to WhatsApp in Brazil, showing coordinated messaging translated into real-world mobilization around January 8th, while [[Oprea2025-lf]] documents hyperactive-user amplification on Facebook during Romania's 2024 EU campaign, and [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceptualizes influence operations in the Philippines as political brokerage rather than freelance disinformation. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] shows independent, volunteer-driven auditing can surface irregular ad practices where platform transparency fails — a concern about opacity that [[Votta2025-xz]] pushes further by interrogating how ad-delivery algorithms themselves (not just advertiser targeting) shape reach and cost, largely invisibly.

Adjacent to coordination sits the question of hyperpartisan attention economies. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] shows Italian populist parties (M5S, Lega) cultivate insular news ecosystems and that M5S uniquely mobilizes supporters to amplify favorable coverage while contesting unfavorable stories — evidence that populist success is intertwined with strategic exploitation of the attention economy, a theme [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] generalizes into three vectors (organizational blurring, microtargeting, strategic amplification) reshaping both practice and scholarship. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary analysis sharpens this into a specific claim: it is not populism or right-ideology alone but their conjunction — radical-right populism — that predicts misinformation sharing, linking elite strategy to a broader "disinformation order." [[Iris2026-pg]] shows a parallel dynamic on the demand/supply side of legacy media: Radical Right actors receive disproportionate visibility relative to their polling strength across five countries ahead of the 2024 EU elections, a structural bias independent of outlet orientation.

## National Case Studies: Narrative Contests and Strategic Deception

Several papers examine how political and media elites contest authority and truth during acute national moments. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] dissects how Brazilian parliamentarians pursued epistemic authority during the Manaus COVID crisis via distinct rhetorical postures (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, proximity-based brokerage), while [[Rossini2026-mj]] tracks how Bolsonaro's disinformation about voting systems eroded post-election trust even as mainstream news use bolstered it — together painting Brazil as a site where both elite narrative contestation and mass-level trust erosion are visible and measurable. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] offers a complementary lens on the US, arguing that election misinformation (2020, 2022) is sustained not by individual false claims but by collective "deep storytelling" performed jointly by influencers, elites, and audiences — a framework that helps explain why single-post misinformation audits (of the kind [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] undertakes at Facebook-wide scale) may miss contextual meaning-making even while precisely quantifying diffusion and virality.

The UK provides a parallel case in elite strategic deception: [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorizes "strategic lying" around Brexit and the 2019 election as an evolution of spin, weaponizing the correction cycle itself for amplification — a mechanism that resonates with [[Mosca2026-yh]]'s Italian "Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification" model, in which "fake news" accusations become rhetorical weapons in negative campaigning rather than genuine epistemic correctives, and with [[Balluff2026-bv]]'s quasi-experimental detection of covert media capture in Austria's "Inseratenaffäre," where government advertising secretly bought favorable coverage.

## Platform-Specific and Cross-Platform Campaigning

A growing subset addresses how campaigns adapt messaging to platform affordances. [[Chadwick2015-ii]] supplies the foundational hybrid-media-system framework underlying much of this work, while [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] applies it empirically to Italy's 2013 "second screen" talk-show politics, finding narrow, TV-anchored participation. More recent studies extend this to newer platforms and formats: [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailor securitizing visual rhetoric differently across Facebook/X versus TikTok, prioritizing memetic content for younger audiences; [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] uses multimodal LLMs to quantify concentrated visibility strategies on Instagram during the 2021 German election; [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] trace Scandinavian populist parties' negative-sentiment and anger-eliciting Facebook strategies and their engagement payoffs; [[Lukito2026-il]] documents differentiated cross-platform curation by right-leaning US outlets on Twitter versus Truth Social; [[Much2026-gu]] identifies "gendered media spaces" in podcasting that incidentally sorted young men toward conservative content in the 2024 US election; and [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] extends agenda-building theory to non-human actors, showing social bots meaningfully shaped 2022 Ohio campaign messaging while leaving the press largely unaffected. [[Schulte2026-df]] synthesizes this cross-platform turn methodologically, arguing the 2025 German election reveals not a shortage of studies but a lack of reusable, standardized infrastructure for multi-platform electoral observability.

## Methodological Infrastructure and the Access Problem

A final, self-reflexive cluster addresses the conditions of possibility for all this research. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] validate LLM-based pipelines (embeddings, classification, clustering) for Italian political content, grappling with the granularity and evaluation challenges of "LLM-in-the-loop" designs. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] step back to assess the regulatory and infrastructural landscape itself: both survey how the EU Digital Services Act has reshaped (and only partially restored) researcher data access after the Meta/CrowdTangle contraction, with Lukito's "price, proficiency, permission" framework diagnosing structural inequities — WEIRD-centric access, scarce consumption-side data, ephemeral platform goodwill — that shape which countries and questions get studied at all. Read together with [[Schulte2026-df]]'s infrastructural argument, this cluster suggests that the field's next challenge is less about finding new effects than about building durable, equitable, cross-platform observational capacity to keep studying them.
