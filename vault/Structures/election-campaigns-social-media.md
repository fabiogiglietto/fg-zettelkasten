# Election Campaigns on Social Media

## The Shape of the Field

This body of work traces a single, evolving question across a decade of national elections: how does the architecture of social media platforms—their algorithms, ad systems, coordination affordances, and data regimes—reshape who controls political attention and truth-claims during campaigns? The corpus moves from early hybrid-media accounts of Italy and the UK, through the Meta-collaboration studies of the US 2020 election, into a proliferating set of studies on coordinated networks, hyperpartisan pages, and generative-AI persuasion in Brazil, Germany, Austria, and beyond. Read together, the papers stage a running argument about whether platforms *cause* polarization and misinformation, or merely *channel* pre-existing partisan strategy.

## From Hybrid Media to Platformed Campaigning

The Italian case anchors much of this arc. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]] establish the founding premise—that TV and social media logics interpenetrate rather than substitute—showing that even high-volume Twitter activity around the 2013 Italian elections reflected narrow, TV-driven participation rather than genuine agenda-disruption. This hybrid-media lens recurs as connective tissue: [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] extrapolates it into a big-data era, identifying three vectors of transformation (blurred organizational boundaries, micro-targeted advertising, strategic amplification) that subsequent papers in this collection empirically pursue one by one. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] operationalizes this for the 2018 Italian election, showing that Five Star Movement supporters built insular news ecologies whose asymmetric share/comment dynamics let the "cyber party" strategically manage its own visibility—a finding that anticipates the corpus's recurring interest in populist parties' distinctive platform fluency, echoed later in Scandinavian ([[Kalsnes2025-zb]], [[Larsson2026-ro]]) and pan-European ([[Iris2026-pg]], [[Tornberg2025-ir]]) contexts.

## Coordination as the New Unit of Analysis

A second cluster shifts the analytic object from content to *behavior*. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] proposes coordinated link-sharing as an ecological alternative to content- or actor-based disinformation detection, validated on Italian 2018/2019 data and later scaled into a continuously updating workflow in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], which surfaces hyperpartisan M5S networks, religious clickbait operations, and proselytizing bot networks within the 2022 Italian snap election. This behavioral logic travels: [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] applies network-based coordination detection to the 2019 UK election, while [[Kansaon2025-id]] extends it into WhatsApp's encrypted ecosystem in Brazil, linking coordinated messaging directly to real-world mobilization around the January 8 insurrection. [[Oprea2025-lf]] complements this with a manual "Authenticity Matrix" applied to Romanian EU-election Facebook shares, and [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes the entire phenomenon sociologically, theorizing influence operations in the Philippines as political brokerage rather than isolated propaganda. Methodological innovation accompanies this substantive turn: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] test LLM-based embedding and classification pipelines for clustering Italian political news, arguing that generative AI can support—if carefully validated—this kind of large-scale coordination and narrative research, a concern about tooling and infrastructure that reappears in [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]].

## Attention, Advertising, and the Question of Effects

A third strand interrogates whether all this platform activity actually moves anyone. The Meta-collaboration studies on the US 2020 election form a tight, mutually referential set: [[Guess2023-ur]] and [[Guess2023-ai]] find that neither algorithmic feed removal nor reshare removal produced detectable attitude change despite substantially altering exposure; [[Allcott2025-jb]] extends this null pattern to political advertising itself, while [[Appel2026-qr]] shows that deceptive networks reached tens of millions but that correlations with downstream beliefs evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Freelon2020-yp]] steps back to interrogate the whole collaboration methodologically, arguing its precise-but-perishable findings cannot generalize and that governance must therefore be proactive rather than research-contingent. Against this null-effects consensus stand sharper counter-cases: [[Kim2026-wg]] finds real turnout suppression from geo-racially targeted 2016 ads once heterogeneous effects (not just averages) are examined, and [[Lin2025-xp]] demonstrates that conversational AI dialogues produce persuasion effects exceeding traditional ads across four national elections—suggesting that null findings for ads and feed algorithms may not generalize to more targeted or conversational forms of influence. [[Votta2025-xz]] adds a further wrinkle: ad delivery algorithms themselves, not just advertiser targeting, shape reach and cost, complicating any clean causal story about "exposure."

## Misinformation's Anatomy: Concentration, Not Ubiquity

Running beneath these debates is a consistent empirical motif: misinformation exposure and sharing are heavily concentrated, not evenly diffused. [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], and [[Guess2020-rr]] establish this for 2016 Twitter and Facebook data, finding fake-news consumption low overall but skewed toward small, older, highly partisan subpopulations—a pattern [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Appel2026-qr]] reconfirm at Facebook-wide scale for 2020, and [[Yang2023-cg]] extends into the visual domain, finding that a quarter of sampled political images carried misinformation. [[Allcott2017-yz]] frames the underlying economics. This concentration finding recurs as a methodological corrective throughout the corpus: effects that look negligible on average (as in [[Kim2026-wg]]) or diffuse in aggregate (as in [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment) turn out to be powerfully consequential for specific targeted or highly engaged subgroups, suggesting that null-effect findings in this literature are often an artifact of aggregation rather than genuine absence of influence.

## Elite Strategy: Lying, Media Capture, and Epistemic Authority

A distinct but converging thread treats misinformation not as an emergent platform pathology but as deliberate elite communication strategy. [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorizes "strategic lying" in Brexit and the 2019 UK election as an evolution of spin, where rebuttals paradoxically amplify falsehoods. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] generalizes this across 26 countries, showing that misinformation-sharing is not explained by populism or right-wing ideology alone but specifically by their conjunction in radical-right populism. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents Brazilian parliamentarians contesting journalistic gatekeeping directly during the Manaus COVID crisis, while [[Balluff2026-bv]] uncovers covert media capture in Austria's advertising-for-coverage scandal, and [[Mosca2026-yh]] theorizes "fake news" accusations themselves as rhetorical weapons of negative campaigning in the Italian press. [[Rossini2026-mj]] closes this thread by tracing how Bolsonaro's disinformation about voting systems eroded electoral trust differentially depending on citizens' informational pathways—reaffirming mainstream journalism's role as a resilience mechanism even among the incumbent's own supporters. Prochaska2025-lf complements this with a narrative-level account of how election-fraud "deep stories" are sustained collectively across the 2020–2022 US cycle, showing that manipulation often lies in *how* stories are told, not just their factual content.

## Visibility, Salience, and Media Attention

A parallel concern is not falsity but *disproportion*—which parties and candidates get seen. [[Iris2026-pg]] finds the Radical Right systematically overrepresented in European news coverage relative to electoral strength across five countries, while [[Eady2025-vm]] shows that ideologically extreme legislators dominate elite news-sharing in the US, skewing the visible political information environment toward polarization. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] argues methodologically that such visibility studies need multi-level (story/outlet/content) mapping rather than crude outlet-level partisanship scores. Platform-specific visual strategy is examined by [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] (German candidates on Instagram) and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] (Sweden Democrats' cross-platform securitizing memes), both showing that concentrated visibility and platform-tailored aesthetics are deliberate campaign choices, not incidental. [[Lukito2026-il]] extends this to cross-platform curation of right-leaning US outlets between Twitter and Truth Social, and [[Much2026-gu]] introduces "gendered media spaces" to explain how Trump's 2024 podcast strategy achieved incidental ideological sorting among young men without targeting ideology directly.

## Infrastructure and Access as the Meta-Problem

Finally, a self-reflexive cluster diagnoses the field's own methodological constraints. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] survey how the EU Digital Services Act reshapes—but only partially repairs—platform data access, with the latter's "price, proficiency, permission" framework explaining structural inequities in who can do this research at all. [[Schulte2026-df]] demonstrates concretely, via the 2025 German election, how scraping constraints and API limits bias what can be studied on TikTok versus X. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] shows crowdsourced volunteer auditing as a stopgap for Brazilian ad transparency. Together with [[Freelon2020-yp]]'s critique of the Meta2020 partnership, these papers argue that the central bottleneck in election-and-social-media research is no longer conceptual but infrastructural: without durable, equitable, cross-platform data regimes, the substantive questions raised throughout this topic—about coordination, persuasion, visibility, and elite strategy—will remain perpetually reactive to platforms' own shifting terms of access.
