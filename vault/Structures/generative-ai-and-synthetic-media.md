# Generative AI and Synthetic Media

## Synthetic Visual Persuasion and the Aesthetics of Deception

The most concrete evidence in this collection concerns what generative AI actually looks like when deployed for persuasion. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] offers the sharpest empirical anchor: a hybrid LLM/embedding pipeline applied to over 2,300 images from coordinated Facebook gambling groups shows an exponential, structurally-detected surge in posting volume immediately following ChatGPT's November 2022 launch. Crucially, the paper argues GenAI does not invent new persuasion logics but *intensifies and recombines* pre-existing drivers — aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, cultural localization — through a hyper-real, dreamlike visual register that lets industrial-scale networks exploit gaps between Meta's paid-ad and organic-content governance. [[Manovich2026-ih]] provides a complementary, medium-theoretic account of exactly this aesthetic: generative image models are "structurally conservative," biased toward familiar academic-photographic conventions and resistant to genuine defamiliarization — which helps explain why AI-generated persuasive imagery reads as uncannily polished rather than alien, and why it blends so easily into existing genres of manipulation rather than replacing them.

This "intensification, not replacement" thesis is tested experimentally by [[Hameleers2026-mc]], whose pre-registered study directly compares textual, AI-image, and decontextualized-video disinformation across two issues. The finding that visual disinformation only outperforms text when *ample authentic footage already exists* (Ukraine) but not otherwise (MH370) pushes back against alarmist assumptions that synthetic imagery is inherently more persuasive — a useful corrective that should be read alongside Giglietto's finding that AI content proliferates by volume and cultural fit rather than by any singular persuasive superiority. [[Choi2026-bz]] adds a psychological mechanism operating downstream of exposure: difficulty authenticating one deepfake carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to shape confidence and vulnerability toward *later* fake content — suggesting that repeated encounters with synthetic media reshape audiences' discernment capacities cumulatively rather than case-by-case. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] and [[Emilio2026-ik]] extend the visual-persuasion problem into consequential, real-world harm: Schiffrin's policy brief maps the deepfake financial-fraud "scam ecosystem" and argues that regulation must target platform/telecom/banking gatekeepers rather than individual victims, while Ferrara's "Generative AI Paradox" essay generalizes the concern into "synthetic realities" — layered fabrications of content, identity, interaction, and institutional trust — culminating in the paradox that ubiquitous synthetic media may make societies rationally discount *all* digital evidence.

## Coordinated Campaigns: From Bot Networks to Agentic Swarms

A second arc traces how generative and agentic AI reshape coordinated inauthentic behavior itself. [[Orlando2025-ul]] is the pivotal methodological contribution here: using generative agent-based modeling, it shows that LLM agents *spontaneously* reproduce hallmark IO coordination signatures (network densification, narrative homogenization, synchronized amplification) — and, strikingly, that merely making agents aware of their teammates' identities produces coordination nearly as strong as explicit collective deliberation. This finding gives empirical teeth to the more speculative claims in [[Schroeder2026-im]], which theorizes "malicious AI swarms" fusing LLM reasoning with multi-agent architectures to manufacture consensus, poison training data ("LLM grooming"), and erode institutional trust at a qualitatively new scale beyond IRA-era botnets. Both papers converge on a key normative point: symmetric "defensive" AI cannot simply counter malicious swarms, because the attention economy structurally rewards the manipulative tactics that ethical actors are constrained from using.

Detection research, meanwhile, is racing to catch up with platform-specific affordances. [[Luceri2025-tr]] shows that CIB-detection methods built for text-centric platforms (Twitter/Facebook) do not transfer cleanly to TikTok's video-first structure, while [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies *sound* itself as an underexamined, platform-specific propaganda vector in TikTok's Ukraine-war disinformation, with #Ukraine content amassing 36.9 billion views in three months. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] complicates the picture of TikTok as a uniformly hostile space: in the Canadian immigration debate, pro-immigration content actually outweighs anti-immigration content, though generative AI is beginning to enable racist "speculative worldbuilding" (AI-imagined dystopian futures) as a new misinformation register within an otherwise pluralistic platform vernacular. [[Nangle2026-yo]] extends the coordination question to ideology and affect rather than falsity per se, showing how far-right Instagram accounts use humor, memes, and algorithmic proximity to "soften" extremist content — a reminder that synthetic-media-adjacent normalization strategies operate through affect and platform logics as much as through fabricated facts.

## Does Generative AI Actually Persuade People?

A cluster of experimental papers asks the underlying causal question these threat models assume: can AI actually change minds better than humans? [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] and [[Lin2025-xp]] both answer, with unusual rigor, yes — and via mechanisms that reframe the disinformation debate. Hackenburg's tournament pitting frontier AI against professional canvassers and world-champion debaters finds AI reliably more persuasive, with the advantage traced not to rapport or humanness but to sheer *information throughput* (fact density); constraining AI to human writing speed erases the gap entirely. Lin's multi-country election experiments corroborate this: AI persuasion again operates through the "central route" of relevant facts and evidence rather than manipulation, is not dependent on personalization, and is *more* effective on out-party voters — challenging assumptions about entrenched motivated reasoning. Yet Lin also surfaces an asymmetry with clear disinformation-landscape implications: AI models arguing for right-leaning candidates across three countries produced systematically less accurate claims, echoing broader concerns (raised in [[Waight2026-ts]] and [[Beacken2026-zb]]) about structural, not just technical, sources of political bias in generative systems. Together these two papers suggest the "threat" of AI persuasion is less about deception than about scale and asymmetric accuracy — a more empirically grounded but arguably more unsettling picture than the swarm-based scenarios above.

## Structural and Geopolitical Shaping of AI Outputs

A third strand shifts from what AI *does* to disinformation toward what shapes AI *itself*. [[Waight2026-ts]] demonstrates concretely that state-coordinated media is memorized into training corpora and measurably tilts commercial LLM outputs toward pro-regime valence when queried in lower-media-freedom languages — a "laundering" mechanism that operationalizes concerns about institutional capture of supposedly neutral tools. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s large-scale audit of Grokipedia provides a parallel case study at the level of a single actor: Musk's AI encyclopedia is shown to be heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically more likely to cite blacklisted and unreliable sources, especially on controversial and political topics, revealing how ideological intent can be embedded in ostensibly automated knowledge infrastructure. [[Beacken2026-zb]] generalizes this structural argument comparatively, arguing against technologically deterministic accounts: GenAI propaganda adoption in Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, and Turkey varies according to local political-economic conditions rather than following a uniform trajectory — a useful corrective to narratives (implicit in some swarm and synthetic-reality papers) that treat GenAI's effects as everywhere the same. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s Kenya-based experiment closes this arc empirically by showing that deepfake impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated through audience comments and partisan motivated reasoning — reinforcing Beacken's structural point at the level of reception rather than production, and extending disinformation research's geographic center of gravity beyond North America and Europe.

## Trust, Truth, and Epistemic Security

Running beneath the empirical papers is a set of more conceptual interventions asking what "truth" and "trust" even mean once generation replaces retrieval. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues that fact-checking's traditional categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot capture AI outputs, proposing "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category demanding relational, indicator-based evaluation rather than binary verification — a framework that dovetails with [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s call to shift research priorities from artifact-level authenticity toward measurable "epistemic security." [[Suk2026-ai]] situates this shift historically, proposing a multilevel framework (individual/institutional/societal) for trust in generative AI built on decades of mass- and social-media trust scholarship — a useful scaffold for the two empirical trust studies in the set. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that Swiss audiences rate AI-generated, AI-assisted, and human-written news as equivalent in quality when authorship is undisclosed, and disclosure produces only a short-term curiosity bump rather than durable acceptance; [[Mattis2026-gu]] refines this further by disaggregating disclosure by journalistic task, finding fact-checking disclosures incur the steepest trust penalty while idea/image generation incur the smallest, with heterogeneous audience segments rather than a single "AI-averse public." Read together with Dierickx and Ferrara, these papers suggest the disinformation problem is increasingly inseparable from a broader crisis of *how audiences and institutions verify anything at all* in an environment of probabilistic, emergent outputs.

## The Broader Transformation: Platforms, Discourse, and the Post-Social Turn

Finally, several papers step back to ask how generative AI is reconfiguring the media ecosystem and its public discourse about itself. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] offers the most ambitious reframing: the "social media" paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication — with generative AI's "platforms without users" severing the constitutive link between platforms and human content production. [[Baym2026-tr]]'s decade-later retrospective corroborates this pessimistic trajectory empirically, showing how data extraction, algorithmic opacity, and now generative AI training on user content have compounded rather than resolved the harms she identified in 2015. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] and [[Stanusch2026-ec]] turn to how AI itself is discursively constructed: Nguyen's content analysis of 18,000+ news articles finds mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs are real but far from dominant, serving critique as often as hype, while Stanusch's controversy-mapping of the Altman firing identifies industry strategies of "premediation" and "preclusion" that displace present accountability onto speculative futures — both papers showing that the discursive terrain on which disinformation debates unfold is itself actively shaped by industry and journalistic framing, not a neutral backdrop. [[Manovich2026-ih]] closes the arc by insisting that any account of generative AI's social effects should be paired with attention to its properties *as a medium* — its built-in "media cognition," its conservatism, its entanglement of style and content — a reminder that the persuasive and disinformation-relevant power of synthetic media is inseparable from the specific affordances and historical baggage of the models producing it.
