# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The anniversary mood: naming what social media has become

The occasion for much of this literature is quite literal: journals and scholars marking ten- or twenty-year anniversaries and asking whether the vocabulary coined at social media's founding still describes anything real. [[Boyd2026-op]] delivers the sharpest verdict, proposing that "social media" be retired in favor of "parasocial media" — platforms now reward scrolling over posting, influencer content over peer exchange, and one-sided attachment over reciprocity. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] arrives at a structurally similar diagnosis through a different route, arguing that TikTok's "clustered publics" have supplanted boyd's own earlier concept of "networked publics," replacing explicit interpersonal connection with algorithmically inferred interest-similarity. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] generalizes both moves into a paradigm-level claim: the "social media" concept itself is exhausted, dissolving into three post-social formations — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication — each demanding its own methods and normative frame. Read together, these three pieces trace a single argument from symptom to system: boyd names the affective consequence, Gerbaudo specifies the algorithmic mechanism, and Törnberg and coauthors generalize it into a call for a genuinely post-social media studies. [[Marwick2026-ss]] supplies the emotional counterpoint and historical baseline against which this decline is measured, using her own LiveJournal years to show what reciprocal, intimate, small-scale sociality actually felt like before consolidation — a memory that haunts nearly every essay in this set as the road not taken.

## Political economy: extraction, scams, and the broligarchy

If the first cluster asks *what social media has become*, a second asks *why*, insisting the transformation was not technologically inevitable but the product of specific economic choices. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits her own 2015 diagnosis of four threats — wealth inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious creative labor, unchecked data extraction — and finds all four intensified, now compounded by generative AI's appropriation of user content and the political ascendance of tech "broligarchs." [[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes the extractive logic to its most literal conclusion, arguing that scams are no longer peripheral criminal noise but a *central organizing logic* of platform economies: anti-fraud infrastructures double as surveillance and data-governance regimes, and the boundary between "scam" and "legitimate hustle" has become a site of active political-economic boundary-work rather than a stable moral line. [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Bruns2026-yv]] both locate the origin of this drift in governance failure and commercial capture rather than user behavior — Marwick pointing to the loss of infrastructures of care, Bruns showing how Twitter's leadership abandoned its "barrier-free conversation" mission for revenue well before Musk's acquisition accelerated its "enshittification." [[Helmond2026-ll]] historicizes the whole arc from the vantage of her own decade-old coinage, arguing that "platformization" itself needs recalibration: the retirement of Facebook's Like button marks the symbolic end of the social-graph paradigm and the pivot toward AI-inferred personalization, with Big Tech now extending platform logics into wholly new sectors (space, defense, energy) under the banner of "Big AI."

## Twitter's death as paradigm case

Twitter/X functions across this literature as the emblematic case study for the whole arc of decline, and two pieces treat it as an object in its own right. Bastos2025-ya offers an infrastructural retrospective on "Twitter studies" as a research subfield built on unusually open API access, mourning the loss of that scholarly commons as much as the platform's transformation. [[Bruns2026-yv]] extends this into a fuller political history, tracing Twitter's flat, open network structure — valuable for crisis reporting, hashtag activism, and elite-public interaction — through commercial mismanagement, algorithmic amplification of right-wing content, and Musk-era collapse, then turning a skeptical eye on successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky), which he argues largely clone Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. Both essays converge with Marwick's and Baym's broader claims: what died with Twitter was not merely a product but a research infrastructure and a mode of public discourse whose replacement remains unresolved.

## How platforms manage — and obscure — their own decline

A more empirical strand examines the discursive and operational mechanics by which platforms narrate and administer this transformation. [[De2026-ld]] introduces "changecraft" to describe how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively stage infrastructural and ideological pivots as continuity, creator benevolence, or democratization, even as they consolidate power and deepen user dependency — an empirical demonstration of exactly the depoliticized self-presentation that the anniversary essays diagnose from the outside. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] grounds the political-economy critique in hard governance data, showing that DSA-mandated transparency reveals stark linguistic inequities in human moderation, with Global South languages chronically under-resourced relative to English — a concrete instance of the extractive, unevenly distributed infrastructure that Swartz, Baym, and Helmond describe more abstractly.

## Platformization's next frontier: AI hype as the new political economy

Several papers extend the platformization narrative directly into the discourse and political economy of generative AI, treating it as the sector into which platform logics — and platform critique — must now migrate. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] offers the most systematic reframing, arguing AI is best understood as a "Project" of world-building capital rather than a technology, and identifying five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that misdirect accountability efforts away from network power and financialization — a direct descendant of Baym's and Helmond's political-economic skepticism, now aimed at AI rather than social platforms. [[Dodds2026-df]] performs an analogous move for journalism, treating "AI hype" as a systemic infrastructure that redistributes legitimacy and resources rather than mere narrative, echoing changecraft's insight that platforms actively stage their own transformations rather than simply concealing them. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]] map the imaginaries sustaining this hype empirically: Stanusch et al. show how the Altman controversy surfaced industry strategies of "premediation" and "preclusion" that absorb critique into speculative futures, while Hepp's ethnography of the Bay Area's "quiet futuring" locates the ideational infrastructure — Effective Altruism, Rationalism, pioneer communities — that feeds those loud industry narratives. [[Richter2026-bt]] closes the arc with a longitudinal, comparative account of who actually drove AI discourse before the GenAI boom, finding US individualization (tech-influencer dominance) against German institutionalization — a finding that helps explain, in miniature, why the US platform economy that Baym, Boyd, and Swartz describe has proven so susceptible to consolidated, personality-driven power, while offering a comparative counterpoint suggesting alternative trajectories were and remain possible.

## The shape of the argument

Across these sixteen pieces, a single narrative arc emerges: an origin story of reciprocal, intimate, open sociality (Marwick, Bastos); a mechanism of transformation via algorithmic clustering, parasocial engagement, and post-social fragmentation (Gerbaudo, Boyd, Törnberg); a political-economic explanation rooted in extraction, scam logics, and platformization's expansion (Baym, Swartz, Helmond); an emblematic case of collapse (Bruns, Bastos); empirical evidence of how platforms manage and unevenly govern that collapse (De, Tonneau); and finally a forward migration of the same critical apparatus onto the AI industry now absorbing platform capital, discourse, and imaginaries (Vertesi, Dodds, Stanusch, Hepp, Richter). The overall thrust is diagnostic rather than nostalgic: none of these authors believe the current trajectory was technologically necessary, and most end — explicitly (Baym, Marwick) or implicitly (Vertesi, Bruns) — with a call to redirect scholarship from critique toward intervention, before the same extractive and hype-driven logics that reshaped social media fully consume the AI economy now rising in its place.
