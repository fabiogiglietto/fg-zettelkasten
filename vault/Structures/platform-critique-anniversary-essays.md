# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The diagnosis: has "social" media stopped being social?

The anchor of this topic is a cluster of anniversary essays — many written for *Social Media + Society*'s twentieth-anniversary issue — that converge on a single, unsettling observation: the thing scholars spent two decades calling "social media" no longer behaves the way the name implies. [[Boyd2026-op]] states the case most bluntly, proposing that the field retire "social media" for "parasocial media," since feeds now reward scrolling over posting and one-sided attachment to influencers over reciprocal exchange. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] arrives at a structurally similar conclusion from a different angle, arguing that TikTok's "clustered publics" — statistically inferred neighborhoods of shared interest — have supplanted the "networked publics" of the friending-and-following era, shifting the unit of circulation from people to items. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] synthesizes and radicalizes both moves, declaring the "social media" paradigm itself obsolete and proposing three successor formations (algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, AI-mediated communication) that no longer share a common analytic core — citing, tellingly, Meta's own admission that only 7–17% of feed time involves content from people one follows.

Where these structural diagnoses risk technological determinism, [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Baym2026-tr]] insist on political-economic contingency: nothing about recommender systems or scale required the loss of intimacy. Marwick's autoethnography of LiveJournal recovers a counter-memory of reciprocal, vulnerable, small-scale sociality that today's platforms "actively foreclose," while Baym revisits her own 2015 manifesto to show that the four threats she named then — inequality, opaque algorithms, precarious labor, data extraction — have not just persisted but metastasized into "broligarchy." [[Helmond2026-ll]] supplies the infrastructural hinge connecting these accounts: the retirement of Facebook's Like button literalizes the shift Gerbaudo and Törnberg describe, marking Meta's decoupling from the social-graph paradigm and its reorientation toward AI-driven inference — while also warning that "platformization" itself, the concept she coined a decade ago, has been diluted by overuse and needs re-anchoring around the notion of making domains "platform-ready."

## Scams, extraction, and the political economy beneath the interface

A second thread pushes past questions of sociality into political economy proper, arguing that today's platforms are organized around extractive and outright illegitimate economic logics. [[Swartz2026-zb]] is the sharpest statement here: scams are not peripheral criminal noise but a "central organizing logic" of platform economies, with anti-fraud infrastructure doubling as a governance mechanism that legitimizes surveillance. This dovetails with [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" — the discursive labor platforms perform to narrate monetization and control shifts as creator benevolence or community-driven democratization — and with [[Vertesi2026-lv]]'s account of AI as a "Project" of world-building capital that deploys rhetorical "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory framings) to keep critics chasing artifacts rather than the underlying assemblage of financialized power. Read together, Swartz, De, and Vertesi describe a single phenomenon at different altitudes: boundary-blurring extraction (Swartz), the discourse that naturalizes it (De), and the capital networks that benefit from both (Vertesi). [[Helmond2026-ll]]'s closing gesture toward "Big AI" — Nvidia, Anduril, Palantir, satellite compute — is effectively Vertesi's Project of AI observed from the vantage of platform studies rather than STS.

## The death of Twitter, and the erosion of a research object

A more elegiac register runs through papers mourning Twitter specifically, both as a public good and as scholarly infrastructure. [[Bruns2026-yv]] traces the platform's arc from a uniquely open, flat network good for hashtag activism and crisis reporting to its "enshittification" under commercial pressure and then Musk's ownership, and is skeptical that Mastodon, Threads, or Bluesky have solved the underlying moderation and governance failures rather than merely cloning Twitter's form. Bastos2025-ya narrates the same rupture from the researcher's side: the loss of Twitter's API ecosystem (REST, Streaming, Academic, Compliance) that had underwritten an entire subfield of "Twitter studies," particularly in political communication. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] sits adjacent to this discussion, asking empirically whether the alternative platforms that emerged from this fragmentation actually drive discursive polarization — a question Bruns raises polemically and Kristensen approaches with data, though the surviving abstract leaves the findings thin.

## Platforms, polarization, and democratic accountability

A parallel line asks what all this means for democracy. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] states directly that platforms bear responsibility for global democratic backsliding. [[Bennett2025-xs]] offers the most theoretically ambitious account, arguing that the dominant "technocentric" paradigm (misinformation, algorithmic amplification) and the "institutionalist" paradigm (parties, elites, surrogate organizations) have talked past each other, and proposing "digital surrogate organizations" as the missing bridge — networks like QAnon or Stop the Steal that pull parties toward illiberalism through organizationally-enabled connective action rather than mere persuasion. This is a productive complication of the technocentrism that [[Munger2025-cz]] separately interrogates from a metascience angle: reviewing the Meta2020 partnership, Munger argues that even the most rigorous platform experiments lack "temporal validity" (platforms change too fast for findings to generalize) and "poetic validity" (core concepts like "echo chamber" are operationalized inconsistently), concluding that reactive social science cannot keep pace with platforms and that proactive regulatory disclosure is needed instead. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] converges on a similar regulatory pivot from a different direction, arguing — in the DSA era — that causal-effects research is the wrong paradigm altogether and that scholars should study platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure. [[Goldberg2026-eb]], the most constructive entry in this set, surveys how LLM-enabled technologies (collective dialogue systems, bridging systems, community moderation, proof-of-humanity) might reshape digital public squares for the better, while cataloguing the same access, surveillance, and information-distortion failures that motivate Lewandowsky's and Bennett's alarm.

## Whose evidence? Industry capture of the research apparatus

Underlying Munger's and Bechmann's methodological worries is a more institutional problem: platforms control the very data needed to study them, and that dependency compromises the evidentiary base for regulation. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this explicitly through analogy to tobacco, pharma, and food science, identifying restricted data access, selective funding, and institutional entanglement as structural vulnerabilities exposed by the Meta2020 case Munger dissects. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] supplies the empirical teeth for this concern, finding that roughly half of high-profile social media research in top journals carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small elite of scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping — yielding an estimated "industrial saturation" of about 80% of the literature, and a topical bias toward user-focused questions (misinformation sharing) rather than platform-scale dynamics. Together, Heiss and Bak-Coleman argue that the field's empirical authority on the very questions Lewandowsky, Bennett, and Goldberg raise is compromised at the root.

## The AI turn: the next platformization

The final arc gathers papers treating generative AI as the successor terrain onto which all the above dynamics are migrating. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] and [[Helmond2026-ll]] both mark AI as the inflection point that dissolves the social-media paradigm and reorganizes Big Tech around "Big AI." [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] offers a granular case study of this shift in the search domain, showing how Google's AI Overviews are discursively contested by Google, journalists, and SEO marketers — with the user's own voice and questions of environmental or democratic cost conspicuously absent from all three discourses, an absence that echoes Vertesi's "decoys" and De's "changecraft." [[Dodds2026-df]] extends the critique to journalism itself, arguing that AI hype is not discourse but *infrastructure* — a systemic force redistributing legitimacy and resources — while noting that hype renders invisible the same hidden labor and material costs that recur across this literature. [[Hepp2026-oi]] grounds these abstractions ethnographically, distinguishing Silicon Valley's "loud futuring" (CEO hype) from a "quiet futuring" among labs, EA/Rationalist communities, and pioneer groups that supplies the ideational reservoir — AGI arrival, job loss, geopolitical race — that loud futuring amplifies, and that presents itself as apolitical while being constitutively political. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] closes the loop by insisting that all of this — hype, discourse, imaginaries — must be read through the "Project of AI's" underlying financialization and network power, the same warning against mistaking symptom for cause that runs through the whole topic, from Swartz's scams to Bak-Coleman's captured science.

Read as a single arc, these twenty-two pieces trace a decade in which the "social" promise of social media curdled into scam, spectacle, and surveillance (Swartz, Marwick, Boyd, Baym); in which the platforms and their scholarly observers became mutually entangled to the point of epistemic compromise (Heiss, Bak-Coleman, Munger, Bastos); and in which the object of study is now dissolving again, under AI, into new configurations — clustered publics, parasocial feeds, quiet futuring, AI Overviews — that the field's inherited vocabulary of "networked publics" and "platformization" can only partially name.
