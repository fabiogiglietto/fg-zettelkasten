# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The Unraveling of "Social" Media

A striking convergence runs through this year's anniversary and retrospective essays: the vocabulary invented to describe Web 2.0 sociality no longer fits what platforms have become. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has displaced posting and that reciprocal peer exchange has given way to one-sided attention toward influencers and algorithmically curated content. [[Marwick2026-ss]] arrives at a kindred diagnosis from the inside, contrasting her LiveJournal-era experience of intimacy and reciprocal writing with the alienation and surveillance of contemporary platforms, insisting that this trajectory was a political-economic choice, not a technological inevitability. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits her own decade-old manifesto and finds all four of her original threats—inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction—not merely persisting but intensifying, crystallized in the figure of the "broligarch." [[Tornberg2026-lc]] pushes furthest toward conceptual rupture, arguing the entire "social media" paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations: algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication, each demanding distinct analytic tools. Where boyd and Marwick mourn a lost sociality and Baym calls for engaged intervention, Törnberg's essay is the most structurally ambitious, treating the shift from social-graph to interest-graph recommendation (evidenced starkly by Meta's own admission that only 7–17% of feed time involves followed accounts) as the hinge of a paradigm change rather than a mere degradation.

[[Helmond2026-ll]] and [[Bruns2026-yv]] supply institutional and infrastructural specificity to this broader mood. Helmond, revisiting the concept she coined a decade ago, uses the retirement of Facebook's Like button and Google's satellite-based "Project Suncatcher" to argue that platformization is entering an AI-centered phase in which the open web's behavioral signals are being superseded by in-app inference and AI-driven personalization—while warning that "platformization" itself has become analytically diluted through overuse. Bruns narrates Twitter/X's specific arc from flat, open network to Musk-accelerated "enshittification," and is skeptical that Mastodon, Threads, or Bluesky represent genuine alternatives rather than reproductions of Twitter's unresolved moderation failures. Read together with Törnberg's typology, these essays suggest the field is converging on a shared story: the "social" affordances that defined the 2006–2016 platform decade (open graphs, reciprocity, chronological circulation) have been systematically dismantled in favor of algorithmic, AI-inflected, and increasingly closed architectures.

## Extraction, Scam, and Changecraft: The Political Economy Underneath

A second cluster interrogates the economic logics that produced this transformation. [[Swartz2026-zb]] offers perhaps the most conceptually generative move, arguing that scams are not peripheral to platform economies but a central organizing logic—"network scams" in which believer, shill, and victim roles blur, and where anti-fraud infrastructures double as governance mechanisms that authorize surveillance and define economic citizenship. This reframes the platform decline narrated by boyd and Marwick not as decay but as the maturation of an extractive logic always latent in the system. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] extends this political-economy lens to AI specifically, characterizing AI development as a "Project" of world-building through which capital and infrastructure are assembled, and identifying five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that redirect accountability discourse away from the underlying concentration of network power—a warning that resonates with Swartz's point that boundary-work around legitimacy (what counts as "scam" versus "innovation") is itself a mechanism of capital consolidation.

[[De2026-ld]] supplies the discursive mechanics by which platforms manage and legitimate this ongoing extraction, coining "changecraft" to describe how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X narrate infrastructural and ideological shifts as continuity, creator benevolence, or democratization, masking power consolidation behind an aesthetics of progress. This dovetails with Helmond's account of platformization's "collaborative" unfolding through third parties drawn in by promises of mutual benefit, and with Bruns's account of Twitter's rhetorical drift from open-conversation idealism to revenue-speak. Meanwhile [[Bennett2025-xs]] and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] push this political-economy diagnosis toward its societal stakes, arguing that platform dynamics are implicated in democratic backsliding—Bennett and Livingston via a "communication-as-organization" framework in which digital surrogate organizations (QAnon, Stop the Steal networks) pull parties toward illiberalism, and Lewandowsky more polemically insisting platforms must be held accountable for enabling authoritarian trends. Together these pieces insist that the "death of social media" story is inseparable from a story about capital, governance, and power consolidation—not just changing user behavior.

## AI as the New Platform Substrate

A third arc traces how generative AI is folded into, and reconfigures, the platform order the other essays describe. Törnberg and Helmond both identify AI-mediated communication as a genuinely new formation—"platforms without users," in Törnberg's phrase—severing the historic link between platforms and human-generated content. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] offers a granular case study of this shift in the search domain, showing how Google's AI Overviews are discursively contested by Google, journalists, and SEO marketers, with Google straddling "commercial platform" and "public information infrastructure" roles while user and environmental concerns are conspicuously absent from all three actors' framings. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]] turn to the industry's own self-narration: Stanusch shows how the Altman firing/rehiring controversy surfaced dominant imaginaries (Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, Techno-Hagiography) that the industry deploys via "premediation" and "preclusion" to occupy the future and absorb present critique, while Hepp's ethnography of the Bay Area distinguishes this "loud futuring" from a "quiet futuring" among labs and Effective Altruist/Rationalist communities that supplies the ideational reservoir loud futuring later amplifies. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] complements these industry-side accounts with a media-side one, finding that journalistic coverage of LLMs is dominated by a techno-capitalist master frame and concentrated overwhelmingly on ChatGPT/OpenAI, with mentalistic-agentic framing present but far from hegemonic. Vertesi's decoy framework arguably supplies the connective theory here: Stanusch's imaginaries, Hepp's quiet futuring, and Nguyen's media frames can each be read as sites where AI's political economy is simultaneously obscured and legitimated through recurring rhetorical moves (existential risk, inevitability, safety-washing).

## The Erosion of the Research Infrastructure Itself

A final, more reflexive strand concerns what these transformations mean for the scholarly apparatus documenting them. Bastos2025-ya and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] chart the collapse of open API access—from Twitter's relatively generous developer ecosystem to X's commercial paywall—with Giglietto and Puschmann showing this as a general platform pattern (CrowdTangle's closure, Reddit's pricing, TikTok's restrictions) and evaluating the EU's DSA Article 40 as a fragile, unevenly implemented attempt to convert data access from platform philanthropy into regulatory right. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] complicates any comforting assumption that independent academia has filled this gap, documenting that roughly half of high-profile social media research carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small set of repeatedly-engaged scholars and editors, and skewed toward user-focused rather than platform-scale questions—a finding that reads as an empirical instantiation of Vertesi's "decoy" thesis at the level of the scientific field itself. [[Moran2025-qn]] closes this arc by documenting the parallel institutional unraveling of professional trust-and-safety work under political and economic pressure, suggesting that the infrastructures of accountability—both scholarly and corporate—are eroding in tandem with the "social" fabric the other essays mourn.

## Synthesis

Taken together, these nineteen essays do not simply lament change; they converge on a shared claim that the past decade's platform trajectory—from open graphs to algorithmic feeds, from user-generated content to AI-mediated and AI-synthetic content, from data philanthropy to walled gardens—was authored by identifiable economic and political choices, is actively narrated and legitimated by the industry (changecraft, premediation, quiet futuring), and has hollowed out both the sociality platforms once enabled and the research infrastructure needed to study them. Where they diverge is in prescription: Marwick and boyd gesture toward smaller-scale, care-oriented alternatives; Baym calls for interdisciplinary, impact-oriented scholarship; Vertesi and Swartz insist on redirecting critique toward political economy and away from decoys; and Giglietto, Bastos, and Bak-Coleman insist that without structural guarantees of data access and research independence, even that redirected critique may remain compromised.
