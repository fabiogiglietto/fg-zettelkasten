# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The anniversary diagnosis: has "social media" ended?

A cluster of essays written for *Social Media + Society*'s twentieth-anniversary issue converges on a single verdict: the thing scholars have spent two decades calling "social media" no longer exists in the form that justified the name. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring the term altogether in favor of "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has replaced posting and algorithmically distributed influencer content has replaced peer reciprocity. [[Marwick2026-ss]] arrives at a kindred conclusion through autoethnography, contrasting LiveJournal's intimacy and granular privacy with the alienation of contemporary platforms, and insisting that this trajectory was a political-economic choice, not a technological inevitability. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits her own 2015 manifesto and finds every one of her original four threats—wealth inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction—intensified rather than resolved, now compounded by generative AI's appropriation of user content and the political ascendance of a tech "broligarchy." [[Helmond2026-ll]] performs a parallel retrospective on her own coinage of "platformization," arguing the term has been drained of analytic force through overuse and proposing it be redefined as a situated form of Manovich's "transcoding," newly visible in Meta's retirement of the Like button and Google's turn to space-based AI infrastructure. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] supply the structural vocabulary for what these essayists are describing from the inside: a shift from "networked publics" built on explicit interpersonal ties to algorithmically "clustered publics" and, further, to a fully "post-social" configuration of algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private enclaves, and AI-mediated communication that dissolves the user-generated-content/social-graph model altogether. Read together, these pieces trace one continuous arc—from Gillespie's foundational account of platforms' self-legitimating rhetoric ([[Gillespie2010-as]]) and van Dijck's framework of the contested "platform society" ([[van-Dijck2018-up]]), through a decade of platformization, to a present that several authors now describe as post-platform or AI-dominated ([[Tornberg2026-im]]).

## Political economy, extraction, and the scam as organizing logic

Running beneath the periodization essays is a harder claim about what has actually driven the change: an intensifying political economy of extraction. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] frames the platform society as a struggle between private gain and public value in which infrastructural monopolists embed American corporate values into sectors like news, transport, health, and education. [[Baym2026-tr]] and [[Marwick2026-ss]] update this account with figures on wealth concentration and generative AI's data appropriation. [[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes the argument furthest, arguing that social media has entered a "scam age" in which fraud is not peripheral criminality but a central organizing logic of platform economies—network scams, hustle culture, and anti-fraud infrastructures jointly constituting new forms of governance and economic citizenship. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes a cognate dynamic for misinformation: "misinformation-at-scale" is not a bug to be patched but a structural output of engagement-optimized, advertising-funded platforms, whose moderation regimes expanded opportunistically during COVID and the 2020 election before being dismantled once political cost outweighed benefit. [[Tornberg2026-im]] extends the extraction logic into the AI era, theorizing a shift from network-effect monopolies to "cognitive lock-in," in which rent is now extracted from dependence on personalized AI systems rather than from social graphs—an enclosure, he argues, of cognition itself rather than merely of sociality. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] generalizes this critique into a methodological warning: critiques of AI that fixate on ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, or regulatory "decoys" end up legitimating the very "Project of AI" they mean to hold accountable, when the real site of power is financialized infrastructure and network assembly.

## How platforms talk about themselves

A second strand examines the discursive machinery by which platforms manage their own legitimacy amid this extraction. [[Gillespie2010-as]] inaugurates the theme, showing how "platform" itself was chosen for its connotations of neutrality and openness. [[Petre2019-zh]] documents the mirror-image move, "platform paternalism," whereby platforms moralize the boundary between legitimate strategy and illegitimate "gaming" in ways that track their own commercial interests while disadvantaging resource-poor creators. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows Meta's Newsroom performing comparable discursive work, framing problematic content as an "outside" threat (coordinated inauthentic behavior) while erasing the role of its own Groups architecture and hyperpartisan power-users. [[De2026-ld]] names this family of practices "changecraft," identifying three strategies—making change visibly aesthetic, reframing ideological pivots as continuity, and patchworking cumulative restructuring—by which Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X narrate transformation as benevolent and participatory even as it consolidates power. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] traces an equivalent discursive contest over Google's AI Overviews, in which Google, journalists, and SEO marketers fight to define a reconfiguration of search as either seamless innovation or epistemic risk. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] closes this arc by exposing an entire governance mechanism—algorithmic "reduction" or demotion—that platforms have kept largely outside public and scholarly scrutiny precisely because it escapes the vocabulary built around removal-based content moderation.

## The erosion of independent knowledge about platforms

A distinct but related set of papers argues that researchers' capacity to even describe these transformations has been structurally compromised. [[Bruns2019-nr]] documents the post-Cambridge-Analytica "APIcalypse," in which platforms used privacy scandal as cover to choke off independent scholarly data access; Bastos2025-ya eulogizes the resulting loss of "Twitter studies" as a research infrastructure after the X rebrand. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its companion [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] extend this into a systematic account of industry capture of the scientific record itself, showing that roughly half of high-profile social media research carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small clique of repeatedly-engaged authors, editors, and reviewers, with industry-tied work skewed toward user-blaming topics and away from scrutiny of platform design. [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates this dependency alongside tobacco- and pharma-style industry influence and calls for DSA-style mandated data access. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a complementary meta-scientific critique from inside the system: even the celebrated Meta2020 academic partnership, he argues, produced numerically narrow, rapidly decaying findings whose external validity collapses given how fast platforms change—evidence, he concludes, that reactive empirical social science cannot govern platforms and that proactive, mandated self-disclosure is required instead.

## Platform-specific fractures: Twitter's death, TikTok's clusters, YouTube's limits

Several papers trace these macro-dynamics through the lived collapse of particular platforms. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter's arc from a uniquely open, prosocial public-debate infrastructure to its "enshittification" under Musk, arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) clone its form without solving its underlying moderation and governance failures. [[Wang2026-ub]] supplies empirical confirmation at the level of a specific migrating community: even highly motivated academic early-adopters largely failed to sustain their move to Mastodon, with retention hinging on niche server communities rather than network strength. [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] document how platform architecture itself outpaces moderation on TikTok and YouTube respectively—jihadist content embedding itself in TikTok's vernacular culture through audio camouflage and meme infiltration, and Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology" persisting across YouTube's recommendation system long after formal deplatforming—both cases illustrating Gillespie's point ([[Gillespie2022-jx]]) that governance of circulation, not just content, is now the real battleground. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes the underlying shift that enables such dynamics: TikTok's move from people-centric networked publics to item-centric clustered publics constructed from implicit behavioral signals, a model other platforms are now converging toward.

## Generative AI as the next rupture

Closing the arc, a substantial subset of the collection treats generative AI not as an addendum to platformization but as its next phase, continuous with yet rupturing the platform society. [[Tornberg2026-im]] and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] provide the overarching theoretical frames already noted. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers an empirical case study of this rupture's content politics, showing Grokipedia to be a largely derivative but ideologically skewed AI fork of Wikipedia that cites far more unreliable and "blacklisted" sources on controversial topics. [[Waight2026-ts]] and [[Richter2026-bt]] examine how state and corporate power already shape the discourse feeding these systems—state-controlled media measurably biasing LLM outputs, and starkly divergent US/German stakeholder ecologies on Twitter shaping each country's subsequent AI governance. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]] map the industry's own self-narration, from the "premediation" and "preclusion" strategies visible in the Altman controversy to the "quiet futuring" of Bay Area AI communities that supplies ideas later amplified by CEOs' "loud futuring." [[Dodds2026-df]] situates journalism's complicity in this hype-production, while [[Topinka2026-hb]] theorizes "slopaganda" as a new, affectively-rather-than-cognitively persuasive genre that AI's rhetorical attunement makes possible. Against this, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] stake out a more reformist register, proposing AI-enabled bridging and deliberation systems and demonstrating that disclosing persuasive *intent*—not mere AI identity—meaningfully blunts chatbot persuasion. Taken together with the Delphi-based anticipatory assessment of misinformation futures in [[Mahl2026-hc]], the long-run decline in public trust in news documented in [[Fletcher2026-lv]], the democratic-backsliding warning of [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]], and foundational accounts of the "disinformation order" ([[Bennett2018-ys]]) and platform-driven toxicity and polarization ([[Di-Domenico2026-zq]], [[Zhao2025-ny]], [[Kristensen2025-ni]]), this corner of the topic suggests that the platformization story is entering a new chapter whose scams, extractions, and epistemic risks are structurally continuous with the old one—now simply rerouted through generative infrastructures that are, if anything, even harder to see, audit, or hold accountable.
