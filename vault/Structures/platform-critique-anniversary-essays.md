# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The anniversary moment: why so many retrospectives now

A striking number of these papers are explicitly occasioned by anniversaries — Social Media + Society's twentieth-anniversary issue produces [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Marwick2026-ss]], [[Baym2026-tr]], and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] in close conversation, while [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits "platformization" a decade after coining it and Bastos2025-ya eulogizes Twitter's open-API era as a closed chapter of research infrastructure. This clustering is itself a symptom: a generation of scholars who built the vocabulary of social media studies — networked publics, participatory culture, platformization, surveillance capitalism — is now watching that vocabulary come apart from the objects it was built to describe. The shared move across these essays is diagnostic rather than celebratory: each asks not "what is social media doing to us" but "has social media, as a category, ceased to exist in the form we theorized it."

## From "social" to "parasocial" to "post-social"

The most direct expression of this rupture is terminological. [[Boyd2026-op]] argues that the "social" in social media is now a misnomer: feeds reward scrolling over posting and one-sided attention to influencers over reciprocal exchange, and she proposes retiring the term in favor of "parasocial media." [[Tornberg2026-lc]] generalizes this further, arguing that the entire social-media paradigm — built on user-generated content circulating through a social graph — is dissolving into three distinct "post-social" formations: algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated chatbot communication, each requiring different analytic tools. [[Marwick2026-ss]] supplies the autoethnographic counterpoint, using her own LiveJournal years to show what reciprocity and intimacy actually felt like before platforms optimized for scale, and insisting that today's alienation is a contingent political-economic outcome, not an inevitability. [[Helmond2026-ll]] triangulates these claims infrastructurally: Meta's retirement of the Like button is read as the symbolic end of the social-graph paradigm and the pivot toward AI-driven inference, a shift she folds into a redefinition of platformization itself as a transcoding process extending now into "Big AI" and even space-based compute (Google's Project Suncatcher). Together these four essays stage the same argument from four angles — terminological, infrastructural, affective, and methodological — that the platform configuration theorized circa 2010–2015 has given way to something else.

## Political economy, unfinished business

A second cluster insists that what has changed is less the technology than the intensification of political-economic dynamics already visible a decade ago. [[Baym2026-tr]] explicitly re-audits her own 2015 diagnosis — wealth inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction — and finds all four worse, crystallized in the "broligarchy" and in generative AI's appropriation of user-generated content as training fodder. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter's specific arc from prosocial, flat-network openness to Musk-era "enshittification," using Doctorow's framework (also invoked by [[Boyd2026-op]]) to show how mismanagement and commercial pressure, not technological necessity, hollowed out a once-valuable public-debate infrastructure — and finds that would-be successors (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) inherit the same governance failures rather than solving them. Bastos2025-ya narrows the lens to research infrastructure itself: Twitter's suite of APIs enabled an entire "Twitter studies" subfield, and its closure is a rupture for computational social science comparable to the platform ruptures these other essays describe for users. [[Petre2019-zh]], though earlier, supplies the conceptual hinge connecting this cluster to platform governance studies proper: "platform paternalism" names how platforms moralize the boundary between legitimate and illegitimate visibility-seeking in ways that mask their own material interests — a dynamic that anticipates and informs [[De2026-ld]]'s account of "changecraft," the discursive strategies (aestheticized infrastructure, continuity-framing, patchworking) through which Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X narrate constant change as benign and participatory while consolidating power.

## Scams, extraction, and the blurring of legitimacy

[[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes the political-economy argument into new territory by arguing that scams are not peripheral to platform economies but constitutive of them: the same anti-fraud infrastructures that govern "scam" also authorize surveillance and legitimate certain forms of capitalism (hustle culture, crypto, MLMs) over others. This essay reframes the "decline" narrative common to [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Boyd2026-op]] in economic rather than affective terms — what has eroded is not just reciprocity but the line between legitimate and illegitimate value extraction, with "network scams" replacing the dyadic con. Read alongside [[Petre2019-zh]]'s account of platforms policing "gaming" while gaming the system themselves, and [[De2026-ld]]'s changecraft, a consistent picture emerges: platforms have always needed a vocabulary of moral boundary-work (organic vs. artificial, scam vs. hustle, continuity vs. rupture) to legitimate extraction, and that vocabulary has only grown more elaborate as the underlying economy has become more exploitative.

## Theoretical anchors: platforms before the reckoning

Two foundational texts, chronologically prior to the retrospective wave but filed here as its necessary baseline, show how far the conversation has traveled. [[Gillespie2010-as]] established that "platform" itself was always a piece of discursive positioning — a term whose computational, architectural, figurative, and political connotations let YouTube claim neutrality while making substantive editorial interventions. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] scaled this insight to "the platform society," arguing platforms had penetrated the core of Western institutions (news, transport, health, education) and converted public-value questions into contests with corporate infrastructure. Both texts anticipated almost every move the 2026 retrospectives make: Gillespie's skepticism about platform neutrality rhymes with [[De2026-ld]] and [[Petre2019-zh]]; van Dijck's public-vs-private-value framing underwrites [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Baym2026-tr]]'s calls for infrastructures of care. What the newer essays add is the sense of an ending — that the platform-society configuration van Dijck described is itself now being superseded.

## The AI society and the political economy of hype

That supersession is theorized most directly by [[Tornberg2026-im]], which posits a tendential shift "from the platform society to the AI society": network-effect monopolies give way to "cognitive lock-in," predictive logics give way to generative ones, and platform-era struggles over visibility give way to "alignment politics." This essay functions as connective tissue between the retrospective cluster and a set of papers specifically diagnosing AI's political economy and discourse. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] argues that AI critique is systematically misdirected into five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that distract from the underlying "Project of AI" — financiers and corporations assembling networks of capital and infrastructure. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this with ethnographic texture, distinguishing Silicon Valley's "loud futuring" (CEO hype) from the "quiet futuring" of Bay Area communities (Effective Altruism, Rationalism) that supplies its ideological reservoir. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Dodds2026-df]] examine how these imaginaries circulate discursively — the former mapping industry "premediation" and "preclusion" strategies during the Altman controversy, the latter theorizing AI hype in journalism as a systemic infrastructure rather than mere narrative, with journalists caught playing both hype-maker and hype-watcher. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] grounds these dynamics in a concrete case, showing how Google's AI Overviews are discursively fought over by Google, journalists, and SEO marketers, with users' and environmental concerns conspicuously absent — a finding that echoes Dodds's point about hype's erasure of hidden costs and labor. [[Li2026-zv]] closes this arc from the audience side, finding that ordinary users experience "AI slop" as a fuzzy, contested, only partly negative category, complicating both platform-decay narratives and techno-hype accounts alike.

## Democracy, disinformation, and trust at platform scale

A parallel strand extends the political-economy argument into democratic institutions. [[Bennett2018-ys]] established the "disinformation order" thesis — that strategic disinformation reflects institutional legitimacy crisis more than media technology — and [[Bennett2025-xs]] now updates it, arguing that platforms enable new "digital surrogate organizations" that pull conservative parties toward illiberalism, using Trump's MAGA transformation as the central case. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] asks a related, narrower empirical question about whether alternative platforms themselves drive discursive polarization. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] provides the long-run empirical backdrop against which these claims should be read: trust in news has been declining since the 1980s — before the internet — and accelerated only around 2000, with the US a global outlier rather than a representative case, and with trust actually rising in unfree-press countries. This essay usefully complicates the declinist instincts of the anniversary essays by insisting on description over narrative, a methodological caution that resonates with [[Hegarty2026-qr]]'s argument, discussed below, that decline itself is a story that needs historicizing rather than simply accepting.

## The problem of knowing what counts as evidence

Underwriting all of this is a quieter methodological crisis: how can scholarship make any of these claims when platforms control access to the data needed to test them? [[Heiss2026-qv]] names this directly, arguing that platform-academic entanglement structurally resembles tobacco- and pharma-industry capture of research, and that regulatory mechanisms like the EU's DSA Article 40 are so far inadequate. Bastos2025-ya's elegy for Twitter's API ecosystem is the concrete историческое case of this abstract problem — the loss of a specific, unusually open data infrastructure that had made an entire subfield possible.

## Decline as genre, not just diagnosis

Finally, [[Hegarty2026-qr]] stands slightly outside — and above — this whole cluster by treating "internet decline narratives" as an object of study in their own right. Its argument lands as a useful, even uncomfortable, gloss on nearly every other paper filed here: IDNs periodize a threshold of decline, assign blame to Big Tech, and imagine a lost golden age, but in doing so they risk reproducing corporational determinism, erasing user agency, and romanticizing an internet that was always exclusive, Western, and male-dominated. Read against this frame, [[Marwick2026-ss]]'s LiveJournal nostalgia, [[Boyd2026-op]]'s parasocial renaming, [[Bruns2026-yv]]'s Twitter elegy, and even [[Baym2026-tr]]'s "it's bad, what now" all become instances of a recognizable cultural form — legitimate critiques, certainly, but also situated performances of generational loss that this topic cluster, taken as a whole, cannot simply narrate from the inside. The collective arc of these twenty-five papers is thus double: a genuine diagnosis of platformization's exhaustion and reconfiguration around AI, scam logics, and democratic erosion — and, running alongside it, an emerging self-awareness that the retrospective genre itself demands the same critical scrutiny its authors direct at the platforms they mourn.
