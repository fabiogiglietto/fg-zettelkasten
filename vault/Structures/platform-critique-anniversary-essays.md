# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The Reflective Turn: Diagnosing Two Decades of Social Media

A cluster of anniversary essays—published to mark twenty years of "social media" as concept and object of study—converges on a shared diagnosis: whatever social media once was, it is not that anymore, and the analytic vocabulary built to describe it has become an obstacle rather than a tool. [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Baym2026-tr]], [[Marwick2026-ss]], [[Helmond2026-ll]], and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] each revisit foundational commitments—boyd's own "networked publics," Baym's 2015 manifesto on the "struggle for society," Helmond's coinage of "platformization"—and find the terrain underneath them dissolved. The essays differ in emphasis but rhyme in structure: a golden-age or at least a formative moment, a set of political-economic mechanisms that eroded it, and a call to reconceptualize.

boyd's contribution is the most terminologically blunt: she proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has replaced posting, algorithmically curated influencer content has replaced peer exchange, and one-sided attention has replaced reciprocity ([[Boyd2026-op]]). Marwick arrives at a similar diagnosis autobiographically, contrasting the intimacy and reciprocal vulnerability of LiveJournal-era blogging with the surveillance and alienation of contemporary platforms, insisting throughout that this trajectory was contingent, not inevitable—a political-economic choice rather than a technological destiny ([[Marwick2026-ss]]). Baym extends this contingency argument into an audit: the four threats she named in 2015—wealth inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious creative labor, data extraction—have all worsened, now compounded by "broligarch" wealth concentration and generative AI's appropriation of user content ([[Baym2026-tr]]). Where Marwick and boyd look backward at what was lost, Helmond and Törnberg look at what structurally replaced it. Helmond argues that "platformization," her own decade-old coinage, has been diluted into a synonym for "digitization" and needs sharpening into a concept of platform-specific "transcoding," tracing the shift from social-graph capture (epitomized by the retirement of Facebook's Like button) toward AI-centered infrastructure extending even into space-based compute ([[Helmond2026-ll]]). Törnberg pushes furthest, arguing that "social media" as a unifying paradigm is simply exhausted, fractured into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—each demanding distinct methods ([[Tornberg2026-lc]]). Gerbaudo supplies the mechanism underlying much of this: the shift from "networked publics" organized around explicit interpersonal ties to "clustered publics" organized around statistically-inferred interest, with TikTok as the paradigm case and "TikTokification" as the industry-wide contagion ([[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]). Read together, these pieces trace a single arc—from reciprocal, graph-based sociality to algorithmically-inferred, parasocial, post-social spectatorship—even as they disagree about how much agency, nostalgia, or redemption remains available to scholars and users.

## Political Economy, Power, and the Platform as Discursive Actor

A second thread interrogates not what platforms *feel like* to users but how they are *owned, governed, and narrated*. [[De2026-ld]] coins "changecraft" to describe how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively manage constant policy and feature churn—rendering infrastructural shifts aesthetically visible while masking ideological pivots as mere continuity, a finding that dovetails with Helmond's account of platforms quietly abandoning old capture mechanisms (the Like button) for new ones without acknowledging the magnitude of the shift. Weinbrand's study of Google's AI Overviews performs a parallel discourse analysis at the level of search rather than social feeds, showing Google, journalists, and SEO marketers contesting the meaning of a new generative layer atop search, with the user's voice and environmental costs conspicuously absent from all three ([[Weinbrand2026-sf]])—an absence that echoes Dodds et al.'s broader claim that "AI hype" functions as systemic infrastructure rather than mere narrative, redistributing legitimacy toward tech executives while erasing labor and environmental costs from journalistic sourcing ([[Dodds2026-df]]).

Underneath this discursive management lies material consolidation, which several essays insist scholars have been too slow, or too compromised, to name. Bak-Coleman et al.'s empirical audit of high-profile social media research finds roughly half of published studies carry undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small elite of repeat-collaborating scientists who also staff editorial boards—yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the field and a systematic topical bias toward user-level misinformation research over platform-scale critique ([[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]]). This finding gives empirical teeth to Vertesi and colleagues' more theoretical argument that AI accountability discourse is captured by "decoys"—ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, and regulatory framings that appear critical but actually co-constitute the "Project of AI" as a vehicle for capital and network-power consolidation ([[Vertesi2026-lv]]). Hepp's ethnography of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring" complements this by showing how Effective Altruist and Rationalist communities supply the ideational reservoir—AGI arrival, job loss, geopolitical race—that "loud futuring" CEOs later amplify, while presenting themselves as apolitical even as they shape AI's power geometries ([[Hepp2026-oi]]). Stanusch's controversy-mapping of the Altman firing episode identifies the same industry strategies at a more granular, single-event scale: "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning industry as the only legitimate problem-solver) ([[Stanusch2026-ec]]). Richter's longitudinal comparison of US and German AI Twitter discourse shows how these dynamics are not universal but nationally path-dependent: US discourse individualized into tech-influencer dominance while German discourse institutionalized toward academia, advocacy, and government—divergent formative trajectories that arguably explain today's transatlantic regulatory gap ([[Richter2026-bt]]). Swartz's essay on the "scam age" and Bastos's elegy for open Twitter APIs both register, from different angles, the same underlying condition: legitimate and illegitimate economic activity, and legitimate and illegitimate research access, have become entangled with platform infrastructure to the point of indistinguishability ([[Swartz2026-zb]], Bastos2025-ya).

## Democracy, Extremism, and the Question of Platform Responsibility

A third cluster asks what all this consolidation and algorithmic reconfiguration has done to democratic discourse and extremist mobilization. Lewandowsky's commentary states the accountability claim baldly: platforms bear responsibility for global democratic backsliding, alongside more than 2,300 documented authoritarian-echoing actions by the second Trump administration ([[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]). Bennett and Livingston complicate any simple platform-effects story by insisting that "technocentric" disinformation research must be integrated with institutionalist accounts of party capture—arguing that digital "surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Stop the Steal networks) function as connective-action infrastructures that pull conservative parties toward illiberalism, a dynamic with European parallels ([[Bennett2025-xs]]). Bruns's retrospective on Twitter's "death" narrates a parallel institutional failure at the platform level: a once-uniquely-open, prosocial network progressively "enshittified" through commercial pressure, weaponized disinformation, and finally outright far-right capture under Musk, with successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) reproducing Twitter's form without solving its governance failures ([[Bruns2026-yv]]). Wang's empirical study of the academic Twitter-to-Mastodon exodus supplies granular evidence for this diagnosis of failed alternatives: even a highly motivated, well-networked cohort of early adopters mostly drifted back or lapsed, with retention hinging on niche server communities rather than migrated social capital ([[Wang2026-ub]]).

Several papers examine how platform architecture itself, rather than any single bad actor, produces polarization and extremism as structural outputs. Dehghan's large-scale Reddit study finds minimal cross-ideological overlap even between discursively similar subreddits, arguing that polarization is "sedimented" through an entanglement of moderation, design, and discourse rather than caused by any one factor ([[Dehghan2026-sy]]). Nangle's and Karo's studies of Instagram and TikTok show extremist and far-right actors exploiting affective softening, memetic camouflage, and algorithmic proximity to normalize content that would be flagged if presented directly—Karo's typology of audio camouflage, emoji codes, and "bait-and-switch" editing demonstrates that these evasion tactics are platform-structural rather than ideology-specific, appearing similarly among jihadist, far-right, and anti-vaccine communities ([[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Karo2026-dn]]). Gaw's ethnography of Philippine influence operations reframes manipulation itself as political brokerage, embedded in political-economic infrastructures rather than freelance disinformation ([[Gaw2025-ru]]), while Rodarte's study of Brazilian parliamentarians during the Manaus crisis shows politicians themselves becoming media producers who contest journalistic gatekeeping through competing claims to epistemic authority ([[Rodarte2026-dk]]). Kristensen's brief note on alternative platforms and discursive polarization sits adjacent to this cluster, asking whether platform choice itself—rather than content or actor—drives fragmentation ([[Kristensen2025-ni]]). Against this generally pessimistic backdrop, Brady et al.'s Bluesky field experiment offers a rare interventionist counterpoint: engagement-based feeds do amplify moralized, toxic content and distort perceived social norms around partisan animosity, but a "diversified extremity" algorithm can measurably reduce these harms without sacrificing platform enjoyment—evidence that algorithmic redesign, not just critique, remains a viable lever ([[Brady2026-ln]]). Goldberg et al.'s position paper extends this optimism further, arguing LLM-enabled bridging systems, collective dialogue tools, and community moderation could offer a genuine "second paradigm shift" for digital public squares, while cautioning against synthetic participation eroding democratic legitimacy ([[Goldberg2026-eb]]).

## Generative AI and the Aesthetics of Degraded Content

A final thread turns from platform architecture to the cultural residue of generative AI flooding these infrastructures. Galip's theoretical piece reads "AI slop" and "brainrot" through Ngai's theory of the gimmick—forms that work simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," exposing capitalism's own contradictions while retaining ambiguous critical potential ([[Galip2026-ix]]). Tang's ethnography of Chinese AI-slop creators empirically grounds this theory, showing how virality itself has been industrialized into a manageable, benchmarked production process via matrix account systems ([[Tang2026-gu]]), while Sbaraini-Fontes's survey of Italian audiences shows that publics differentiate their trust across AI configurations—trusting AI-mediated search more than AI-produced news—suggesting that audience skepticism about "slop" is neither uniform nor naive ([[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]]). Together with Weinbrand's discourse analysis of Google's AI Overviews, these pieces suggest that the platformization story once told about user-generated content and social graphs ([[Helmond2026-ll]], [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]) is now repeating itself one level up, with generative AI systems becoming the new site of extractive logic, contested legitimacy, and cultural degradation that these retrospective essays, collectively, are straining to name.
