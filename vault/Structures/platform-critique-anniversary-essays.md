# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The Arc of Argument

These five essays, gathered as anniversary reflections and position pieces, share a diagnostic mood: something has gone badly wrong with the platform-mediated internet, and the task now is less to describe novelty than to name the political-economic machinery that produced our present. Read together, they trace a movement from *platformization as infrastructural expansion* to *platformization as extractive world-building*, with scams, AI, and financialization increasingly named as the organizing logics rather than the aberrations.

## From Platformization to Big AI

[[Helmond2026-ll]] provides the connective conceptual spine. Revisiting her decade-old coinage, Helmond argues that platformization has entered an AI-centered phase: the Like button's retirement marks the end of the social graph and open-web signal extraction, while Project Suncatcher signals platforms' move off-earth in pursuit of compute. She warns that the term has been analytically dulled by conflation with digitization, and reclaims it as a situated form of *transcoding* — making domains platform-ready. This reframing sets the stakes for the others: what looks like a technological shift toward AI is better read as a reorganization of platform business models and infrastructural power. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] pushes this further, insisting AI is not a technology but a *Project* — a world-building assemblage of capital, infrastructure, and network power. Their five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) function as a methodological warning to the other essays' concerns: critique that stays at the level of algorithmic artifact or content harm risks legitimating the political economy underneath.

## The Worsening Diagnosis

[[Baym2026-tr]] offers the most explicit before/after ledger. Revisiting her 2015 manifesto, she finds each of her four threats — inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction — dramatically intensified, with tech "broligarchs" now translating wealth into political power and generative AI recycling user content into non-consensual harms. Her call to move beyond critique-for-its-own-sake toward engaged, cross-sector collaboration resonates with Vertesi's refusal of decoys, though the two prescribe different exits: Baym urges collaborative intervention, while Vertesi warns that even participatory efforts get enrolled into the Project of AI. Together they mark a scholarly self-reckoning: a decade of critical platform studies has not slowed the machine.

## Extractive Logics: Scams and the Loss of Intimacy

[[Swartz2026-zb]] radicalizes the political-economic diagnosis by arguing that scams are not a pathology of the platform economy but its central organizing logic. The withering of traditional institutions produces scam-shaped substitutes — MLMs, crypto, hustle courses, creator economies — routed through platform infrastructures, while anti-fraud regimes legitimate the very surveillance that trains AI. This dovetails with Baym's account of the aspirational influencer economy and with Helmond's Big AI turn: what Swartz calls the "scam age" is arguably the affective and economic surface of the infrastructural shifts Helmond and Vertesi describe. [[Marwick2026-ss]] provides the essential counter-image: a fieldnote from LiveJournal's reciprocal, small-scale, semi-private sociality, invoked not as nostalgia but as evidence that the present is a contingent outcome of political-economic choices. Where Swartz sees scams filling the void left by failing institutions, Marwick reminds us that platforms themselves dismantled the intimate infrastructures that once did that work.

## Points of Convergence and Tension

The essays converge on three propositions: that the present state of social media is *contingent, not inevitable* (explicit in Marwick, Baym, and Vertesi's rejection of the "inevitability decoy"); that *political economy, not interface or content, is the proper object of analysis* (Helmond, Vertesi, Swartz, Baym); and that *AI is the next terrain* on which platform power is being consolidated. They diverge on prescription. Marwick gestures toward small-scale, care-oriented alternatives; Baym toward interdisciplinary collaboration; Vertesi toward market-structure regulation and network-power analysis; Swartz toward treating scams as a legitimate object of governance research; Helmond toward conceptual precision as a precondition for critique. A productive tension runs between Baym's call for engagement and Vertesi's warning that engagement itself can be a decoy — a debate this collection stages without resolving, and which arguably defines the next decade's research agenda.
