# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The Anniversary Moment: Why Now?

A striking number of these papers arrive as deliberate retrospectives, timed to the twentieth anniversary of *Social Media + Society* and similar milestones. [[Baym2026-tr]], [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Marwick2026-ss]], and [[Helmond2026-ll]] each revisit a foundational text or concept from roughly a decade or two earlier — Baym's own 2015 manifesto, boyd's naming of "social media," Marwick's lived experience of the early blogosphere, Helmond's original coining of "platformization" — and ask what has become of it. The genre itself is instructive: these are not new empirical interventions so much as acts of conceptual stock-taking, and their convergence on pessimistic diagnoses (despite differing objects and methods) suggests something more than personal nostalgia. Read together, they trace a shared arc: an early internet of reciprocity and intimacy, a middle period of networked-publics optimism, and a current moment widely judged to have curdled into extraction, spectacle, and precarity.

## Naming the Present: Parasocial, Post-Social, Scam

The most consequential move across this literature is terminological — a felt need to rename "social media" because the word no longer describes what it points to. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes "parasocial media" to capture the shift from reciprocal peer exchange to one-sided consumption of influencer and algorithmic content. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] goes further, arguing the entire "social media" paradigm is dissolving into three distinct post-social formations: algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated chatbot communication. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the mechanism underlying this shift with unusual precision, theorizing TikTok's move from "networked publics" built on explicit interpersonal ties to "clustered publics" built on inferred behavioral similarity — a morphological transformation that other platforms are now imitating ("TikTokification"). [[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes the renaming project into starker territory still, arguing that scam logics — not sociality, not even parasociality — are the organizing principle of the contemporary platform economy, with anti-fraud infrastructure itself becoming a mechanism of governance and surveillance. Taken together, these essays converge on a diagnosis: whatever "social" meant in "social media" has been supplanted — by interest-clustering, by parasocial spectacle, by extraction, by scam — and the field's vocabulary has not caught up.

[[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Bruns2026-yv]] ground this abstract renaming in concrete platform histories. Marwick's LiveJournal fieldnotes recover a texture of vulnerability and small-scale reciprocity that she argues was foreclosed not by technological necessity but by political-economic choice — a claim [[Boyd2026-op]] and [[Baym2026-tr]] echo directly. Bruns narrates Twitter's arc from flat, open network to Musk-era "enshittification," using Doctorow's framework (also invoked by boyd) to describe a common trajectory: platforms that once enabled prosocial discovery are captured by revenue logics, abandoned moderation commitments, and eventually weaponized disinformation. Both essays end skeptically about successor platforms — Marwick dismisses Bluesky as mimicking legacy Twitter rather than reimagining infrastructure, while Bruns finds Mastodon, Threads, and Bluesky all replicating Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. [[Wang2026-ub]] offers empirical confirmation at the level of user behavior: even a highly motivated cohort of academic early-adopters largely failed to sustain their Mastodon migration, with retention hinging on niche server identity rather than the strength of transplanted networks — a granular demonstration of the structural stickiness these essays theorize.

## Platformization's Next Phase: Political Economy and Infrastructure

[[Helmond2026-ll]] anchors the collection's political-economy thread, returning to her own decade-old concept to argue that platformization is entering an AI-centered phase — Meta's retirement of the Like button and Google's space-based AI infrastructure (Project Suncatcher) signal a move from harvesting web-based social signals to in-house AI inference, and from "platform earth" to extraterrestrial compute frontiers. This resonates with [[Baym2026-tr]]'s tracking of "broligarch" wealth concentration and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]'s blunter claim that platforms bear direct responsibility for global democratic backsliding. [[De2026-ld]] supplies the discursive complement: platforms don't merely change, they *narrate* change strategically — through "changecraft," rendering infrastructural shifts aesthetically visible while reframing ideological pivots as continuity, thereby masking power consolidation. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] documents an earlier iteration of the same phenomenon in Meta's Newsroom discourse, showing how "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" and similar floating signifiers displace responsibility onto external bad actors while Meta postures as the only capable solver — language that has since migrated into EU and Australian regulation itself, illustrating platforms' capacity to shape the very policy discourse meant to constrain them.

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] radicalizes this argument at the level of AI specifically, reframing AI development as a "Project" of world-building capital accumulation and identifying five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that redirect accountability efforts away from underlying financialization and network power. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]] extend this into the sociotechnical-imaginaries literature, tracing how industry discourse (Longtermism, Techno-Hagiography, "quiet futuring" in the Bay Area) manages controversy and legitimizes speculative futures while eliding present harms — labor, climate, geopolitics. [[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes these dynamics, showing that the stakeholder composition driving AI discourse diverged sharply by country well before the GenAI boom, with lasting consequences for regulatory trajectories in the US versus Germany. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] closes this thread by showing the same discursive contest playing out around Google's AI Overviews, where Google, journalists, and SEO marketers battle over the meaning of a shift from indexical search to generative epistemic authority — notably absent from all three: any user voice or environmental reckoning.

## Data, Access, and the Research Infrastructure Crisis

A parallel set of papers diagnoses platformization's effect on the capacity to *study* platforms at all. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya eulogize the "Golden Age" of open Twitter APIs, quantifying a genuine research contraction — a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications by 2024 — following the 2023 pricing shutdown. [[Heiss2026-qv]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] extend this into a structural critique of industry capture: because platforms alone control the data needed to study them, researcher independence is compromised in ways precisely analogous to tobacco and pharmaceutical science, with roughly half of high-profile social media research carrying undisclosed industry ties. [[Peters2026-mo]] examines the emerging regulatory remedy, showing how the EU's Digital Services Act renders "data quality" a politically contested category, actively fought over by NGOs, academics, and platforms rather than a neutral methodological given. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] proposes a conceptual corrective to this whole research paradigm, urging scholars to treat platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure rather than chase narrow causal effects — a call that dovetails with [[Moran2025-qn]]'s account of the collapsing trust-and-safety profession, where layoffs and political pressure are dismantling the very practitioner infrastructure that made platform accountability legible from the inside.

## Fragmentation, Moderation, and the Limits of Governance

A final cluster examines how platformization's logics play out in specific contested arenas. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] complicates simple polarization narratives by showing that Reddit's political subreddits exhibit minimal cross-ideological overlap even among discursively similar communities — polarization here is "sedimented" through an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse rather than reducible to any single cause, a finding [[Kristensen2025-ni]] gestures toward from the angle of alternative platforms. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents how jihadist propagandists exploit TikTok's recommendation architecture and vernacular culture to evade moderation through mundane, meme-embedded tactics, arguing (in step with [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi consensus) that infrastructural rather than purely content-based governance is required. Mahl's expert panel crystallizes this governance turn at scale: across ten thematic areas, platform and journalism governance are rated more important than individual-level literacy fixes, even as generative AI's disruptive potential went unanticipated as recently as 2022 — a blind spot the AI-hype essays ([[Dodds2026-df]], [[Chadwick2013-ns]]) diagnose retrospectively, showing how journalism's own community-of-practice norms and sourcing habits helped naturalize AI's inevitability narrative rather than interrogate it. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s long-run trust data situates all of this historically, showing that the erosion of institutional trust in news predates the platform era by decades — a caution against attributing every contemporary ill to platformization alone, even as it confirms that digital media accelerated a decline already underway.

## The Arc

Read as a whole, this body of retrospective writing narrates platformization's second decade as a story of disillusionment following overreach: the "social" promise of Web 2.0 gives way to parasocial spectacle, scam logics, and algorithmic clustering; open research infrastructures collapse under commercial pressure even as industry capture of remaining scholarship deepens; and platforms' own discursive machinery — changecraft, newsroom framing, AI imaginaries — works continuously to stay ahead of the critiques these very papers are trying to mount. The through-line is a refusal of technological inevitability: from Marwick's LiveJournal to Vertesi's "Project of AI," these essays insist that today's platform condition is the product of contingent political-economic choices, and that naming it accurately — parasocial, post-social, scam-saturated, platformized-into-AI — is itself a precondition for imagining otherwise.
