# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## From Networked Publics to Platform Society: The Baseline

Any retrospective on platformization has to start from the conceptual infrastructure it is retrospecting *on*. [[Gillespie2010-as]] supplies the founding diagnosis: "platform" is not a neutral descriptor but a piece of strategic discourse that lets intermediaries like YouTube claim simultaneous legitimacy with users, advertisers, and regulators while eliding the tensions among them. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] scales this up into a structural account—the "platform society" as a contested terrain where private infrastructures (the Big Five) absorb public institutions (news, transport, health, education) and embed US-centric values into supposedly neutral code. [[Chadwick2013-ns]] adds the "hybrid media system," showing old and new media bound by adaptation and interdependence rather than replacement. Together these three set the terms—platform rhetoric, platform power, platform hybridity—against which the 2026 anniversary essays measure what has actually happened.

## The Anniversary Reckoning: Social Media's Broken Promise

A cluster of essays, several written explicitly for *Social Media + Society*'s twentieth-anniversary issue, converge on a single verdict: the thing called "social media" has curdled into something its name no longer describes. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits her own 2015 manifesto and finds all four original threats—wealth inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious creative labor, data extraction—not merely persisting but intensifying, crystallized in the "broligarchy" and in generative AI's appropriation of user content. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has replaced posting and one-sided engagement with influencers has displaced reciprocal friendship—a shift she traces to Doctorow's enshittification dynamic rather than to any technological necessity. [[Marwick2026-ss]] grounds the same argument autoethnographically, contrasting LiveJournal's intimacy and reciprocal vulnerability with the surveillance and alienation of contemporary platforms, insisting the current trajectory was a political-economic choice, not an inevitability. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own coinage of "platformization" a decade on, using Facebook's retirement of the Like button and Google's space-based "Project Suncatcher" to argue the concept itself needs retooling for an AI-centered phase, lest it collapse into a synonym for "digitization." Read together, these four essays form something like a genre: the founding scholars of social media studies auditing their own vocabulary and finding it obsolete.

## Algorithmic Succession: TikTokification and the Post-Social Turn

Two papers push this reckoning into explicit theory-building about what comes *after* networked publics. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] names the successor formation "clustered publics"—statistically assembled, interest-based groupings inferred from implicit signals like watch time, displacing the explicit friending/following logic of first-generation platforms, with TikTok as the paradigm case and other platforms undergoing "TikTokification." [[Tornberg2026-lc]] generalizes this into a call for a "post-social media studies," arguing that the social-graph paradigm is dissolving under three simultaneous pressures: algorithmic recommendation (echoing Gerbaudo's clustered publics), generative AI's synthetic content flooding feeds, and user retreat into private, bounded spaces. Both papers supply empirical texture to boyd's and Marwick's more essayistic claims—Meta's own court data showing only 7–17% of feed time involves followed accounts, for instance—turning "parasociality" and "broken connections" into measurable features of a reconfigured algorithmic architecture rather than mere nostalgia.

## Scams, Extraction, and the New Political Economy of Attention

[[Swartz2026-zb]] introduces a register largely absent from the anniversary essays' vocabulary of loss: scam logic as *constitutive* of, not parasitic on, platform economies. Where Baym and boyd diagnose extraction and precarity, Swartz pushes further to argue that the very boundary between legitimate platform capitalism and criminal scamming has dissolved, with anti-fraud infrastructures themselves becoming governance mechanisms that authorize surveillance and define economic citizenship. This essay complements the "it's bad" cluster by relocating the harm: not simply that platforms have become parasocial or extractive, but that they have become infrastructures for a trillion-dollar scam economy in which the discourse of "scam" performs its own boundary-work, legitimating some hustle-culture capitalism while criminalizing other versions of the same thing.

## Platforms as Discourse: Managing Change, Managing Memory

If Helmond and boyd ask what platforms *have become*, [[De2026-ld]] asks how platforms *narrate* their own becoming. "Changecraft" names the discursive strategies—visible infrastructural aesthetics, ideological pivots reframed as continuity, incremental "patchworking"—through which Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X manage the optics of transformation while consolidating power, a finding that dovetails with Gillespie's founding insight that platform rhetoric is never innocent. This essay essentially supplies the discourse-analytic mechanics behind the trajectory the anniversary essays lament: platforms don't just change, they stage change as creator benevolence and democratization even as they deepen dependency.

## Twitter's Death and the Research Infrastructure Crisis

A related sub-arc tracks platformization's effects on scholarship itself, using Twitter/X as the exemplary casualty. Bastos2025-ya offers an elegiac inventory of the APIs that made "Twitter studies" possible before the platform's rebranding. [[Bruns2019-nr]] documents the prior rupture—the post-Cambridge-Analytica "APIcalypse"—arguing that platforms weaponized privacy concerns to suppress exactly the critical, public-interest research that might hold them accountable. [[Bruns2026-yv]] extends the story to its endpoint: Twitter's flat, open structure once enabled prosocial crisis communication and hashtag activism, but mismanagement, commercialization, and Musk's acquisition accelerated its "enshittification," leaving successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) replicating its form without solving its governance failures. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] closes this loop empirically, showing that roughly 80% of high-profile social media research is now entangled with industry ties—undisclosed in most cases—meaning the field studying platforms' decline is itself substantially captured by the platforms it studies.

## Platformization Meets AI: The Next Decade's Frontier

The volume's final arc follows platformization logics into the generative-AI era, where Helmond's "Big AI" reorientation becomes the organizing frame. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] offers the sharpest political-economy critique, arguing AI is a "world-building Project" whose critics are repeatedly diverted into five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that distract from underlying capital and infrastructure concentration. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this with ethnographic texture on Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring"—the AGI, job-loss, and geopolitics imaginaries circulating in Bay Area labs and communities that feed the "loud futuring" of CEOs. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] examines how industry discourse around the Altman controversy deploys "premediation" and "preclusion" to monopolize AI's future-framing, while [[Dodds2026-df]] treats AI hype itself as a structuring infrastructure within journalism, redistributing legitimacy before technologies materialize. [[Beacken2026-zb]] grounds these abstractions comparatively, showing generative-AI propaganda's uptake varies by local political-economic structure across six democratically weakened countries—a useful corrective to any single deterministic narrative of AI's effects.

## Democracy, Illiberalism, and the Limits of Technocentric Explanation

Finally, [[Bennett2025-xs]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] push the retrospective arc toward its political stakes. Bennett and Livingston argue that technocentric accounts of disinformation and algorithmic amplification must be integrated with institutionalist accounts of party and elite behavior, proposing "digital surrogate organizations" as the connective tissue between platform affordances and democratic backsliding—a structural complement to Bruns's platform-level account of Twitter's decline and to Beacken's comparative caution against technological determinism. Kristensen's shorter piece on alternative platforms and discursive polarization sits adjacent to this question, probing whether platform fragmentation itself (the same fragmentation Gerbaudo and Törnberg theorize structurally) is driving polarized discourse. Together, this closing cluster insists that platformization's decade-long trajectory cannot be assessed purely at the level of feeds and affordances; it has become inseparable from the health of democratic institutions themselves.
