# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## A disciplinary anniversary, read as a verdict

Six of the essays gathered here were written for the same occasion — the twentieth-anniversary issue of *Social Media + Society* — and they function less as individual retrospectives than as a single multi-voiced reckoning. [[Baym2026-tr]] sets the terms explicitly, revisiting her own 2015 diagnosis of four threats (wealth inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction) only to conclude that all four have worsened rather than been managed. [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Marwick2026-ss]], [[Bruns2026-yv]], [[Helmond2026-ll]], and [[Swartz2026-zb]] each supply a different register for the same verdict: the social web that briefly existed in the 2000s has been displaced by something structurally different, and the difference is not accidental drift but the accumulated result of commercial and infrastructural choices. Read together, they constitute a structure of argument rather than a set of separate complaints — each essay supplies a piece of causal machinery (parasociality, platformization, scam logics, moderation collapse, lost intimacy) for what is ultimately one story about enshittification and the political economy of attention.

## From reciprocity to parasociality: diagnosing the loss

The affective core of this story is a before/after contrast. [[Marwick2026-ss]] grounds it autobiographically in LiveJournal, arguing that granular privacy controls and a small, self-selected userbase once sustained reciprocal, vulnerable, community-building writing that today's platforms actively foreclose. [[Boyd2026-op]] generalizes this into a conceptual proposal: "social media" has become a misnomer for platforms that reward scrolling over posting and one-sided engagement with influencers over mutual exchange, and she argues scholarship should rename the object of study "parasocial media" to stop smuggling in assumptions of reciprocity that no longer hold. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates the same arc through the specific case of Twitter/X, showing how a flat, open network structure that once enabled hashtag activism and crisis communication was progressively dismantled by revenue-seeking mismanagement, algorithmic amplification of outrage, and ultimately a hostile takeover — with successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) replicating Twitter's form without solving its underlying moderation and governance failures. Across all three, the emphasis falls on contingency: nothing about digital communication technology required this outcome; particular design and business-model decisions produced it, and different decisions remain imaginable.

## Political economy as root cause, not backdrop

This refusal of technological inevitability is the load-bearing claim connecting the retrospectives to the field's founding theoretical vocabulary. [[Gillespie2010-as]] supplies the prehistory: his analysis of how YouTube's "platform" rhetoric strategically flattened distinct constituencies (users, advertisers, media partners, regulators) into a single reassuring metaphor of neutral, egalitarian infrastructure already identified the discursive sleight-of-hand that the anniversary essays now diagnose as having hardened into entrenched power. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] extended that critique into a systemic framework — the "platform society" as a contested terrain between private corporate value and public value, embedded unevenly across sectors (news, transport, health, education) and dominated by a handful of US infrastructural giants. [[Baym2026-tr]] updates this political economy with current figures: Zuckerberg's net worth nearly quintupling since 2015, Musk crossing $700 billion, and concentrated wealth converting directly into political power (the "broligarchy"). Where van Dijck worried about platforms colonizing public institutions, Baym and Boyd describe a further concentration in which even the participatory promise of user-generated content has been appropriated as training fodder for generative AI, producing a crisis of authenticity that compounds rather than replaces earlier harms.

## Platformization's next phase: infrastructure becomes AI

[[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own decade-old coinage of "platformization" and argues the concept now needs rescuing from conceptual erosion — it has been conflated with mere digitization — and redefined as a situated, collaborative process of making domains "platform-ready." Her illustrative cases (Meta retiring the Like button in favor of in-app and AI-inferred behavioral data, Google's space-based AI infrastructure project) mark a shift from the social-graph paradigm toward an AI-centered phase in which new actors (Nvidia, defense contractors) and new sectors (military, energy, space) are drawn into the platform logic. [[Tornberg2026-im]] theorizes this same transition at greater conceptual depth, arguing for a tendential shift "from the platform society to the AI society": network-effect monopolies give way to "cognitive lock-in," predictive logics give way to generative ones in which theory becomes infrastructural and research becomes a rented service, and struggles over visibility are displaced by "alignment politics" over what models can say or refuse. Both essays insist the AI moment is not a rupture from the platform economy but its reorganization — Helmond and Tornberg effectively supply the infrastructural and economic vocabulary for what Baym registers emotionally as worsening and what Boyd registers experientially as parasociality.

## Scams, hype, and imaginaries: the discursive supports of extraction

If platformization names the structural process, [[Swartz2026-zb]] names its ambient cultural logic: a "scam age" in which the boundary between legitimate and illegitimate economic activity dissolves, anti-fraud infrastructures double as surveillance and governance mechanisms, and scammy alternatives to failing institutions (MLMs, crypto, creator hustle culture) are themselves dependent on platform infrastructure. This dovetails with a cluster of essays examining how the AI industry narrates its own inevitability to forestall scrutiny. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] shows the Sam Altman controversy generating industry imaginaries — Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, Techno-Hagiography — deployed through "premediation" and "preclusion" to displace present harms into speculative futures while absorbing critique. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this with an ethnography of Bay Area "quiet futuring," arguing that Effective Altruist and Rationalist communities supply the long-termist ideas that CEOs' "loud futuring" later condenses and amplifies, all while presenting itself as apolitical. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] names the underlying mechanism most directly: AI accountability discourse is repeatedly captured by "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that distract from the material political economy of financialization and network power, a claim that functions as the conceptual mirror of Swartz's boundary-work argument about scams. [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] extend this hype-critique into journalism and search specifically — showing how AI hype operates as structuring infrastructure within newsrooms' sourcing practices, and how Google's AI Overviews are discursively framed by competing actors (Google, journalists, SEO marketers) in ways that conspicuously omit the user's voice and any reckoning with environmental or democratic costs.

## Governance's retreat and the choreography of change

A final cluster examines what remains of platform governance once the scam-saturated, hype-driven political economy described above has consolidated. [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the literal unraveling of the trust-and-safety profession under political pressure and layoffs, suggesting the institutional capacity to mitigate harm is itself eroding just as Bruns's account of Twitter's moderation collapse would predict. [[Crosset2026-mq]] offers a comparative legal reading of EU/US content-moderation legislation, finding that despite divergent regimes (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation) all converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" — transparency and auditing requirements that quietly convert platforms into quasi-public infrastructures without resolving the underlying power asymmetries. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] works at a finer grain, building a "law-to-design" framework that maps dark-pattern taxonomies onto the EU Digital Services Act's autonomy-violation categories, giving regulators and HCI researchers shared vocabulary for enforcement — precisely the kind of conceptual tool Gillespie's 2010 essay found lacking. And [[De2026-ld]] closes the loop by analyzing how platforms themselves narrate their own continual change: "changecraft" describes the discursive strategies (visible aesthetic redesign, ideological pivots framed as continuity, incremental "patchworking") through which Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X manage the appearance of responsiveness while consolidating power — the contemporary, highly professionalized descendant of the populist "platform" rhetoric Gillespie first diagnosed in 2010.

## The arc as a whole

Taken together, these eighteen papers trace a single trajectory: Gillespie's early warning that "platform" rhetoric obscures substantive curatorial power; van Dijck's elaboration of that power into a contested societal condition; a decade of consolidation that the 2026 anniversary essays now narrate as decline — toward parasociality, scam logics, and the death of reciprocal publics; and a present moment in which platformization is being re-routed through generative AI, producing new hype-infrastructures, new decoys, and new governance arrangements that largely reproduce rather than resolve the underlying asymmetries of power. The throughline is the shared insistence, across autobiographical essay and discourse analysis alike, that none of this was inevitable — which is also what makes the collection, finally, a call to imagine otherwise.
