# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The anniversary moment: why now?

A striking number of these papers are occasioned by the same calendrical coincidence—twenty years since the coinage of "social media," ten years since the coinage of "platformization"—and they converge on a shared diagnostic move: using the anniversary as a vantage point from which to declare that the object named by these terms no longer exists in its original form. [[Helmond2026-ll]], [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Marwick2026-ss]], and [[Baym2026-tr]] all appear in the same *Social Media + Society* retrospective issue, each returning to a foundational concept (platformization, the social in social media, the lived texture of the early internet, Baym's own 2015 "struggle for society" manifesto) only to find it inadequate to 2026's platform landscape. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] generalizes this mood into an explicit disciplinary argument: the "social media" paradigm itself is unraveling as an analytical frame, not just as a lived experience, and the field needs to fracture into parallel vocabularies for algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication. Read together, these essays stage a genre of reflexive stock-taking in which the discipline interrogates its own founding terms as much as it interrogates the platforms themselves.

## From networked publics to algorithmic clusters

The most consistent empirical thread across this cluster is the demise of the "networked public"—boyd and Papacharissi's foundational image of social media as peer-to-peer, graph-structured sociality—and its replacement by algorithmically curated, interest-based circulation. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] gives this shift its most systematic theorization, coining "clustered publics" to describe TikTok's item-centric, watch-time-driven assembly of audiences, in contrast to the people-centric "networked publics" of Facebook-era platforms. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] supplies the broader evidentiary backbone (Meta's own admission that only 7–17% of feed time involves followed accounts; the engineering-level privileging of passive dwell-time metrics over active social signals) and frames this as the "TikTokification" of the entire industry. [[Boyd2026-op]] names the resulting experiential mode "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has displaced posting and that one-sided attention to influencers has displaced mutual friendship as the platform's central social logic. [[Helmond2026-ll]] locates this same transition infrastructurally, using Facebook's 2026 retirement of the Like button to show how platforms are abandoning open-web social-graph signals in favor of in-app behavioral inference and AI-driven recommendation—platformization's "second decade" now oriented around AI infrastructure rather than social connection. Together these pieces describe a single underlying morphological shift, documented from the interface, the infrastructure, the metrics, and the lived experience simultaneously.

## Loss, nostalgia, and the political economy of decline

A second thread treats this transformation elegiacally, insisting that the degraded present was not technologically inevitable but the product of contingent corporate choices. [[Marwick2026-ss]] contrasts LiveJournal's intimacy and reciprocity with contemporary platforms' alienation and surveillance, explicitly framing the present as a political-economic outcome rather than a natural endpoint. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter's specific trajectory from flat, open, prosocial debate space to its "enshittified" afterlife as X, implicating revenue pressure, abandoned moderation commitments, and Musk's acquisition, while arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) have cloned Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits her own decade-old list of threats—inequality, opacity, precarious labor, data extraction—and finds all four intensified, now compounded by generative-AI exploitation of user-generated content and the rise of the "broligarchy." These essays share a tonal register (nostalgia, disappointment, moral reckoning) and a structural claim: concentration, financialization, and algorithmic optimization—not technology per se—produced the decline, echoing [[van-Dijck2018-up]]'s earlier argument that platforms are not neutral infrastructures but vehicles for contested values, and [[Gillespie2010-as]]'s founding observation that the rhetoric of neutral "platforms" has always obscured real curatorial power.

## Failed exits and the stickiness of the old order

Against this narrative of transformation, [[Wang2026-ub]] offers an important empirical check: even highly motivated academic communities attempting to migrate from Twitter to Mastodon after the Musk acquisition largely failed to sustain the move, with most either returning to Twitter or drifting across competing alternatives (Bluesky, Threads). This complicates the "death of Twitter" thesis in [[Bruns2026-yv]]—decline and disillusionment do not automatically translate into successful exit, and decentralized alternatives face their own structural barriers (discoverability, social capital transfer, fragmented moderation). Relatedly, [[De2026-ld]] shows how incumbent platforms manage the appearance of change itself, coining "changecraft" to describe how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively stage transformation—framing ideological pivots as continuity, masking monetization shifts behind creator-benevolence rhetoric—thereby consolidating power even while narrating responsiveness. Read against [[Bruns2019-nr]]'s account of the post-Cambridge-Analytica "APIcalypse," in which platforms used privacy rhetoric to choke off independent scholarly scrutiny, a consistent pattern emerges: platforms retain control not only over infrastructure and attention but over the very discourse and data through which their transformations can be studied or contested.

## Extraction intensifies: scams, toxicity, and the AI pivot

A further arc traces how extractive logics have deepened and diversified. [[Swartz2026-zb]] argues that scams have become a central organizing logic of platform economies rather than peripheral criminal noise, with anti-fraud infrastructure doubling as a surveillance and governance mechanism. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows empirically how influencer credibility itself becomes an infrastructural vector for misinformation and toxic echo chambers, complicating simple source/content distinctions in misinformation research. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] extends his earlier skepticism about platform neutrality into the governance register, documenting how "reduction"—algorithmic demotion rather than outright removal—has become a largely invisible but equally consequential form of content moderation, concentrating curatorial power while evading the accountability mechanisms built around visible removal. These essays collectively suggest that extraction and harm have not only intensified quantitatively (per Baym's wealth-concentration figures) but have proliferated into new, harder-to-see forms: scam infrastructures, source-driven toxicity, and shadow moderation.

## The AI horizon and the next political economy

The final arc gestures toward where platformization is heading next: an AI-centered reorganization of power that several papers argue is being actively obscured by hype and misdirection. [[Helmond2026-ll]] traces platformization's expansion into space-based AI infrastructure (Google's Project Suncatcher) and the entry of new "Big AI" actors (Nvidia, Anduril, Palantir). [[Vertesi2026-lv]] offers the most pointed political-economic critique, arguing that AI should be understood as a "Project"—a world-building enterprise assembling capital and infrastructural power—and that mainstream critiques of AI (ontological, safety, regulatory, and inevitability framings) function as "decoys" that inadvertently stabilize this project rather than challenge it. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] empirically documents one instance of this dynamic, showing how industry discourse around the Sam Altman controversy deployed longtermist and techno-hagiographic imaginaries to "premediate" and "preclude" critique, displacing present harms onto speculative futures. [[Dodds2026-df]] extends the point to journalism itself, arguing that AI hype is not merely discourse but a structuring infrastructure that redistributes legitimacy and resources within newsrooms. Finally, essays like [[Bennett2025-xs]] and [[Bechmann2026-dr]] push the political stakes further still, arguing that platform scholarship must move beyond individual-level, causal-effects paradigms (of disinformation or algorithmic harm) toward institutional and infrastructural accounts of how networked publics reorganize political power and democratic life—a call that rhymes with [[van-Dijck2018-up]]'s founding insistence that platforms are contested terrain between private and public value, and with [[Gillespie2010-as]]'s claim, now fifteen years old, that our vocabulary for naming platform power remains inadequate to the task.

Taken as a whole, this cluster traces a coherent arc: from the optimistic "networked publics" framework of the Web 2.0 era, through platformization's infrastructural consolidation, to today's algorithmically clustered, AI-saturated, scam-adjacent platform economy—with each successive essay insisting that naming this new configuration correctly, rather than merely lamenting it, is the discipline's most urgent task.
