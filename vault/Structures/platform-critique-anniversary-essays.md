# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The Anniversary Diagnosis: What Happened to "Social" Media?

The core of this collection is a cluster of essays written for anniversary or retrospective occasions—most explicitly the *Social Media + Society* twentieth-anniversary issue—that converge, from different angles of personal and disciplinary history, on a single verdict: the thing once called "social media" no longer functions as advertised. [[Boyd2026-op]] makes the terminological case most bluntly, arguing that platforms now reward scrolling over posting and one-sided attachment to influencers over reciprocal exchange, and proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media." [[Marwick2026-ss]] arrives at a kindred conclusion autoethnographically, contrasting the intimacy and reciprocity she experienced on LiveJournal with the surveillance and alienation of contemporary platforms, insisting that this outcome was a contingent political-economic choice rather than a technological inevitability. [[Baym2026-tr]] closes the loop by revisiting her own decade-old manifesto and finding that all four of the threats she named then—wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious creative labor, and data extraction—have not merely persisted but intensified, now compounded by generative AI's appropriation of user content. Read together, these three essays function as a triptych: boyd supplies the conceptual rupture, Marwick the felt, historical texture of what was lost, and Baym the empirical ledger of harms accumulated. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] then generalizes this diagnosis into a paradigm-level claim, arguing that the very category of "social media" is dissolving into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private enclaves, and AI-mediated communication—that no longer share the user-generated, socially-circulated content that once unified the field, a claim it backs with Meta's own admission that the vast majority of feed time now involves content from neither friends nor followed accounts.

## Platformization's Second Decade: Infrastructure, Discourse, and Power

If the anniversary essays diagnose symptoms, [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits the underlying machinery she named a decade ago. Returning to "platformization" on its own anniversary, Helmond argues the term has been diluted through conflation with mere "digitization" and needs sharpening around the idea of "transcoding"—rendering domains platform-ready. Her illustrative cases, Facebook's retirement of the Like button and Google's satellite-based AI infrastructure, trace exactly the same arc that Tornberg formalizes: platforms decoupling from the social graph and open web in favor of AI-driven inference, with Big Tech repositioning as "Big AI" and extending platform logics into wholly new sectors (space, energy, defense). [[De2026-ld]] supplies the missing discursive mechanics of this transition, coining "changecraft" to describe how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X narrate their own restructuring—staging infrastructural shifts as aesthetic progress, recasting ideological pivots as continuity, and using incremental "patchworking" to consolidate power while appearing responsive. Where Helmond documents *what* platforms are becoming, De documents *how* they talk their way through becoming it, and both extend a shared genealogy running through Gillespie, van Dijck, and Plantin.

[[Bruns2026-yv]] offers the most detailed case study of this dynamic: the specific, sequential "death" of Twitter, from its early prosocial affordances through commercial capture, algorithmic amplification of reactionary content, and finally Musk-era enshittification. Bruns's insistence that Twitter's decline resulted from cumulative mismanagement and weaponized disinformation—not inherent flaws in openness—echoes Marwick's and boyd's shared refrain that none of this trajectory was technologically necessary, and his skepticism toward Mastodon, Threads, and Bluesky as mere clones without solving the underlying governance problem previews the volume's broader worry that "post-Twitter" futures may simply reproduce old failures in new architectures.

## Extraction, Scams, and the Political Economy Beneath the Interface

Running underneath these platform-level accounts is a harder political-economic argument about what actually sustains engagement-driven media once reciprocity has thinned out. [[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes this furthest, arguing that scams have become a *central organizing logic* rather than a peripheral pathology of platform economies—that the boundary between legitimate hustle and outright fraud is dissolving, and that anti-fraud infrastructures themselves become new surveillance and governance mechanisms. This dovetails with Baym's account of precarious influencer labor and data extraction, and with Marwick's characterization of contemporary platforms as extractive and hostile to vulnerability: all three describe an economy in which institutional withering pushes users toward monetized, algorithmically mediated substitutes for community, work, and trust. [[Heiss2026-qv]] extends the extraction logic to the scholarly field itself, arguing that platforms' monopoly over the data needed to study them structurally compromises the independence of social media research in ways directly analogous to tobacco- and pharma-industry capture—an argument that implicitly asks whether the very retrospectives filed here can be written free of the industry they describe.

## Platforms, Democracy, and the Erosion of Shared Epistemic Ground

A second strand takes the platformization diagnosis into explicitly political territory. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] states the accountability claim most directly: platforms bear real responsibility for global democratic backsliding and must answer for it. [[Bennett2025-xs]] complicates this by arguing that purely "technocentric" accounts of disinformation are insufficient without an institutionalist lens; platforms matter not simply because they spread false content but because they generate new "digital surrogate organizations" that pull parties toward illiberalism, a mechanism vividly on display in the MAGA-era Republican Party and its European analogues. This organizational reading of platform power complements Bruns's account of "participatory disinformation" undermining Twitter's public-debate function and links back to Tornberg's warning that post-social formations erode the shared epistemic infrastructure democratic discourse depends on. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] supplies a long durée empirical backdrop to these claims, showing that trust in news has been declining since the 1980s, accelerating with digitization around 2000, and diverging sharply by press-freedom regime—evidence that platformization intensified rather than originated the erosion of shared epistemic ground. [[Balluff2026-ev]] adds a caveat to over-simple counter-public narratives, finding that alternative and legacy German media covering Nord Stream 2 actually reference largely overlapping entities, differing more in network structure and self-referential media-mentioning than in the cast of actors invoked—a reminder that platformization's political effects are structural and relational rather than purely a matter of divergent content universes.

## The AI Inflection: Platformization's Next Phase

Several papers extend the retrospective arc forward into the generative-AI moment that Tornberg and Helmond both flag as platformization's emergent frontier. [[Hepp2026-oi]] maps the ideational infrastructure behind this shift, distinguishing Silicon Valley's "loud futuring" (CEO hype) from a "quiet futuring" among labs, Effective Altruist and Rationalist communities that supplies the long-termist imaginaries later amplified publicly. [[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes this by tracing a decade of stakeholder composition in US and German AI discourse on Twitter, showing the US drifting toward individualized tech-influencer dominance while Germany institutionalized around academic and governmental actors—a divergence the paper argues pre-structures each country's subsequent GenAI regulation. [[Dodds2026-df]] turns to journalism's complicity in this hype, arguing AI hype functions as systemic infrastructure that redistributes legitimacy and resources well before technologies mature, with journalists occupying a contradictory role as both hype-makers and hype-watchers. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] offers a close case of this dynamic in Google's AI Overviews, showing how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers contest the meaning of a single product feature that quietly repositions Google from indexical gatekeeper to generative epistemic authority—an almost perfect miniature of Helmond's transcoding thesis applied to search. Finally, [[Beacken2026-zb]] resists treating this AI inflection as a uniform global force, showing that generative AI's use for political propaganda across six democratically weakened states varies with local structural conditions rather than following any single deterministic trajectory—a useful corrective note that closes the collection's arc: platformization's next act, like its first, will be contingent, unevenly built, and open to contestation rather than technologically predetermined.
