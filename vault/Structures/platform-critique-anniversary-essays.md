# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## A disciplinary gut-check

Many of the papers gathered here were written for the same occasion: Social Media + Society's twentieth-anniversary issue, which asked its core theorists to look back at the terms they helped coin and ask whether those terms still describe anything real. [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Marwick2026-ss]], [[Baym2026-tr]], [[Helmond2026-ll]], and [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] form the spine of this retrospective mood, each returning to a founding concept — networked publics, platformization, the struggle for society — and finding it strained past its original meaning. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] generalizes the diagnosis into an explicit thesis: the "social media" paradigm itself, built on user-generated content circulating through a social graph, is dissolving, and the field needs new vocabulary for what comes after. Read together, these essays are less a celebration of an intellectual tradition than an autopsy of its object of study.

## From networked publics to parasocial, clustered, and scrolling publics

The central empirical claim threading through this cluster is that the defining mechanism of "social" media — reciprocal, people-centric connection — has been displaced by algorithmic curation organized around content and interest rather than relationship. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] gives this the most precise theoretical treatment, distinguishing first-generation "networked publics" (explicit, people-centric, visible) from TikTok-style "clustered publics" (implicit, item-centric, opaque), and showing how the rest of the industry is undergoing "TikTokification." [[Boyd2026-op]] arrives at a parallel but more polemical conclusion from inside the very tradition that named networked publics in the first place: she proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media," since feeds now reward scrolling over posting and influencer attention over mutual friendship. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] supplies the hardest evidence for this shift — Meta's own court data showing only 7–17% of feed time involves content from one's own network — and extends the argument toward three "post-social" formations (algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private enclaves, AI-mediated chat), explicitly citing boyd's and Gerbaudo's frameworks as scaffolding it is trying to move beyond. [[Marwick2026-ss]] supplies the affective counterpoint and historical baseline: her LiveJournal fieldnotes recover what networked, reciprocal intimacy actually felt like before this transformation, insisting the shift was a political-economic choice, not a technological inevitability — a claim that quietly underwrites all three of the theoretical essays above.

## Political economy as the throughline, not the add-on

If the "social" in social media is eroding, the papers agree the cause lies in political economy rather than interface design. [[Baym2026-tr]] makes this most directly, revisiting her own 2015 diagnosis of four structural threats (wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious creative labor, data extraction) and finding all four worse, now crystallized in the figure of the tech "broligarch." This argument extends a much older lineage: [[Gillespie2010-as]] shows how the very word "platform" was built to perform exactly the kind of boundary-obscuring, stakeholder-pleasing discourse that later papers describe as entrenched practice, while [[van-Dijck2018-up]] formalizes platformization as a contest between private gain and public value that was decided, structurally, in favor of a handful of US firms well before most users noticed. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] and [[Petre2019-zh]] trace how that power operates in practice and in rhetoric: reduction (algorithmic demotion without removal) and "gaming" accusations both show platforms wielding moralized, opaque authority to manage visibility while deflecting scrutiny of their own economic interests. [[De2026-ld]] names the discursive mechanism binding these cases together — "changecraft," the strategic narration of platform change as continuity, benevolence, and participation even as power consolidates — effectively generalizing Gillespie's and Petre's case studies into a theory of how platforms talk about themselves over time.

## Extraction intensifies: scams, surrogate networks, and illiberal connective action

Several papers push the political-economy argument toward its most corrosive endpoint: a digital economy in which extraction and deception are not aberrations but organizing logics. [[Swartz2026-zb]] is the sharpest statement of this, arguing that social media has entered a "scam age" in which the boundary between legitimate and illegitimate economic activity is actively, continuously renegotiated, and in which anti-fraud infrastructure itself becomes a vector of surveillance and governance. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] generalizes this suspicion of surface-level categories into a methodological warning for AI accountability research: critiques organized around "decoys" (ontology, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulation) actually stabilize the underlying "Project of AI" rather than contesting it, echoing Swartz's point that naming something a scam (or a safety concern) does real boundary work for capital. [[Bennett2025-xs]] extends the extractive-logic argument into party politics, showing how platform-enabled "digital surrogate organizations" have reorganized the Republican Party around illiberalism — a reminder that platformization's political economy has reshaped not just attention markets but institutional structures of democracy itself.

## The AI rupture: from platform society to AI society

A dense sub-cluster asks whether generative AI constitutes a genuine epochal break or merely platformization's next sector. [[Tornberg2026-im]] states the thesis most comprehensively: the platform society's network-effect monopolies are giving way to "cognitive lock-in," prediction is giving way to generation, and visibility politics is giving way to "alignment politics" — a reorganization the paper insists is continuous with, not external to, the political economy [[van-Dijck2018-up]] described a decade earlier. [[Helmond2026-ll]] arrives at a structurally similar conclusion from the platformization side of the ledger, using Facebook's retirement of the Like button and Google's orbital AI infrastructure to argue platformization is entering an AI-centered, "Big AI" phase that demands conceptual retooling rather than further diffusion of the term. [[Hepp2026-oi]] and [[Stanusch2026-ec]] examine the imaginative and discursive work sustaining this transition: Hepp's "quiet futuring" account of Bay Area AI communities and Stanusch's controversy-mapping of the Altman firing both show how industry actors manage AI's meaning through premediation, mythologization, and the strategic deflection of present-day harms into speculative futures — a move [[Dodds2026-df]] identifies as systemic in journalism's own coverage of AI hype, and which [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows operating concretely in Google's framing of AI Overviews as a seamless, almost infrastructural evolution of search. [[Topinka2026-hb]] supplies the affective register of this same transition at the level of content itself: "slopaganda" is AI output optimized not to deceive but to attune to feed logics, intensifying existing ideological formations rather than disrupting them — arguably the cultural symptom of the political-economic shift the other AI-era papers describe structurally.

## Death, decline, and the search for successors

A final strand asks what happens to public debate once a dominant platform's political economy curdles. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter's arc from flat, open network to Musk-era "enshittification," concluding that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) have cloned Twitter's form without solving its governance failures — a verdict that dovetails with [[Marwick2026-ss]]'s warning against merely replicating legacy platforms and with [[Kristensen2025-ni]]'s narrower empirical question of whether alternative platforms actually reduce, or merely relocate, discursive polarization. Taken as a whole, this collection traces a single arc: from networked publics, through platformization and its political economy of extraction and scam logics, into an AI-saturated "post-social" condition — with each generation of scholars insisting, against technological determinism, that none of this was inevitable, and that the vocabulary built to describe the last decade must now be rebuilt to survive the next.
