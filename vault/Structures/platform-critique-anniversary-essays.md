# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The anniversary diagnosis: from social to parasocial

The anchor texts here are a cluster of anniversary essays written for *Social Media + Society*'s twentieth year, each revisiting a foundational claim about what "social" media once meant and arguing that it no longer holds. danah boyd's [[Boyd2026-op]] makes the sharpest terminological move, proposing "parasocial media" to replace "social media" as a description of platforms where scrolling has displaced posting and one-sided attachment to influencers has displaced peer reciprocity. Alice Marwick's [[Marwick2026-ss]] arrives at a kindred diagnosis autobiographically, contrasting the intimacy and reciprocal vulnerability of LiveJournal-era blogging with the alienation and surveillance of contemporary platforms, insisting that this trajectory was a political-economic choice rather than a technological inevitability. Nancy Baym's [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits her own 2015 manifesto to argue that the four threats she named then—inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction—have not merely persisted but intensified, crystallized in the figure of the "broligarchy." Anne Helmond's [[Helmond2026-ll]], written from platform studies rather than personal history, supplies the conceptual throughline: platformization, the term she coined a decade ago, has curdled from describing infrastructural expansion into an over-stretched synonym for "digitization," and needs to be redefined around AI-centered "transcoding" as platforms decouple from the open web (Meta's retirement of the Like button) and reach toward new frontiers (Google's space-based AI infrastructure). Read together, these four essays converge on a single verdict: the social web imagined two decades ago has been financialized, consolidated, and algorithmically hollowed out, and the field's own vocabulary is due for retirement alongside it.

## Theorizing the post-social turn

Where the anniversary essays diagnose loss, a second cluster tries to name the structure that has replaced it. Petter Törnberg's [[Tornberg2026-lc]] proposes that "social media" as an analytic category is dissolving into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—driven by the shift from social-graph to interest-based recommendation, generative AI's severing of the platform–user link, and users' retreat into bounded, trust-based spaces; it marshals Meta's own court data (7% of Instagram time, 17% of Facebook time spent on followed content) as evidence that the "network" in networked publics has already evaporated. Paolo Gerbaudo's [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the finer-grained mechanism for the "algorithmic broadcasting" side of this story, theorizing TikTok's shift from boyd-and-Papacharissi-style "networked publics" to "clustered publics"—statistically inferred, item-centric groupings built from implicit signals like watch time rather than explicit ties—and documenting the "TikTokification" of rival platforms around this same logic. Helmond's redefinition of platformization as AI-era transcoding ([[Helmond2026-ll]]) dovetails with both: all three essays locate 2024–2026 as an inflection point at which the infrastructural logics organizing online publics were reorganized around inference, recommendation, and AI rather than declared social connection.

## Political economy: broligarchs, scams, and the AI pivot

Baym's wealth-concentration data ([[Baym2026-tr]]) sets the terms for a broader argument, made most forcefully by Jenny Swartz's [[Swartz2026-zb]], that extraction has become the organizing logic rather than an aberration of platform economies. Swartz reframes scams as central rather than peripheral to social media—"network scams" in which believer, shill, and victim roles blur across MLMs, crypto, and hustle-culture creator economies—and shows how anti-fraud infrastructure doubles as a pretext for expansive behavioral surveillance, mirroring the same data-capture logic Helmond identifies in the AI pivot. Janet Vertesi and colleagues' [[Vertesi2026-lv]] push this political-economy critique furthest upstream, arguing that AI itself is best understood not as a technology but as a financialized "Project" through which capital assembles infrastructural and network power, and that critical and accountability discourse is repeatedly diverted into "decoys" (ontological debates, inevitability narratives, safety framing, disruption talk, regulatory theater) that end up legitimating the very consolidation they seem to critique. This lineage—from boyd's parasociality through Baym's broligarchy to Swartz's scam economy and Vertesi's decoy-critique—traces an arc from diagnosing symptoms to indicting the deeper capital logic that produces them.

## Platforms narrating themselves

A parallel strand examines how platforms discursively manage their own transformation and culpability, extending Tarleton Gillespie's foundational analysis. [[Gillespie2010-as]] establishes the template: the word "platform" itself does rhetorical work, letting YouTube claim populist neutrality to users while claiming media-partner status to advertisers and limited-liability conduit status to regulators, obscuring the substantive curatorial power being exercised underneath. His later [[Gillespie2022-jx]] extends this into the algorithmic era, arguing that "reduction"—algorithmic demotion without removal—has become the dominant, least-visible form of content moderation, one that reifies rather than questions the platform's underlying logic of engagement. Hurcombe's [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] applies this discourse-analytic lens directly to Meta's Newsroom, showing how "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" and kindred floating signifiers externalize responsibility for problematic content onto outside bad actors while diffusing into EU and Australian regulatory language. De et al.'s [[De2026-ld]] generalizes this into the concept of "changecraft," identifying recurring strategies—aestheticized visibility of infrastructural change, reframing ideological pivots as continuity, incremental "patchworking"—across Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X's public communications. Together this cluster shows platforms actively managing, rather than merely concealing, their own consolidation of power, a discursive counterpart to the political-economic arguments above.

## The death of Twitter and the research infrastructure it took with it

Twitter/X functions across several papers as the paradigm case of platform decline and its consequences for the field itself. Axel Bruns's retrospective [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates the platform's arc from uniquely open, prosocial infrastructure for crisis reporting and hashtag activism to a Musk-era casualty of "enshittification," and argues that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) have cloned Twitter's form without solving its moderation and governance failures. Wang et al.'s [[Wang2026-ub]] empirically tests this verdict on one migrating community, finding that even highly motivated, well-networked academic early adopters largely failed to sustain the Mastodon migration, with retention concentrated only in field-specific servers. This decline is inseparable from a parallel research-infrastructure collapse: Bruns's earlier [[Bruns2019-nr]] documented the post–Cambridge Analytica "APIcalypse" as platforms' active suppression of critical scholarship rather than genuine privacy protection; Bastos's Bastos2025-ya and Murtfeldt et al.'s [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] together eulogize the "Twitter studies" subfield this API access enabled, with the latter quantifying a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024 following $42,000/month Enterprise pricing. Allcott et al.'s earlier [[Allcott2019-gn]] measurement of misinformation diffusion—showing Facebook engagement with fake news falling relative to Twitter's after 2016—stands as a data point from the pre-APIcalypse era whose replication has since become impossible, underscoring what was lost.

## Industry capture of the evidence base

A closely related concern is that the platforms' control over data access has compromised the independence of social media scholarship itself. Heiss et al.'s [[Heiss2026-qv]] draws explicit parallels to tobacco, pharma, and food-industry science, arguing that platforms' exclusive control of behavioral data makes academic research uniquely vulnerable to subtle, often undisclosed influence. Bak-Coleman et al.'s [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] substantiates this empirically at scale, finding roughly half of high-profile social media research in top journals carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small repeatedly-engaged cohort who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, with an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" once authors, editors, and reviewers are combined—and a systematic topical bias toward user-focused misinformation research over platform-dynamics research. Peters et al.'s [[Peters2026-mo]] examines the regulatory countervailing force, tracking how the EU's Digital Services Act Article 40 data-access provisions were shaped by contested stakeholder framings of "data quality," with platforms like Meta strategically inverting researchers' own arguments to resist obligations. Moran's [[Moran2025-qn]] extends this institutional erosion into the practitioner side, documenting upheaval in the professional "trust and safety" field itself amid layoffs and shifting political pressure. Bechmann's [[Bechmann2026-dr]] offers a conceptual response, arguing the DSA era demands moving beyond narrow causal-effects paradigms toward treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure in its own right.

## AI hype and the new imaginaries

As platformization pivots toward AI (per Helmond's [[Helmond2026-ll]] and Törnberg's [[Tornberg2026-lc]] accounts), a cluster of papers examines the discourse constructing this transition as inevitable and benevolent. Dodds et al.'s [[Dodds2026-df]] frames AI hype as a systemic infrastructure redistributing legitimacy and resources within journalism itself, with journalists occupying a contradictory dual role as both hype-makers and hype-watchers. Stanusch et al.'s [[Stanusch2026-ec]] dissects the Sam Altman controversy as a moment revealing industry strategies of "premediation" and "preclusion" that manage critique by displacing it into speculative futures or absorbing it as a problem only the industry can solve. Hepp's [[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates the picture ethnographically, distinguishing Silicon Valley's "loud futuring" (CEO pronouncements) from a "quiet futuring" among labs and communities (Effective Altruism, Rationalism) that supplies the ideational reservoir loud futuring later amplifies. Richter et al.'s [[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes this by tracing which stakeholders actually drove AI discourse on Twitter from 2012–2021, finding sharply diverging US (industry/influencer-dominated) and German (institutionalized, multi-stakeholder) trajectories that prefigure each country's subsequent GenAI regulation. Weinbrand's [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] extends the analysis to Google's AI Overviews, showing Google, journalists, and SEO marketers contesting the meaning of a generative reconfiguration of search itself, with users' own voices and environmental costs conspicuously absent throughout. Vertesi's decoy framework ([[Vertesi2026-lv]]) supplies the connective critique across this whole cluster: hype, safety framing, and regulatory courtship are not separate from the political economy of AI but constitutive of it.

## Governance, moderation, and democratic stakes

A final set of papers assesses what all of this has done to platforms' democratic and epistemic functions. Karo et al.'s [[Karo2026-dn]] documents how TikTok's recommendation architecture and moderation gaps are exploited by jihadist propagandists through "everyday extremism," echoing Gillespie's argument that reduction-based moderation ([[Gillespie2022-jx]]) leaves structural vulnerabilities unaddressed. Dehghan et al.'s [[Dehghan2026-sy]] complicates simple platform-effects narratives of polarization, showing on Reddit that fragmentation is "sedimented" through an entanglement of design, moderation, and discourse rather than reducible to any single cause—a caution echoed in Kristensen's [[Kristensen2025-ni]] more exploratory look at whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization. Lewandowsky's [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and Mahl et al.'s Delphi study [[Mahl2026-hc]] both press toward accountability and coordinated intervention: Lewandowsky links platform dynamics directly to global democratic backsliding, while the 47-expert Delphi in [[Mahl2026-hc]] concludes that no single fix—neither literacy nor fact-checking nor debunking—suffices, and that platform governance and journalism-ecosystem reform matter more, even as nearly half of experts expect the situation to worsen. Fletcher et al.'s long-run survey analysis [[Fletcher2026-lv]] supplies a sobering historical corrective to any nostalgia embedded in these narratives, showing that trust in news has been declining since the 1980s—predating the platform era—though accelerating post-2000, and that majority trust in news was never really the norm to begin with. Read against the anniversary essays that open this note, these governance-focused papers confirm the same verdict from a different angle: whatever "social media" once promised, its current trajectory is neither natural nor fixed, but the product of two decades of political-economic and infrastructural choices that remain, at least in principle, contestable.
