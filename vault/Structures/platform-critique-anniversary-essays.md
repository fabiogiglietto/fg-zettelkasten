# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The retrospective turn: why now?

A striking number of these papers share an occasion: an anniversary. [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Marwick2026-ss]], and [[Baym2026-tr]] all appear in the same anniversary issue of *Social Media + Society*, each taking stock of two decades of "social media" as an analytic category and finding the term worn thin. [[Helmond2026-ll]] performs the same gesture for "platformization," ten years after coining it. This clustering is itself a datum: when a field's founding vocabulary simultaneously comes due for reassessment across multiple authors, it signals that the object of study has genuinely changed shape, not merely that scholars are feeling nostalgic. [[Hegarty2026-qr]] supplies the meta-level diagnosis for this phenomenon, theorizing "internet decline narratives" (IDNs) as a historiographical genre in their own right — one that performs periodizing, blame-assigning, and ideal-imagining work, and that risks smuggling in a corporation-centric, Western-centric, Whiggish story even as it critiques Big Tech. Read against Hegarty's caution, the anniversary essays in this set become more interesting: they are not just offering IDNs but, in various ways, trying to specify precisely *what* changed, *when*, and *for whom* — the very questions Hegarty insists IDNs usually elide.

## From networked publics to algorithmic clusters

The most consistent empirical claim across the collection is that the architecture of "the social" in social media has been displaced by algorithmic curation decoupled from the social graph. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] gives this the fullest theoretical treatment, arguing that TikTok inaugurates a "second generation" of platforms organized around statistically-inferred "clustered publics" rather than boyd-style "networked publics" built on explicit friending and following. [[Boyd2026-op]] arrives at a parallel conclusion from a different angle, proposing that "social media" be renamed "parasocial media" since scrolling, influencer content, and one-sided attention have displaced reciprocal peer exchange. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] synthesizes and radicalizes both moves, marshaling Meta's own court-disclosed data (just 7% of Instagram time involves content from people one follows) to argue that the entire "social media" paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations: algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication. [[Marwick2026-ss]] supplies the elegiac counterpoint from inside this argument, using her own LiveJournal history to show concretely what reciprocity and vulnerability looked like before this shift, insisting the loss was a political-economic choice, not an inevitability. [[Helmond2026-ll]]'s account of Meta retiring the Like button in 2026 functions as a hinge-point artifact for this whole narrative — the literal decommissioning of the infrastructure that once captured social-graph signals, now superseded by AI-driven inference. Taken together, these pieces converge on a shared empirical substrate (declining posting, rising scrolling, algorithmic ranking by watch-time rather than social signal) while disagreeing productively about the right conceptual label for what comes after — clustered publics, parasociality, post-social formations, or an "AI society" ([[Tornberg2026-im]]).

## Platform political economy: extraction, scam, and power consolidation

A second thread insists that these formal/architectural shifts cannot be understood apart from political economy. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] remains the foundational statement here — platforms as a contested site between private gain and public value, not a neutral technological "revolution" — and [[Gillespie2010-as]] supplies the discursive prehistory, showing how the very word "platform" was built to launder commercial curation as neutral infrastructure. [[De2026-ld]] extends this into the present with "changecraft," documenting how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively manage their own transformations (patchworking, creator-benevolence framing, democratization rhetoric) to mask consolidation. [[Baym2026-tr]] ties this directly to wealth: revisiting her own 2015 diagnosis of four threats, she shows all four have intensified, with tech "broligarchs'" net worth and political power metastasizing in tandem with worsening algorithmic and labor conditions. [[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes the political-economy argument to its sharpest point, arguing that scam logics are not peripheral but *constitutive* of platform economies — that the boundary between legitimate hustle and fraud is being actively redrawn, and that anti-fraud infrastructure itself becomes a vector of surveillance and governance. [[Bruns2026-yv]] offers a case study in platform decline as political economy in action, tracing Twitter's arc from prosocial, flat-network openness to algorithmic self-promotion and "enshittification" under Musk, while noting that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) reproduce rather than resolve the underlying governance failures. Bastos2025-ya and [[Bruns2019-nr]] document the research-infrastructural casualties of this same consolidation: the API ecosystem that made "Twitter studies" possible is gone, and post-Cambridge-Analytica "APIcalypse" restrictions were less about privacy than about insulating platforms from critical scrutiny — a point reinforced empirically by [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]], which quantifies just how industry-entangled "independent" platform scholarship actually is (roughly 80% "industrial saturation" once editors and reviewers are counted).

## The generative-AI inflection

A third cluster treats generative AI not as a separate story but as platformization's next phase, deepening rather than interrupting the extractive logics already diagnosed. [[Tornberg2026-im]] is the pivotal theoretical statement, arguing for a tendential shift from "platform society" to "AI society" in which network-effect monopoly gives way to "cognitive lock-in" and the enclosure of cognition itself. [[Helmond2026-ll]]'s Project Suncatcher example and [[Tornberg2026-lc]]'s account of AI chatbot use overtaking posting both operationalize this same transition empirically. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] supplies the sharpest political-economy critique of AI discourse specifically, arguing that even well-intentioned accountability efforts get captured by "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that obscure the underlying "Project of AI" — a capital-accumulation and power-consolidation project dressed as a technical one. [[Hepp2026-oi]] and [[Richter2026-bt]] examine who gets to narrate AI's future, the former distinguishing Silicon Valley's "loud" CEO futuring from the "quiet futuring" of EA/Rationalist-adjacent communities, the latter tracing how US AI discourse shifted from institutional to individual-influencer dominance while Germany's institutionalized. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Dodds2026-df]] both focus on hype as an active, structuring mechanism rather than mere narrative — industry absorbing critique through "premediation" and "preclusion," journalism caught reproducing the hype it is meant to scrutinize. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows Google performing a comparable discursive repositioning with AI Overviews, recasting itself as epistemic authority while erasing user and environmental concerns from the discourse. At the cultural-reception end, [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Li2026-zv]] examine the detritus of this transition — "slop" and "brainrot" — as simultaneously degraded and strangely enchanting outputs, with ordinary users (per Li) offering more ambivalent, less monolithically declinist accounts than elite discourse would suggest, a nice empirical check on Hegarty's warning about IDNs flattening user agency.

## Democracy, trust, and the limits of platform research

A final arc interrogates whether existing scholarly and regulatory tools are adequate to any of this. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers perhaps the most pessimistic methodological verdict, arguing that the Meta2020 partnership shows empirical social science structurally cannot keep pace with platforms that change faster than studies can be designed, published, or replicated — a point echoed institutionally by [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]]'s industry-saturation findings and historically by Bastos2025-ya's and [[Bruns2019-nr]]'s accounts of vanishing data access. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] proposes moving past causal-effects research entirely toward treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure, a reframing partly enabled by the EU's DSA. [[Bennett2025-xs]] makes a structurally similar move for political communication, arguing that both "technocentric" (disinformation-focused) and purely "institutionalist" accounts of democratic backsliding miss how platforms enable new "digital surrogate organizations" that reorganize party politics from outside. [[Lukito2026-il]] and Kristensen2025-ic (on cross-platform curation and alternative-platform polarization) supply granular empirical traction on the fragmented, multi-platform media ecosystem these larger essays describe in the abstract. [[Fletcher2026-lv]], working from half a century of survey data, offers a useful corrective to declinist intuitions: trust in news has indeed fallen, but the decline predates the internet, began in the 1980s, and is not universal — rising, notably, in unfree-press countries — a finding that should discipline any temptation to over-attribute present ills to platforms alone. [[Hallin2004-kg]], though decades older than everything else here, functions as the comparative-institutional baseline against which all these platform-era claims about convergence, fragmentation, and declining trust are implicitly being tested — a reminder that media systems were contested, pluralistic, and historically contingent long before "platformization" became the dominant lens.
