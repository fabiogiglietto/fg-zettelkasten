# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The vocabulary crisis: renaming what "social media" has become

A cluster of anniversary essays converges on a single diagnostic move: the word "social" no longer describes what the platforms do. danah boyd's proposal to rename the object of study "parasocial media" [[Boyd2026-op]] and Nancy Baym's stock-taking against her own 2015 manifesto [[Baym2026-tr]] both argue that scrolling has displaced posting, that professionalized creator content has displaced peer exchange, and that this was a political-economic choice — financialization, consolidation, algorithmic incentive — rather than a technological inevitability. Alice Marwick's autoethnographic return to LiveJournal [[Marwick2026-ss]] supplies the counterfactual: the intimacy and reciprocity of the "old internet" were real, contingent achievements of small-scale, non-extractive infrastructure, not naive nostalgia. Axel Bruns extends the same elegiac register to Twitter specifically [[Bruns2026-yv]], tracing how a genuinely prosocial, flat network structure was progressively "enshittified" through mismanagement and then weaponized disinformation, leaving successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) to inherit its unsolved moderation problems without its openness. Paolo Gerbaudo's account of TikTok's "clustered publics" [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] gives this shift its structural mechanism: the move from explicit, people-centric networked publics to implicit, item-centric interest clusters, a "second generation" of social media that other platforms are now imitating. Anders Törnberg's "post-social media studies" [[Tornberg2026-lc]] pushes this furthest, arguing the very category is dissolving into three divergent formations — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated chatbot communication — such that no single paradigm can any longer hold the field together. Read as a set, these essays perform a collective act of conceptual mourning and reorientation, differing mainly in how much salvageable structure they see in the wreckage: boyd and Baym still want a renamed but continuous research object, while Törnberg and, in a different register, Anne Helmond argue for genuinely new units of analysis.

## Platformization's second act: extraction, scams, and infrastructure beyond the browser

Helmond's own decade-later reassessment of "platformization" [[Helmond2026-ll]] anchors a second thread: the political economy that produced parasocial, post-social media is now expanding into new domains — AI infrastructure, space-based compute, military and energy sectors — while the original social-graph logic (symbolized by the retiring Facebook Like button) recedes. This vertical/infrastructural expansion resonates with Susan Swartz's argument that social media has entered a "scam age" [[Swartz2026-zb]], in which fraud is not peripheral but constitutive of platform business models and governance, with anti-fraud infrastructure itself becoming a mechanism of surveillance and economic citizenship. Both papers insist that today's platform economy cannot be understood through content or discourse alone; it must be traced through infrastructure, capital, and the blurring of legitimate and illegitimate economic activity. Jenny Vertesi and colleagues generalize this move into a methodological warning [[Vertesi2026-lv]]: critiques of AI that fixate on "decoys" (ontology, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulation) inadvertently legitimate the underlying "Project of AI" — the financialized world-building that platformization scholars like Helmond are now tracking into AI and space. Ayşe De's concept of "changecraft" [[De2026-ld]] and Bridget Hurcombe's discourse analysis of Meta's Newsroom [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] supply the micro-level rhetorical machinery for this same macro story: platforms narrate their own restructuring — reframing ideological pivots as continuity, externalizing blame onto "inauthentic" outside actors — in ways that mask power consolidation. Weinbrand's study of Google's AI Overviews [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows the identical discursive logic migrating to search, as Google recasts itself from indexical gatekeeper to generative epistemic authority while eliding user and environmental costs. Stanusch's analysis of the Altman controversy [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and Hepp's ethnography of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring" [[Hepp2026-oi]] extend the same critique to the imaginaries industry actors deploy to pre-occupy AI's future and forestall present-tense accountability — with Richter's longitudinal Twitter study [[Richter2026-bt]] showing empirically how unevenly, and country-specifically, industry voices actually came to dominate that discourse over the 2010s.

## The evidentiary crisis: who gets to study the platforms, and on whose terms

A third arc concerns the material conditions of knowing any of this. Murtfeldt's "eulogy" to the Twitter API [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos's companion retrospective Bastos2025-ya document, with citation counts and API taxonomies respectively, how the closure of open data access dismantled a whole subfield of "Twitter studies," turning what was once the discipline's richest empirical commons into an expensive, gatekept resource. Wang's study of the failed academic migration to Mastodon [[Wang2026-ub]] shows the human cost of that closure in miniature: even a coordinated, well-networked community of scholars could not sustain an alternative once Twitter's infrastructural advantages of scale and discoverability were absent. Peters's analysis of the DSA's Article 40 data-access regime [[Peters2026-mo]] and Heiss's commentary on industry influence over academic research [[Heiss2026-qv]] pick up where the API era's collapse leaves off, showing that even regulator-mandated access is contested terrain where "data quality" becomes a politically negotiated category rather than a neutral methodological one, and where platforms structurally resemble tobacco or pharma industries in their capacity to bias the science studying them. Bak-Coleman's quantitative audit of undisclosed industry ties in high-profile social media research [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] supplies the most damning evidence for this claim, estimating roughly 80% "industrial saturation" of the literature once authors, editors, and reviewers are combined — a finding that retroactively colors how skeptically one should read even well-cited platform-effects research. Bechmann's call to move "beyond causal effects" toward treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure [[Bechmann2026-dr]] can be read as a programmatic response to this same evidentiary bind: if platform-controlled data access biases which causal claims can even be tested, the field's whole epistemic orientation may need to shift.

## Governance under strain: moderation, polarization, and democratic backsliding

A final cluster surveys what platforms' current configuration is doing to public discourse and safety. Moran's interviews with trust-and-safety practitioners [[Moran2025-qn]] document the institutional retrenchment of content moderation itself, a professional field now destabilized by political pressure and layoffs — a finding that resonates with Lewandowsky's blunter claim that platforms bear direct responsibility for global democratic backsliding [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]. Karo's study of jihadist "everyday extremism" on TikTok [[Karo2026-dn]] shows concretely how moderation gaps and platform vernaculars are exploited, while Dehghan's Reddit study [[Dehghan2026-sy]] complicates any simple platform-affordance story of polarization by showing it is "sedimented" through an entanglement of discourse, moderation, and user agency rather than caused by any single design feature — a caution echoed more briefly in Kristensen's inquiry into whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization [[Kristensen2025-ni]]. Mahl's Delphi study [[Mahl2026-hc]] synthesizes exactly this multi-causal picture into policy terms, with expert consensus favoring platform-governance and journalism-ecosystem interventions over individual-level fixes, and anticipating (correctly, in hindsight, given the AI-hype essays above) that generative AI would scramble the landscape faster than experts foresaw. Fletcher's half-century global trend data on trust in news [[Fletcher2026-lv]] situates all of this against a longer durée: institutional trust in media was already declining before the internet and is not a uniquely digital-era pathology, even as its post-2000 acceleration coincides with platformization — a useful corrective to essays that risk over-attributing contemporary malaise to the last decade alone. Dodds's editorial on AI hype in journalism [[Dodds2026-df]] closes the loop back to the collection's opening theme, showing how journalism itself becomes complicit in the same anticipatory, inevitability-laden discourse that Vertesi, Stanusch, and Hepp diagnose within the industry proper — suggesting that the retrospective mood of this whole literature is not simply about mourning social media's past, but about refusing the industry's terms for narrating its future.
