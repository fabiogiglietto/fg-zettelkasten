# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## An anniversary cluster: naming what came after "social media"

A striking number of these papers are explicitly occasioned by anniversaries — twenty years of "social media" scholarship, ten years since Nancy Baym's original manifesto, a decade since Anne Helmond coined "platformization." [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Baym2026-tr]], [[Helmond2026-ll]], and [[Marwick2026-ss]] all use the retrospective form to perform the same diagnostic move: holding up early-2000s platforms (Friendster-era Facebook, LiveJournal) against today's feeds to argue that something categorical, not merely incremental, has changed. boyd's proposal to rename the field's object "parasocial media" ([[Boyd2026-op]]) and Törnberg's call for a "post-social media studies" ([[Tornberg2026-lc]]) converge on the same empirical anchor — Meta's own disclosure that only 7–17% of feed time involves content from one's network — to argue that the social graph has been displaced by algorithmic recommendation and passive spectatorship. Marwick's autoethnographic account of LiveJournal ([[Marwick2026-ss]]) supplies the affective counterpoint to this structural argument: what was lost was not just reciprocity-as-metric but intimacy, vulnerability, and care, and she insists this loss was a political-economic choice, not a technological inevitability. Baym's essay ([[Baym2026-tr]]) is the field's conscience in this cluster: revisiting her own four 2015 threats (inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction), she finds all four worse, and uses this to indict critique-only scholarship, calling instead for collaborative, world-shaping research — a methodological challenge the rest of the cluster implicitly takes up.

Hegarty's historiographical intervention ([[Hegarty2026-qr]]) sits one level up from this whole genre, naming it: "internet decline narratives" (IDNs) are now the dominant mode for narrating recent internet history, performing periodization, blame-assignment, and nostalgia-work. Read against the other anniversary essays, Hegarty's piece is a productive irritant — it warns that declinist nostalgia (implicitly present in Marwick's and boyd's accounts) risks valorizing a smaller, whiter, wealthier "old good internet" and reproducing corporate-centric historiography even while critiquing corporations. The cluster as a whole is thus self-aware about its own genre conventions, even as it keeps reproducing them.

## Genealogies of "platform" as a contested term

Three papers trace the concept of platformization itself across time. Gillespie's foundational analysis ([[Gillespie2010-as]]) shows how "platform" was from the outset a piece of discursive engineering — a word chosen for its connotations of neutrality and openness that let YouTube address users, advertisers, and regulators simultaneously while eliding its curatorial interventions. Van Dijck's *The Platform Society* ([[van-Dijck2018-up]]) scales this up into a societal thesis: platforms don't just intermediate but *produce* social structure, and the 2010s were defined by contests between private platform logics and public values across sectors like transport, news, and healthcare. Helmond's own tenth-anniversary reflection on the term she coined ([[Helmond2026-ll]]) completes the arc by diagnosing the concept's erosion through overuse, and relaunching it as a situated "transcoding" process — newly visible, she argues, in Meta's retirement of the Like button (severing ties to the open-web social graph) and Google's space-based AI infrastructure ambitions. De's concept of "changecraft" ([[De2026-ld]]) extends Gillespie's original insight about platform rhetoric into the present, showing empirically how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively manage constant change — not through concealment but through staged visibility, continuity-framing, and incremental "patchworking" — confirming that platform self-narration remains as central to platform power now as it was in 2010.

## Scams, gaming, and extractive logics as organizing principles

A second strand argues that what has filled the vacuum left by "the social" is not simply algorithmic passivity but an intensified extractive and fraudulent political economy. Swartz's essay ([[Swartz2026-zb]]) is the most provocative claim here: scams are not peripheral noise but a central organizing logic of platform economies, with anti-fraud infrastructures themselves becoming governance mechanisms that license surveillance and define economic citizenship. This genealogically connects to Petre, Nieborg, and colleagues' earlier analysis of "platform paternalism" ([[Petre2019-zh]]), which shows platforms moralizing the boundary between legitimate and illegitimate (gaming the algorithm) visibility strategies in ways that always serve platform material interests — an argument Swartz's scam framework essentially generalizes from cultural production to the whole digital economy. De's changecraft ([[De2026-ld]]) again complements this by showing the discursive techniques (creator benevolence, democratization rhetoric) that launder these extractive rule changes as user-serving.

## The death and diaspora of Twitter

A tight empirical sub-cluster treats Twitter/X's collapse as a natural experiment in platform decline and migration. Bastos's retrospective on Twitter's API ecosystem (Bastos2025-ya) documents the infrastructural conditions that made "Twitter studies" possible and argues their loss is a rupture for computational social science itself. Bruns's essay ([[Bruns2026-yv]]) narrates the fuller arc — Twitter's flat, open network once enabled prosocial crisis communication and hashtag activism, but mismanagement, commercialization, and "participatory disinformation" eroded this, with Musk's acquisition accelerating "enshittification" into a far-right fringe platform. Wang's empirical study of academic migration to Mastodon ([[Wang2026-ub]]) supplies the downstream evidence: even a highly motivated, well-networked cohort largely failed to sustain the move, with retention driven by discipline-specific servers and cross-server engagement rather than internal cohesion — concrete confirmation of Bruns's claim that cloning Twitter's form without solving its governance and discoverability problems produces fragmented, incomplete exodus rather than renewal.

## From platform society to AI society

The largest and most forward-looking cluster asks what comes after platformization, converging on generative AI as the next infrastructural and political-economic rupture. Törnberg and Uitermark's companion pieces frame this most explicitly: where [[Tornberg2026-lc]] diagnoses the dissolution of "social media" into algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private spheres, and AI-mediated communication, [[Tornberg2026-im]] theorizes the macro-transition "from the platform society to the AI society," arguing that platform-era network-effect monopolies are giving way to "cognitive lock-in," predictive logics to generative ones, and visibility politics to "alignment politics" — in each case an enclosure of cognition itself rather than merely of social interaction. Vertesi's political-economy intervention ([[Vertesi2026-lv]]) offers a structurally similar but more polemical argument, naming AI development a "Project" of capital accumulation and identifying five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that distract accountability scholarship from the underlying financialization and network power — a direct challenge to much FAccT-style AI ethics work, and a conceptual cousin of Swartz's point that apparently neutral governance infrastructures (anti-fraud, AI safety) actually legitimate extraction.

Several empirical papers texture this transition. Weinbrand's discourse analysis of Google's AI Overviews ([[Weinbrand2026-sf]]) shows the same "changecraft"-style legitimation work (per [[De2026-ld]]) operating specifically around AI's redefinition of search as epistemic authority. Hepp's ethnography of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring" ([[Hepp2026-oi]]) and Stanusch's controversy-mapping of the Altman firing ([[Stanusch2026-ec]]) both examine how AI imaginaries — Longtermism, techno-hagiography, AGI inevitability — are produced and defended by industry-adjacent communities, with Stanusch's "premediation" and "preclusion" concepts describing precisely the discursive absorption-of-critique mechanism Vertesi theorizes more abstractly as "decoys." Richter's longitudinal stakeholder analysis of AI discourse on Twitter from 2012–2021 ([[Richter2026-bt]]) historicizes this: US discourse tilted toward industry and later individual tech-influencer dominance, while German discourse institutionalized around academia and advocacy, a divergence the authors argue pre-shaped each country's subsequent GenAI governance. Dodds's editorial on AI hype in journalism ([[Dodds2026-df]]) closes the loop by showing how journalism itself — reliant on the same narrow sourcing pool of executives — becomes complicit infrastructure for hype, echoing Hepp's and Stanusch's findings about the self-reinforcing, industry-anchored nature of AI's public imaginaries.

## Platforms, propaganda, and democratic backsliding

A final thread connects platformization explicitly to political crisis. Bennett and Livingston ([[Bennett2025-xs]]) argue that neither purely technocentric (disinformation/algorithm) nor purely institutionalist accounts of democratic backsliding suffice, proposing that platforms enable new "digital surrogate organizations" that pull parties toward illiberalism — a framework that reframes platform effects as organizational rather than merely persuasive. Lewandowsky's shorter commentary ([[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]) makes a blunter accountability claim in the same vein, insisting platforms bear responsibility for global democratic retreat. Beacken and colleagues ([[Beacken2026-zb]]) push back against deterministic readings of this story, showing comparatively that generative AI's use in propaganda across six democratically weakened states is shaped by local structural conditions rather than following one uniform trajectory. Topinka's theorization of "slopaganda" ([[Topinka2026-hb]]) supplies the aesthetic-affective mechanism that threads these political-economy arguments together with the AI-society cluster above: AI political content works not by deceiving but by affectively attuning to ambient platform moods, with models functioning as "ideological attunement machines" that intensify existing reactionary formations rather than imposing new beliefs — a fitting capstone image for a literature that, across all these registers, keeps finding that the infrastructures built to connect, inform, or protect us have instead become engines for extracting attention, capital, and now cognition itself.
