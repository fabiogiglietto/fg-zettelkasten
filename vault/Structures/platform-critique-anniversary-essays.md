# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The anniversary moment: naming what social media has become

Social Media + Society's twentieth-anniversary issue supplies the gravitational center of this cluster, and its contributors converge on a striking consensus: the object named by "social media" two decades ago no longer exists in recognizable form. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes the sharpest terminological intervention, arguing that platforms once built for reciprocal exchange now reward scrolling over posting and one-sided attachment to influencers over mutual friendship — hence "parasocial media." [[Tornberg2026-lc]] arrives at a structurally similar diagnosis from a different angle, showing via Meta's own litigation data that only a fraction of feed time involves content from people one follows, and arguing that recommendation systems, generative AI, and the retreat into semi-private spaces have splintered "social media" into three post-social formations — algorithmic broadcasting, bounded micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication — that no longer share a common analytic frame. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own decade-old coinage of "platformization" and finds it similarly exhausted by overuse, proposing to re-anchor it around AI-centered "transcoding" rather than infrastructural expansion, using Meta's retirement of the Like button and Google's off-world AI compute ambitions (Project Suncatcher) as bookends of a shift from social-graph capture to inferential, AI-driven personalization.

Against this diagnostic register, [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Baym2026-tr]] write more elegiac and activist versions of the same argument. Marwick's autoethnography of LiveJournal recovers a lost mode of intimacy and reciprocal vulnerability, insisting that today's alienating, surveillant environment was a political-economic choice rather than a technological inevitability — a claim that directly complements Boyd's and Törnberg's structural accounts by supplying the counterfactual "what could have been." Baym revisits her own 2015 manifesto and finds that all four of the threats she named then — inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction — have deepened rather than resolved, crystallized now in the "broligarchy" and in generative AI's appropriation of user-generated content. Where Marwick gestures toward small-scale, care-oriented alternatives, Baym calls more pointedly for researchers to abandon critique-for-its-own-sake and pursue cross-sector intervention.

## Twitter as case study in decline

Two essays narrow the anniversary reflection to a single platform's arc. Bastos2025-ya treats Twitter's API ecosystem as a research infrastructure whose closure ruptured an entire subfield ("Twitter studies"), a loss that is less about platform culture than about the political economy of data access itself. [[Bruns2026-yv]] tells the fuller story of that platform's "death," tracing how a genuinely prosocial, flat network structure was eroded first by chronic mismanagement and commercial pressure and then, after Musk's acquisition, by outright weaponization for far-right amplification — and it extends the anniversary essays' skepticism about successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky), arguing that formal cloning of Twitter's affordances without solving its governance failures reproduces the same vulnerabilities. This case study substantiates, empirically, the more abstract claims in Boyd and Marwick about consolidation and enshittification as contingent, not inevitable, outcomes.

## Extraction, scams, and the political economy underneath the platforms

A second strand pushes past the "what happened to sociality" question to ask what economic logic now organizes platforms. [[Swartz2026-zb]] is the most provocative entry here, arguing that scams are not peripheral to platform economies but a central organizing logic — that platform rules, anti-fraud infrastructures, and hustle culture are being jointly rebuilt around a blurred boundary between legitimate and illegitimate economic activity. This reframes Baym's and Marwick's laments about extraction: for Swartz, extraction itself has become indistinguishable from swindling, and anti-fraud governance doubles as a pretext for expansive behavioral surveillance. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] generalizes this suspicion of surface-level critique to AI specifically, arguing that debates over what AI "is," whether it is "inevitable," how "disruptive" it will be, whether it is "safe," and how it should be "regulated" function as decoys that distract accountability scholars from the underlying Project of AI — the assemblage of capital, infrastructure, and network power that a handful of firms are using to restructure whole economies. Read alongside Swartz, Vertesi suggests that scam logics and AI hype are two faces of the same phenomenon: discursive category-work (calling something a scam, or a safety concern, or a regulatory opportunity) that legitimates concentrated economic power while appearing to contest it.

[[Bennett2025-xs]] supplies the macro-political correlate of this extractive turn, arguing that platform-enabled "digital surrogate organizations" — QAnon, Stop the Steal, Proud Boys networks — have done for illiberal party politics what platformized scam and hustle economies have done for the individual user: knit together previously scattered actors into electorally and economically consequential networks. [[Nangle2026-yo]] offers a texture-level illustration of how such normalization actually operates on the ground, showing how far-right Instagram content uses positive affect, humor, and algorithmic proximity to soften extremist messaging into mainstream circulation — a micro-mechanism consistent with Bennett and Livingston's account of connective, organizationally-enabled radicalization.

## Governance's retreat and the bureaucratization of security

If the anniversary essays diagnose platforms' abandonment of sociality and the political-economy essays diagnose their extractive core, a third group examines the institutional apparatus meant to hold platforms accountable — and finds it hollowing out or mutating. [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the practical collapse of the trust-and-safety profession under political pressure and cost-cutting, a finding that sits uneasily beside [[Crosset2026-mq]]'s more structural account of how EU and US legislatures have responded by converting content moderation into "traffic control": three distinct circulation regimes (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimization) that nonetheless converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" via transparency mandates, auditing, and reporting. Where Moran shows platforms retreating from safety commitments, Crosset shows states filling the vacuum not with direct content control but with administrative proceduralization — a complementary rather than contradictory picture of drift and re-regulation happening simultaneously. [[De2026-ld]] bridges these two dynamics discursively, showing how platforms narrate their own incessant policy and feature changes ("changecraft") as neutral, responsive, and community-driven even as they consolidate power and deepen user dependency — the rhetorical labor that makes Moran's retreat and Crosset's re-regulation both look, from the platform's press office, like continuity rather than rupture.

## AI as the next platformization frontier

Finally, several papers extend this whole apparatus of diagnosis onto the generative-AI moment that Helmond and Törnberg flag as the successor to "social media" proper. [[Dodds2026-df]] treats AI hype in journalism as a systemic infrastructure — not mere narrative — that redistributes legitimacy and resources while obscuring hidden labor and material costs, echoing Vertesi's decoy framework but applied specifically to journalistic sourcing and professional practice. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] examines a concrete controversy (the Altman firing/rehiring) to show how industry actors deploy "premediation" and "preclusion" to displace present accountability onto speculative futures — a discursive move structurally identical to Vertesi's "inevitability" and "safety" decoys. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this by mapping the less visible "quiet futuring" of Bay Area AI communities (Effective Altruism, Rationalism) that supplies the ideational reservoir loud CEO discourse condenses and amplifies — showing that the imaginaries Stanusch and Dodds trace at the level of controversy and journalism have a deeper, place-based social infrastructure behind them. Together these AI-focused pieces suggest that the extractive, scam-adjacent, hype-driven dynamics diagnosed for social media proper are now reproducing themselves, with even greater opacity, in the platformization of AI itself — confirming Helmond's and Törnberg's shared prediction that the next decade's structure notes will need a vocabulary built for "post-social" and AI-centered platforms rather than the social-graph paradigm this whole cluster is busy eulogizing.
