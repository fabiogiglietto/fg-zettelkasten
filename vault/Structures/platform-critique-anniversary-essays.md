# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The unraveling of "social media" as a concept

The essays gathered here converge on a single diagnostic occasion: the twentieth-anniversary moment of "social media" scholarship, and the sense that the term itself no longer describes what it once did. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media," arguing that platforms once built for reciprocal peer exchange now reward scrolling over posting and one-sided attention to influencers over mutual friendship. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the structural mechanism behind this shift, theorizing TikTok's move from "networked publics" organized around explicit ties to "clustered publics" assembled algorithmically from implicit behavioral signals like watch time—a second-generation social form that other platforms are now converging toward ("TikTokification"). [[Tornberg2026-lc]] pushes this furthest, arguing the social-media paradigm is dissolving altogether into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated chatbot communication—citing Meta's own admission that only 7–17% of feed time involves content from people one follows. [[Helmond2026-ll]] narrates the same rupture from the vantage of "platformization," her own decade-old coinage: the retirement of Facebook's Like button symbolizes the end of the open-web social-graph paradigm and the pivot toward AI-driven inference, transcoding entire new sectors (space, defense, energy) into "platform-ready" domains. Together these pieces mark less a single event than a shared perception across the field that the analytic vocabulary built for 2006–2015 platforms has come unmoored from what platforms in 2026 actually do.

Personal and historical retrospectives sharpen the normative stakes of this diagnosis. [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Baym2026-tr]] both look backward to argue that today's conditions were not inevitable: Marwick's fieldnotes from LiveJournal recover a lost mode of intimacy and reciprocity foreclosed by contemporary surveillance and scale, while Baym revisits her own 2015 manifesto to show that the four threats she named—inequality, opaque algorithms, precarious labor, data extraction—have all deepened, embodied in the "broligarchy" of Zuckerberg, Musk, and Brin. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates a parallel arc specifically for Twitter/X, tracing its decline from a uniquely open, prosocial infrastructure for crisis reporting and hashtag activism into a mismanaged, Musk-accelerated case of "enshittification," and arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) reproduce Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. The infrastructural cost of that decline is made empirically concrete by [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya, who document the collapse of Twitter's API ecosystem and the resulting stagnation of "Twitter studies," a golden age of open platform research now closing.

## Extraction, scams, and the political economy of platforms

If the first cluster asks what "social" still means, a second asks what economic logic now organizes platforms. [[Swartz2026-zb]] is the volume's most provocative reframing: scams are not peripheral criminality but a central organizing logic of platform economies, with anti-fraud infrastructure itself functioning as a governance mechanism that authorizes surveillance and defines economic citizenship. This resonates with [[Donovan2025-ws]]'s history of "misinformation-at-scale," which likewise insists that engagement-optimized business models generate harm as a feature, not a bug, and traces how platforms migrated from non-intervention to behavioral moderation and back to retrenchment once political backlash arrived. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] generalizes this insight into a theory of AI as a "Project"—a world-building enterprise in which critiques of algorithms, safety, and regulation function as "decoys" that distract from the underlying consolidation of capital and infrastructural power. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this with an ethnography of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring," showing how Effective Altruist and Rationalist communities supply the ideational reservoir that "loud futuring" (CEOs, investors) later amplifies—apolitical self-presentation masking deeply political AGI, labor, and geopolitical framings. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] extend this into discourse analysis proper: the Altman-firing controversy reveals an industry using "premediation" and "preclusion" to occupy the future and deflect present accountability, while Google's AI Overviews are shown to redefine search itself as a contested site of epistemic authority, with users' and environmental concerns notably absent from all sides of the debate. [[Dodds2026-df]] rounds out this cluster by arguing AI hype is not mere narrative but a structuring infrastructure within journalism itself, reshaping funding, sourcing, and legitimacy before AI's promises even materialize.

Labor and creator-economy papers show how these political-economic pressures land on ordinary platform participants. [[Volpe2026-um]] finds that Italian micro-influencers' performances of sustainability and care in second-hand fashion are best read as survival strategies under dual precarity—unstable labor markets and relentless algorithmic production demands—echoing Baym's and Swartz's broader claims that hustle, aspiration, and extraction have become intertwined rather than opposed. [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" then shows how platforms themselves narrate their own restructuring—staging infrastructural shifts as aesthetic progress and ideological pivots as mere continuity—precisely to manage the user dependency and power consolidation that Helmond, Swartz, and Baym describe from outside.

## Moderation, governance, and the limits of platform self-regulation

A third arc concerns how platforms and states manage harmful content once its centrality to the business model is acknowledged. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects Meta's Newsroom discourse, showing how "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" and similar floating signifiers let Meta position itself as both outside the problem and the sole capable solver, a rhetorical move that diffused into regulatory language itself. [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the resulting institutional fallout: the professional field of "trust and safety" is destabilizing under political pressure and layoffs, jeopardizing the moderation capacity Donovan and Hurcombe describe as always politically contingent. Case studies make the stakes concrete: [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows deplatformed figure Andrew Tate sustained by an "ambient ideology" ecosystem that YouTube's recommendation engine outpaces its own removal efforts; [[Karo2026-dn]] finds jihadist propaganda thriving through "everyday extremism" that exploits the same vernacular affordances (memes, emoji, audio manipulation) documented across adversarial communities; [[Nangle2026-yo]] shows far-right Instagram content normalizing extremism through humor and positive affect rather than hostility; and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] documents Filipino Gen Z "dogshows" normalizing legal-but-harmful incivility as entertainment. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] adds algorithmic-auditing precision to this picture, showing that Twitter's post-Musk "For You" feed rewarded agitation and proximity to Musk himself rather than political leaning per se—evidence against both neutral "town square" claims and simple partisan-bias narratives.

Governance responses to these dynamics are themselves fragmenting. [[Crosset2026-mq]] identifies three divergent legal regimes—free circulation, patrolling, flow optimization—that nonetheless converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" via transparency and auditing mandates. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues DSA-era scholarship must move past narrow causal-effects research toward treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure, a call echoed empirically by [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment, which shows engagement-based feeds distort perceived social norms around political dialogue (not just attitudes) and that alternative "diversified extremity" algorithms can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] complicates optimism about crowdsourced alternatives, showing Community Notes' bridging-based design structurally undermoderates polarizing content precisely where electoral risk is highest, while [[Goldberg2026-eb]]'s expert-convened position paper surveys LLM-enabled bridging, deliberation, and moderation tools as a potentially second paradigm shift for digital public squares—cautiously optimistic where Bouchaud is skeptical.

## Contesting the causal narrative, and the crisis of researching platforms at all

Running beneath these accounts is a methodological and evidentiary anxiety about how confidently any of this can be claimed. [[Budak2024-ef]] pushes back against alarmist consensus, arguing public discourse on misinformation's prevalence, algorithmic drivers, and societal harm has diverged from more modest empirical findings—a corrective in tension with Donovan's and Bruns's more totalizing accounts of platform-driven decay. [[Bennett2025-xs]] similarly complicates technocentric explanations of democratic backsliding, arguing that platforms matter less through individual-level disinformation effects than through enabling "digital surrogate organizations" that pull parties toward illiberalism—an institutionalist corrective that reframes [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]'s blunter claim that platforms bear direct responsibility for democratic retreat. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] asks a related empirical question about whether alternative platforms themselves drive polarization, without resolving it.

Underwriting all these disputes is a disquieting infrastructural fact: [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] finds that roughly half of high-profile social media research carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small elite of scientists, editors, and reviewers, systematically skewing the field's topical focus toward user-level misinformation and away from platform-scale dynamics—precisely the blind spot Budak, Bennett, and Tornberg each gesture toward from different directions. Read together with Murtfeldt's and Bastos's elegies for the Twitter API, this suggests the retrospective mood of this literature is not merely about platforms changing under our feet, but about the scholarly apparatus built to study them eroding in tandem—leaving the field, as Baym insists, needing not just sharper critique but new collaborative and infrastructural strategies to keep pace with a platform ecology that has already moved on from "social media" as anyone understood it a decade ago.
