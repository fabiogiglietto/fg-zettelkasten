# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The Anniversary Moment: Diagnosing a Field in Crisis

A cluster of essays written for platform studies' twentieth-anniversary moment converges on a single diagnosis: the thing scholars have called "social media" for two decades no longer behaves as advertised, and the analytic vocabulary built to describe it is running out of grip. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring the term altogether in favour of "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has replaced posting and one-sided attention to influencers has displaced reciprocal peer exchange. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] goes further, declaring the "social media" paradigm itself unraveling under three pressures—algorithmic recommendation replacing the social graph, generative AI producing "platforms without users," and users retreating into semi-private enclaves—and calls for a genuinely post-social vocabulary. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the structural mechanism behind both diagnoses: a shift from "networked publics," built on explicit interpersonal ties, to "clustered publics," statistically inferred from implicit behavioural signals like watch time, with TikTok as the template that other platforms are now imitating. Together these three pieces triangulate the same transformation—call it depersonalization, parasociality, or post-sociality—as a change in the *social form* of platforms, not merely their content or governance.

[[Baym2026-tr]] and [[Marwick2026-ss]] anchor this diagnosis historically and affectively. Baym revisits her own 2015 manifesto to show that the four threats she named then—wealth inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious creative labour, unchecked data extraction—have all intensified rather than resolved, now compounded by "broligarch" political power and generative AI's appropriation of user content. Marwick's autoethnography of LiveJournal supplies the lived counterpoint: a texture of intimacy, reciprocity, and vulnerability that today's platforms actively foreclose, framing the present not as technological destiny but as the sediment of specific political-economic choices. Read alongside [[Bruns2026-yv]]'s obituary for Twitter—which traces the platform's fall from a flat, open, prosocial network into a "far-right fringe platform" under Musk—the anniversary essays collectively narrate a fall from a more open, sociable internet into one organized around extraction, spectacle, and algorithmic curation.

## Platformization's Second Act: From Infrastructure to AI

[[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own foundational concept a decade on, arguing that platformization is entering an AI-centred phase: the retirement of Facebook's Like button signals Meta's decoupling from the open-web social graph in favour of in-app behavioural inference, while Google's space-based AI infrastructure gestures toward a "Big AI" era populated by new actors (Nvidia, Anduril, Palantir) and new sectors (military, energy, space). This essay explicitly worries about the conceptual erosion of "platformization" through overuse, proposing to re-anchor it as a situated form of "transcoding." [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" operationalizes this same terrain empirically, showing how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively manage—rather than conceal—their own transformations, staging infrastructural shifts as aesthetic progress and ideological pivots as mere continuity. Where Helmond narrates platformization's macro-trajectory, De2026-ld supplies the micro-rhetorics by which platforms perform legitimacy while consolidating power, echoing Boyd's and Bruns's suspicion that platform "evolution" is rarely neutral.

The AI turn that Helmond and Törnberg flag as an emerging horizon is elaborated by a second tier of papers focused squarely on AI's political economy and imaginaries. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] argues that AI is best understood as a "Project"—a world-building enterprise through which financiers assemble networks of power—and that mainstream critiques (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) function as "decoys" that inadvertently legitimate this Project rather than contest it. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this with an ethnographic account of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring," showing how Effective Altruist and Rationalist communities supply the ideational reservoir that CEOs' "loud futuring" later amplifies. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] examines a concrete flashpoint—the Sam Altman controversy—to show how industry discourse manages critique through "premediation" and "preclusion," deflecting present harms into speculative futures. [[Dodds2026-df]] extends this to journalism itself, arguing AI hype is a structuring infrastructure that redistributes legitimacy and obscures hidden labour, while [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows Google's AI Overviews enacting a parallel discursive contest over epistemic authority in search. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] strikes a more optimistic note amid this chorus, proposing that LLM-enabled deliberation technologies could yet reshape digital public squares toward participation rather than extraction—an aspiration these other papers would treat with considerable suspicion.

## Extraction, Scam Logics, and the Political Economy of Harm

[[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes the retrospective mood toward its most provocative claim: that scams are not peripheral to platform economies but a central organizing logic, with anti-fraud infrastructures themselves becoming governance mechanisms that authorize surveillance and define economic citizenship. This resonates with Baym's account of "broligarchy" and hustle-culture financialization, and with [[Volpe2026-um]]'s finding that micro-influencers' performances of ethical "care" function simultaneously as reputational labour and survival strategy under precarious platform demands—a small-scale illustration of how platforms convert affect and ethics into extractable content. [[Bennett2025-xs]] scales this extractive logic up to democratic politics, arguing that digitally networked publics have become "digital surrogate organizations" pulling conservative parties toward illiberalism, a claim that [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] echoes in blunter terms by holding platforms directly accountable for global democratic backsliding.

Yet this consensus is not unanimous. [[Budak2024-ef]] cuts against the grain of alarmist narrative, arguing that public discourse about misinformation's harms diverges from the empirical evidence: exposure is concentrated rather than pervasive, algorithms are not the primary driver of exposure, and causal links to societal harm are weaker than commonly claimed. Read against Lewandowsky's and Bennett's confident causal claims, Budak2024-ef introduces a genuine fault line in the topic: how much of the "platforms broke society" narrative is itself a discursive construction requiring the same critical scrutiny that [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Dodds2026-df]] apply to AI hype. [[Kristensen2025-ni]]'s inquiry into whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization sits adjacent to this debate, testing empirically whether platform architecture itself (rather than content or actors) bears responsibility for fragmentation.

## Moderation's Limits and the Persistence of Harm

A cluster of case studies demonstrates concretely how the clustered-public, recommendation-driven architecture theorized by Gerbaudo and Törnberg produces governance failures that outpace remediation. [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows Andrew Tate more visible on YouTube after deplatforming than before, sustained by an "ambient ideology" that recommendation systems amplify faster than moderators can remove. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist supporters embedding propaganda in TikTok's vernacular culture—audio camouflage, meme infiltration, emoji codes—exploiting the same algorithmic and participatory affordances that Gerbaudo identifies as constitutive of "clustered publics." [[Nangle2026-yo]] extends this to far-right normalization on Instagram via humour and positive affect, describing an "affective-discursive ecology" in which platform logics, ideology, and emotion co-produce mainstreaming. Across all three, the diagnosis is structural rather than incidental: it is not that moderation is poorly executed but that recommendation architectures are fundamentally misaligned with removal-based governance, a point that dovetails with De2026-ld's account of platforms discursively managing rather than resolving such contradictions.

## Alternatives and Their Failures

If the anniversary essays gesture toward alternative futures—Marwick's small-scale, care-oriented infrastructures, Bruns's hope for better-moderated successors—the empirical record filed here is sobering. [[Wang2026-ub]] tracks academic Twitter's attempted migration to Mastodon after Musk's acquisition and finds that even a highly motivated, well-networked cohort largely failed to sustain the move, with most users drifting back to Twitter/X or scattering across Bluesky and Threads. Bruns's own account of Mastodon, Threads, and Bluesky as successor platforms reaches a parallel conclusion: they replicate Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. Taken together with Volpe's micro-influencers, who treat platform visibility not as aspiration but as portfolio-building survival, this topic's empirical papers consistently show individual and collective agency straining against, but rarely escaping, the structural logics the anniversary essays diagnose at the macro level—leaving the field, as Törnberg and Boyd both insist, in need of concepts adequate to a landscape that no longer looks "social" in any of the senses the term once promised.
