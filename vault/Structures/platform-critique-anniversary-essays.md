# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The unraveling of "social media" as a category

A striking convergence runs through this year's anniversary essays: the term "social media" itself is diagnosed as failing. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] makes the strongest structural claim, arguing that the social-graph paradigm that gave the term its coherence has been supplanted by algorithmic recommendation, generative AI, and user retreat into semi-private spaces—producing three distinct "post-social" formations that no longer share a common analytic object. [[Boyd2026-op]] arrives at a parallel diagnosis from a different angle, proposing "parasocial media" as the more accurate label for platforms now organized around scrolling, influencer content, and one-sided attention rather than reciprocal exchange. [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Baym2026-tr]] supply the affective and normative register for this same shift: both write as long-time participants revisiting their own earlier optimism (Marwick's LiveJournal years, Baym's 2015 manifesto) to show how intimacy and reciprocity gave way to surveillance, extraction, and outrage amplification. [[Helmond2026-ll]] anchors the diagnosis institutionally, using the death of Facebook's Like button to mark the end of the open-web, social-graph-capture model of platformization and the pivot toward "Big AI" infrastructure. Read together, these essays do not merely lament change—they converge on the claim, most explicit in Marwick and Boyd, that the current configuration was a contingent political-economic outcome, not a technological inevitability, and that the field's vocabulary must now catch up.

## Extraction, scams, and the political economy underneath

A second cluster pushes past diagnosis of platform *form* toward the economic logics that produced it. [[Swartz2026-zb]] offers the most provocative reframing, arguing that scams are not peripheral to platform economies but constitutive of them—entangled with anti-fraud governance, hustle culture, and the blurred boundary between legitimate and illegitimate economic activity that platforms now formalize as infrastructure. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] generalizes this insight to AI: what looks like technical debate over fairness or safety is often a "decoy" that obscures the underlying "Project of AI"—networks of capital and infrastructural power reorganizing markets in their own favor. [[De2026-ld]] documents the discursive machinery that sustains these arrangements day to day, showing how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X narrate constant change as creator-benevolent and participatory even as it consolidates control and deepens dependency—a rhetorical companion piece to Helmond's account of platforms' infrastructural pivot. Baym's ledger of worsening wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, and data extraction supplies the empirical backbone linking all three: the "broligarchy" that Swartz's scam economy and Vertesi's AI Project both ultimately serve.

## Platforms, democratic backsliding, and accountability

A third strand extends this political-economic critique into explicitly political territory. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] states baldly that platforms bear responsibility for democratic retreat, while [[Bennett2025-xs]] offers the most developed causal mechanism, arguing that "digital surrogate organizations"—memes, influencers, partisan networks—have pulled conservative parties toward illiberalism in ways that neither purely technocentric (disinformation-effects) nor purely institutionalist accounts can explain alone. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates this same dynamic through the specific case of Twitter/X, tracing how a once uniquely open, prosocial network structure was eroded by mismanagement and "participatory disinformation" before being captured outright by Musk, leaving successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) to replicate its form without solving its governance failures. [[Beacken2026-zb]] complicates any single narrative of platform-driven democratic decline by showing, comparatively across six weakening democracies, that generative-AI propaganda is shaped by local structural conditions rather than following one universal trajectory—a useful corrective to the more technologically deterministic tone of the Lewandowsky and Bennett pieces.

## The empirical and infrastructural costs of the transition

Several papers document the research and institutional fallout of this platform transformation rather than theorizing it directly. Bastos2025-ya and [[Wang2026-ub]] both examine the post-Twitter/X rupture from the inside of scholarship itself: Bastos as an elegy for the API-enabled "Twitter studies" subfield now foreclosed, Wang through a granular survival analysis showing that even highly motivated academic communities failed to sustain migration to Mastodon, undermining hopes that decentralized alternatives can simply inherit legacy social capital. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] complicates the scholarly vantage point further, revealing pervasive undisclosed industry ties in high-profile social media research—compounding the sense, running through Bastos and Wang, that the infrastructure for independently studying platforms is eroding in tandem with the platforms themselves. [[Moran2025-qn]] extends this institutional erosion to content moderation, documenting how the trust-and-safety profession is being dismantled by political pressure and layoffs just as platforms' extractive and disinformation dynamics (per Bruns, Bennett, Lewandowsky) intensify.

## AI hype, imaginaries, and the blurred line of authenticity

A final set of papers turns to how AI's ascendance is narrated and normalized, echoing Helmond's and Törnberg's claims that generative AI is reconfiguring platforms from within. [[Dodds2026-df]] frames AI hype as a systemic infrastructural force in journalism, not mere discourse—journalists simultaneously producing and policing narratives of inevitability that closely resemble the "changecraft" De describes for platform companies. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] examines a concrete flashpoint, the Sam Altman controversy, showing how industry actors deploy "premediation" and "preclusion" to keep control of AI's future narrative, a discursive strategy that dovetails with Vertesi's account of decoys shielding the underlying political economy. Against this backdrop of hype and blurred authorship, [[Graham2026-fb]] offers a smaller-scale but conceptually resonant case: Reddit's "botting" phenomenon shows that the human/bot binary is already breaking down at the level of everyday platform vernacular, anticipating in miniature the "platforms without users" future that Törnberg forecasts at scale.

Taken together, these nineteen essays trace a single arc: from the empirical unraveling of "social media" as an analytic and lived category, through the extractive and scam-adjacent political economies that underlie it, to its downstream consequences for democratic life, scholarly infrastructure, and the authenticity of what remains legible as human communication online.
