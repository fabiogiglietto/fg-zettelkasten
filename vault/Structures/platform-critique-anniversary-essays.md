# Retrospective Essays on Platformization and Social Media's Trajectory

## The unraveling of "social" media

The anchor of this cluster is a set of anniversary essays, many written for Social Media + Society's twentieth-anniversary issue, that converge on a single diagnosis: the thing called "social media" has stopped being social. danah boyd's [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring the term altogether in favor of "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has displaced posting and that platforms now reward professionalized, one-directional attention to influencers over reciprocal peer exchange. Nancy Baym's [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits her own 2015 manifesto and finds that all four threats she originally named—wealth inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction—have deepened rather than resolved, with "broligarchs" translating platform wealth directly into political power. Alice Marwick's [[Marwick2026-ss]] supplies the affective counter-memory to these structural diagnoses, using her own LiveJournal years to show what has been lost in intimacy, vulnerability, and reciprocal writing, and insisting that this loss was a political-economic choice, not a technological inevitability—a claim that rhymes with boyd's and Baym's rejection of determinism.

Anne Helmond's [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own decade-old coinage of "platformization" and uses Meta's retirement of the Like button and Google's space-based AI infrastructure to argue that the concept itself needs rescuing from conceptual erosion, redefining it as a situated process of making domains "platform-ready" rather than a synonym for digitization. Gerbaudo's [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] and Törnberg's [[Tornberg2026-lc]] give this shift its most systematic theoretical vocabulary: Gerbaudo names the move from "networked publics" to algorithmically-clustered, item-centric "social interest clusters," while Törnberg goes further, arguing the entire "social media" paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—each demanding distinct methods. Bruns's [[Bruns2026-yv]] retrospective on Twitter's "death" operationalizes these abstractions through a single case, tracing how a flat, open network built for prosocial crisis communication and hashtag activism was progressively "enshittified" through mismanagement, algorithmic feed changes, and Musk's acquisition, leaving successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) to inherit its unsolved governance problems without its openness.

## Scams, hype, and extractive logics as organizing principles

A second thread reframes the platform economy not as a degraded version of an earlier ideal but as a positively organized around extraction and deception. Eric Swartz's [[Swartz2026-zb]] essay is the sharpest statement: scams are not peripheral criminality but a central organizing logic of platform economies, with anti-fraud infrastructures themselves functioning as governance mechanisms that authorize surveillance and define economic citizenship. Vertesi's [[Vertesi2026-lv]] political-economy critique of AI extends this logic upward, arguing that AI development is a "Project" of world-building capital accumulation, and that critiques focused on algorithmic particulars function as "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that distract from and legitimate underlying financialization and network power. Stanusch's [[Stanusch2026-ec]] controversy-mapping of the Sam Altman firing shows these same industry dynamics in discursive miniature: "premediation" and "preclusion" strategies displace present harms onto speculative futures while positioning the AI industry as sole arbiter of its own risks. Dodds's [[Dodds2026-df]] editorial on AI hype in journalism and Weinbrand's [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] discourse analysis of Google's AI Overviews both document how these imaginaries are laundered through supposedly neutral intermediaries—journalists and search infrastructure—that reproduce inevitability narratives while erasing labor, environmental cost, and user voice. De's [[De2026-ld]] concept of "changecraft" names the connective tissue: platforms discursively manage their own transformations—staging infrastructural shifts as aesthetic progress, reframing ideological pivots as continuity—precisely to normalize the kind of extractive repositioning Swartz and Vertesi describe at the level of political economy.

## Democracy, polarization, and the limits of causal-effects research

A parallel arc addresses platforms' relationship to democratic backsliding, and stages a methodological argument about how that relationship should be studied. Lewandowsky's [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] short commentary states the accountability claim baldly: platforms are complicit in global democratic retreat. Bennett and Livingston's [[Bennett2025-xs]] essay supplies the more elaborate theoretical intervention, arguing that "technocentric" disinformation research and "institutionalist" accounts of party elites have remained artificially siloed, and proposing that digitally networked publics act as new "digital surrogate organizations" pulling conservative parties toward illiberalism—a framework built explicitly on, and extending, the connective-action tradition. Bechmann's [[Bechmann2026-dr]] conceptual intervention pushes in a complementary direction methodologically, arguing that DSA-era platform research should move beyond narrow causal-effects paradigms toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure. Brady's [[Brady2026-ln]] large-scale Bluesky field experiment operationalizes exactly this reorientation: rather than asking whether algorithms cause attitude change directly, it shows engagement-based feeds amplify moralized/emotional content and distort perceived social norms, with a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm partially repairing both without sacrificing platform enjoyment—concrete evidence that algorithmic design, not just discourse, is a lever for democratic repair. Kristensen's [[Kristensen2025-ni]] brief inquiry into whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization and Oswald's [[Oswald2025-km]] account of the "production-consumption gap"—whereby a small minority of hyperactive posters distorts both citizens' and researchers' perceptions of public opinion—round out this cluster by questioning the representativeness of the very traces on which platform-and-democracy claims are typically built.

## Everyday survival inside the platform machine

Set against these macro-diagnoses are ethnographic and case-based studies of how ordinary users adapt to platform demands from below. Volpe's [[Volpe2026-um]] study of Italian micro-influencers in second-hand fashion shows care and ethical branding operating as intertwined survival strategies against dual precarity—unstable labor markets and algorithmic production pressure—echoing Baym's and boyd's claims about precarious creative labor from the vantage of those actually performing it. Graham's [[Graham2026-fb]] analysis of Reddit "botting" complicates the human/bot binary central to platform-trust debates, arguing that authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance shaped by platform vernacular rather than a technical fact—a finding that quietly undercuts both Weinbrand's and Dodds's assumptions about clean distinctions between synthetic and human content. Wang's [[Wang2026-ub]] survival analysis of academic Twitter's failed migration to Mastodon provides an empirical postscript to Bruns's and Bastos's narratives of Twitter's decline, showing that even a highly motivated, well-connected community could not sustain a full exodus, with retention hinging on niche server communities and diverse cross-server engagement rather than the intensity of prior network ties.

## The crisis of the research apparatus itself

Finally, several papers turn the retrospective lens on platform studies as a field. Bastos's Bastos2025-ya chapter mourns the loss of Twitter's API ecosystem as a rupture in the infrastructure of computational social science, echoing Törnberg's broader claim that established methods are increasingly misaligned with post-social platform structures. Bak-Coleman's [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] audit of industry influence in high-profile social media research closes the loop on the entire topic's political-economy concerns by turning them reflexively on the discipline: roughly half of top-journal social media research has undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small elite of scientists and editors, systematically skewing the field's attention toward user-blaming topics like misinformation sharing and away from platform-level dynamics—a finding that retroactively conditions how skeptically one might read even some of the empirical claims elsewhere in this very corpus.
