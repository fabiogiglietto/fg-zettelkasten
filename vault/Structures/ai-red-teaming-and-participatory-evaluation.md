# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The sociotechnical turn in AI safety practice

The five papers in this cluster share a common polemical move: they refuse to let "AI safety" be reduced to a set of technical procedures performed by engineers on models. Instead, each insists that safety practices—red-teaming, evaluation, moderation, deliberation—are irreducibly social, organizational, and political, and that treating them otherwise produces both worse science and worse governance. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] make this case most directly for red-teaming; [[Matias2025-px]] extends it to quantitative AI evaluation writ large; [[Goldberg2026-eb]] applies the same sociotechnical lens to the design of digital public squares; and [[Vertesi2026-lv]] supplies a structural counter-argument, warning that even sociotechnical and participatory framings can become "decoys" that stabilize rather than challenge the underlying political economy of AI.

## Red-teaming as labor, value judgment, and public practice

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] opens the cluster's core argument by drawing a direct historical analogy between red-teaming and commercial content moderation: both are opaque, market-driven practices in which harm categories are defined internally by companies, labor is outsourced and precaritized, and workers absorb psychological costs (secondary trauma, moral injury) that are largely invisible to the public. The paper's central claim—that red-teaming embeds value judgments, labor arrangements, and human costs that are currently hidden from scrutiny—sets the agenda for the rest of the cluster: red-teaming needs an empirical, interdisciplinary research program before its current form hardens into unquestioned infrastructure.

[[Unknown2025-qj]] picks up this thread empirically, using interviews and participant observation at public red-teaming events to ask what it would mean for red-teaming to serve a genuine "public interest" rather than merely replicate cybersecurity's adversarial, military-derived logic. Its finding that institutional framing shapes what even counts as a harm worth surfacing directly substantiates Gillespie et al.'s worry about internally-defined, non-representative value judgments. Together the two papers trace an arc from diagnosis (red-teaming's hidden labor and value politics) to investigation (what public, event-based red-teaming actually looks like in practice), while both flag the same tension: broadening participation via public events risks becoming extractive, relying on marginalized communities' labor without building durable protections or scale.

## From participation-as-legitimacy to participation-as-rigor

[[Matias2025-px]] reframes the stakes of this participatory turn. Where red-teaming papers emphasize labor conditions and hidden values, Matias et al. argue that public and lived-experience involvement is not just an ethical or legitimacy concession but a *methodological* improvement to the science of AI evaluation itself. Their five-stage framework—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation—gives structure to the more diffuse calls in [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] for interdisciplinary, publicly engaged safety research: it specifies exactly where lived-experience expertise catches what purely technical evaluation misses (e.g., the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, hidden categories in police complaint data). This paper effectively answers the "why bother" question left implicit in the red-teaming papers: participatory methods aren't a distraction from scientific rigor but a route to it, provided the field borrows established participatory-science techniques to manage cost, scale, and validity concerns.

## Extending the frame: public squares and infrastructural power

[[Goldberg2026-eb]] transposes the same sociotechnical-participatory logic onto a different site—digital public squares reshaped by LLMs. Its treatment of collective dialogue systems, bridging systems, and community moderation echoes the labor concerns of [[Gillespie2026-aa]] (moderator burnout, inadequate tooling, declining platform support) and the participatory-science optimism of [[Matias2025-px]] (AI-augmented deliberation as a way to scale inclusive input without replacing human judgment). It also sounds a caution particular to this domain: synthetic AI participation in deliberation may be representationally convenient but democratically corrosive, echoing the broader cluster's worry that automation can quietly hollow out the human, situated labor that participatory processes depend on.

## A structural counterweight

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] closes the cluster with a pointed methodological warning that complicates the others' optimism. Its argument that "safety" framings—along with participatory and accountability gestures more broadly—can function as decoys that "co-construct" rather than challenge the political economy of AI development, is a direct challenge to any assumption that publicizing or democratizing red-teaming and evaluation is inherently emancipatory. Read against [[Gillespie2026-aa]]'s and [[Unknown2025-qj]]'s calls for public-interest red-teaming, or [[Matias2025-px]]'s case for participatory rigor, Vertesi et al. ask whether such reforms risk being absorbed into legitimating the very corporate actors they seek to hold accountable—especially when participatory design itself gets "enrolled" into the inevitability narrative. This does not refute the cluster's central sociotechnical claim so much as sharpen it: recognizing safety evaluation as sociotechnical is necessary but not sufficient; attention must also fall on who controls the infrastructure, capital, and institutional context in which participation occurs, lest labor-conscious, publicly engaged safety work become another site of value extraction dressed in the language of accountability.

## The arc

Taken together, the five papers trace a coherent progression: naming red-teaming's hidden sociotechnical dimensions ([[Gillespie2026-aa]]), empirically investigating how public-interest red-teaming actually unfolds ([[Unknown2025-qj]]), generalizing the participatory-rigor argument across AI evaluation broadly ([[Matias2025-px]]), extending the logic to a cognate domain of public discourse infrastructure ([[Goldberg2026-eb]]), and finally interrogating whether any of these participatory reforms can escape absorption into the larger political-economic "Project of AI" ([[Vertesi2026-lv]]). The through-line is a shared insistence that safety evaluation is inseparable from questions of labor, value judgment, and public accountability—paired with a live, unresolved debate about whether participatory science can genuinely redistribute power or merely legitimizes its concentration.
