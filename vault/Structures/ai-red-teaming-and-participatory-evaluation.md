# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The Sociotechnical Turn in AI Safety

The organizing claim of this cluster is deceptively simple: the practices that are supposed to make AI systems safe—red-teaming, evaluation, benchmarking—are themselves social institutions, not neutral technical procedures, and treating them otherwise produces both bad science and bad labor conditions. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Matias2025-px]] state this thesis most directly, from complementary angles, while [[Ng2026-og]], [[Hepp2026-oi]], and [[Goldberg2026-eb]] extend the argument outward—toward the multi-agent environments AI systems inhabit, the imaginative communities that shape what "safety" even means, and the public discourse infrastructures that AI now co-constitutes.

### Red-Teaming as Labor, Not Just Method

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] opens the strongest indictment: red-teaming has been rapidly institutionalized as *the* safety practice in generative AI, yet almost nothing is publicly known about who performs it, under what values, or at what cost. The paper's central move is historical analogy—red-teaming today recapitulates the trajectory of commercial content moderation a decade earlier, down to outsourcing to crowdworkers, internally and proprietarily defined harm categories, and the invisibilization of the people doing the work. Crucially, the paper insists this is not a transitional problem that automation will solve; treating red-teamers as temporary stopgaps before AI does the probing itself only deepens the neglect of labor conditions, psychological harm (secondary trauma, moral injury), and the narrow demographic basis from which "harm" gets defined. The upshot is a call for an empirical, interdisciplinary research agenda that studies red-teaming as a practice, a labor system, and a value system simultaneously—refusing to let it ossify into an opaque, market-driven default before it is understood.

### Participation as Epistemic Improvement, Not Just Legitimacy

[[Matias2025-px]] picks up the same sociotechnical premise but pivots from labor critique to epistemic argument: public and lived-experience involvement doesn't just make AI evaluation more legitimate, it makes it more *rigorous*. Where [[Gillespie2026-aa]] worries that internally defined harms reflect too narrow a set of values, Matias et al. show concretely how that narrowness produces scientific error—the Allegheny Family Screening Tool's AUC-based evaluation missed a bias that community-driven reanalysis caught; official single-category coding of police complaints hid patterns of sexual violation that community labeling surfaced. The paper's five-stage framework (equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation) offers something the red-teaming paper only gestures toward: a methodological vocabulary, borrowed from participatory and citizen science, for *where exactly* in the evaluation pipeline public involvement adds scientific value rather than merely procedural cover. Read together, these two papers triangulate the same gap from opposite ends: Gillespie et al. diagnose what's missing (empirical attention to the people and values inside red-teaming), and Matias et al. prescribe a method for filling analogous gaps across AI evaluation generally.

### Beyond the Dyad: Social Theory and Population-Level Governance

[[Ng2026-og]] pushes the sociotechnical framing into territory the first two papers don't reach: multi-agent AI systems where safety failures emerge not from individual model behavior but from network dynamics among interacting agents. Its explicit argument that alignment techniques like RLHF address a "dyadic user-operator problem" distinct from population-level phenomena—strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability—extends the volume's core insight (safety is irreducibly social) into a formal register, using empirical data from an AI social network to show these dynamics are observable, not merely speculative. This matters for evaluation and red-teaming practice: if unsafe behavior is an emergent property of agent populations and interaction topology, then red-teaming single models in isolation, however participatory, will systematically miss the hazards that matter most once agents are deployed at scale alongside each other.

### Who Imagines Safety, and Where

[[Hepp2026-oi]] steps back further still, asking not how safety evaluation should be practiced but where the values embedded in current practice come from. Its ethnography of the Bay Area's "quiet futuring"—the dense, locally anchored discourse of labs, pioneer communities, and especially Effective Altruist and Rationalist circles—supplies a genealogy for the very value judgments that [[Gillespie2026-aa]] identifies as opaquely embedded in red-teaming protocols and that [[Matias2025-px]] argues are too narrow absent public input. The paper's account of AGI, job loss, and geopolitics as the discursive glue of this community suggests that current safety practice is shaped less by universal values than by a specific, geographically and socially situated imaginary that presents itself as apolitical while setting agendas that loud futuring (CEOs, investors) later amplifies. This reframes the labor and participation concerns of the first two papers as symptoms of a deeper concentration: safety values are set by a narrow social figuration, not merely a narrow set of corporate employees.

### Safety Practices at the Scale of Public Discourse

Finally, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] extends the sociotechnical and participatory logic to the infrastructures where AI's downstream effects on democratic life are most visible: digital public squares. Its four technology families—collective dialogue systems, bridging systems, community-driven moderation, and proof-of-humanity systems—are themselves evaluation and governance problems requiring exactly the kind of public involvement and labor sensitivity the earlier papers demand. The paper's insistence that LLMs should "augment rather than wholesale substitute" human deliberation, and its concern that synthetic participation is "democratically problematic" even when representationally appealing, echoes Gillespie et al.'s warning against using automation to paper over labor and value questions, while its attention to moderator burnout and under-resourcing directly parallels the red-teaming labor crisis. Here the sociotechnical argument completes its arc: from the hidden labor inside a single evaluation practice, to the participatory methods that could fix it, to the multi-agent dynamics and imaginative communities that set its terms, to the large-scale public infrastructures where all of these forces converge and where the stakes of getting evaluation right are most evidently civic rather than merely technical.
