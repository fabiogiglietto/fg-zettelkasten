# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The core reframing: safety practices as social practices, not technical checks

The papers in this cluster converge on a single foundational move: they refuse to let "AI safety evaluation" remain a purely technical category. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes the case most explicitly for red-teaming, arguing that adversarial probing of AI systems is a sociotechnical practice bound up with value judgments, labor arrangements, and psychological costs — not a neutral engineering procedure. [[Matias2025-px]] makes the parallel case for evaluation more broadly, insisting that AI systems are sociotechnical artifacts whose reliability depends on social and organizational layers that technical metrics alone cannot capture. Both papers share a conviction that treating safety work as merely technical is not just ethically thin but *scientifically* impoverished — it produces worse evaluations, not just less legitimate ones. [[Unknown2025-qj]] extends this reframing empirically, using interviews and observation at public red-teaming events to show how the very definition of "harm" or "vulnerability" is shaped by institutional framing and participant composition, reinforcing the claim that red-teaming is a site of social negotiation rather than technical discovery.

## Who gets to define harm, and who bears the cost of finding it

A throughline connecting [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] is the question of who performs safety labor and under what institutional conditions. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] draws a sustained analogy to commercial content moderation: red-teaming is being outsourced, informalized, and rendered invisible in ways that echo the "ghost work" literature, while red-teamers absorb real psychological harm — secondary trauma, moral injury — from inhabiting adversarial personas without adequate institutional support. This labor-and-value critique sharpens a point implicit in [[Unknown2025-qj]]: when companies define harms internally, using homogeneous internal teams constrained by NDAs and incentive structures, the resulting harm taxonomy reflects corporate risk management rather than the full diversity of affected publics. [[Unknown2025-qj]]'s public red-teaming events are offered as a partial corrective — a way of pulling vulnerability discovery outside the firm — but [[Gillespie2026-aa]] cautions that volunteer/event-based models risk extractive reliance on marginalized participants without solving the underlying labor and well-being problems. Together these two papers frame red-teaming as contested terrain: whose values count as the baseline for "safe," and who is asked to do the often-traumatic work of finding where that baseline breaks.

## Participation as scientific method, not just democratic gesture

[[Matias2025-px]] pushes the argument furthest toward methodology, proposing that public involvement is not a concession to legitimacy but an epistemic resource that improves the actual rigor of AI evaluation. Its five-stage framework — equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation — gives structural specificity to what [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] gesture toward more diagnostically: concrete points in the evaluation pipeline where lived-experience expertise catches what professional evaluation misses (e.g., the Allegheny Family Screening Tool case, where community reanalysis surfaced bias invisible to the original AUC-based evaluation). This paper effectively supplies the participatory-science toolkit that the labor- and practice-focused papers imply is needed but do not fully elaborate, positioning citizen and stakeholder involvement as continuous with — rather than opposed to — scientific validity.

## Structural obstacles: industry control of the evidence base

[[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] supplies the political-economy backdrop against which these participatory ambitions must be read. If red-teaming and evaluation are to be opened to publics and to independent scrutiny, that scrutiny depends on data access, funding independence, and publication freedom — all of which this paper shows are currently compromised by industry control over research on its own products. Its catalog of suppression, selective funding, design bias, and "performative collaboration" (echoing the Meta/Social Science One case) explains why calls in [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Matias2025-px]] for external, coordinated research networks face structural headwinds: the same asymmetries that let companies define red-teaming's harm categories internally also let them shape what independent researchers can even study. Read together, these three papers suggest that sociotechnical safety evaluation cannot be achieved by better methodology alone; it also requires institutional reform — mandated data access, disclosure norms, funding safeguards — to make participatory and public-interest evaluation practically possible rather than merely desirable.

## Peripheral threads: agency and transparency as adjacent sociotechnical questions

Two papers sit at the edges of this cluster's core argument but extend its logic into adjacent territory. [[Anicker2024-vp]] approaches sociotechnicality from social theory, arguing that AI "agency" itself is a socially granted and monitored status rather than an inherent technical property — a claim that resonates with the broader cluster's insistence that safety-relevant categories (harm, vulnerability, agency) are constituted through social practice, not discovered by inspection. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] operationalizes a version of the participatory-evaluation ethos on a narrower question — chatbot persuasion — showing empirically that transparency about *what a system is trying to do* (intent) matters more than transparency about *what it is* (identity). This finding quietly reinforces the volume's central claim: meaningful safety and accountability mechanisms must engage the social and pragmatic dimensions of AI systems' use, not just their formal technical properties, echoing [[Matias2025-px]]'s insistence that technical transparency without social context is evaluative theater.
