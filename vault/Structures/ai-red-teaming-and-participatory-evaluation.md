# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## Red-teaming as a sociotechnical practice, not a technical checklist

The anchor claim running through this topic is that evaluating AI systems for safety is fundamentally a social, institutional, and labor practice, not a self-contained technical procedure. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] states this most directly, arguing that AI red-teaming has been normalized as a central safety ritual while remaining almost entirely opaque in its methods, personnel, and value commitments. By tracing red-teaming's genealogy to commercial content moderation, the paper shows how the same pathologies—internally defined harm taxonomies set by non-representative designers, outsourcing to precarious crowdworkers, and the psychological toll of adversarial, transgressive labor—are being reproduced in generative AI safety work almost without public notice. Crucially, the paper insists that automation narratives about red-teaming are a distraction: the people currently doing this work face real risks (secondary trauma, moral injury) that a purely technical framing renders invisible.

[[Unknown2025-qj]] extends this claim empirically, using interviews and participant observation at public red-teaming events to ask what red-teaming looks like when it is explicitly oriented toward the "public interest" rather than internal corporate risk management. It finds that red-teaming's cybersecurity and military origins do not translate cleanly into a practice meant to surface harms to diverse publics: institutional framing determines what even counts as a vulnerability worth flagging. Read alongside [[Gillespie2026-aa]], this paper supplies the fieldwork that substantiates the earlier essay's call for an empirical research agenda—showing concretely how organizational context and participant composition shape red-teaming's outputs, and suggesting that "democratizing" red-teaming through public events is possible but uneven and non-trivial to scale.

## Participatory science as a route to scientific rigor, not just legitimacy

[[Matias2025-px]] reframes the stakes: public involvement in AI evaluation is not merely a legitimacy exercise or political concession but a way to produce *better science*. Where [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] focus on the labor and institutional politics of one particular safety practice (red-teaming), Matias generalizes the argument across the evaluation pipeline, identifying five distinct moments—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation—where lived-experience expertise materially improves validity and reliability. The Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis and the Chicago police complaint relabeling examples function as existence proofs: professional, purely technical evaluations missed harms (biased rankings, hidden allegations of sexual violation) that community-involved reanalysis surfaced. This paper supplies the affirmative, methodological complement to the labor-and-harms critique offered by the red-teaming papers: if red-teaming's current opacity and outsourcing are a problem, participatory science methods are one concrete answer for how public involvement can be structured to strengthen rather than merely legitimate evaluation.

Together these three papers stake out the topic's core position: AI safety evaluation is sociotechnical through and through, its rigor depends on who is included and how their labor and expertise are organized, and both the harms of neglecting this (precarious, traumatized red-teamers; missed algorithmic biases) and the benefits of addressing it (richer measurement, more valid inference) are empirically demonstrable rather than speculative.

## Extending the sociotechnical lens: populations, benchmarks, and automation pressures

The remaining three papers do not share this topic's participatory-labor framing directly, but each stress-tests or complicates it in instructive ways. [[Ng2026-og]] pushes the sociotechnical argument to a different register: rather than asking who evaluates AI, it asks what evaluation and design must assume once AI agents themselves form social systems. Its case that agentic AI populations require social-theoretic structural priors (heterogeneity, network dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) rather than single-task assumptions is a structural analogue to the human-centered claims above—both insist that treating AI systems (or their evaluation) as isolated technical artifacts misses emergent, relational dynamics that only a social-science lens can capture. Where Matias argues participatory methods are needed to evaluate AI's effects *on* people, Ng argues something like social theory is needed to understand AI's effects *among* AI agents—an important boundary case for how far "sociotechnical" evaluation must extend.

[[Hackenburg2026-ud]] is instructive from the opposite direction: it is itself a large-scale, carefully designed human-comparison study—professional canvassers, elite debaters, laypeople—used as an evaluation method for a specific capability (persuasion) with direct safety implications. It illustrates, empirically and at scale, exactly the kind of human benchmark work that participatory and public-interest framings depend on, while also sharpening the stakes: if frontier systems reliably out-persuade even coached experts, the question of who is qualified to serve as an evaluative comparator—and under what labor conditions professional persuaders/debaters are recruited into that role—echoes the labor concerns raised for red-teamers, even though the paper itself does not frame it that way.

[[Jayaram2026-wd]], finally, functions as something of a foil to the topic's central argument. PAT is presented as a scalability solution to an overwhelmed peer-review system, moving toward automation of a traditionally human, judgment-laden evaluative practice. Its four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review (author tool through total automation) raises, in a different domain, precisely the question [[Gillespie2026-aa]] warns against dismissing too quickly: automating an evaluative labor practice does not eliminate the underlying sociotechnical questions of whose values and judgments the automated system encodes, what human expertise gets displaced, and what new labor (e.g., verifying PAT's own hallucinations) gets created in its place. Read against the topic's core papers, Jayaram's optimism about orchestrated, inference-scaled review agents serves as a useful counterpoint, illustrating the techno-solutionist trajectory that Gillespie, Matias, and the public red-teaming study are collectively arguing against—one where efficiency and automation quietly displace the messier, but more accountable, work of keeping evaluation genuinely public, participatory, and answerable to the people it affects.
