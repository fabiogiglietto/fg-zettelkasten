# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The core reframing: safety as sociotechnical, not technical

The organizing move across this cluster is a refusal to let "AI safety evaluation" remain a purely technical category. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] states the claim most directly for red-teaming: adversarial probing of generative models is not a neutral engineering procedure but a practice freighted with value judgments, labor arrangements, and psychological costs that mirror the earlier, poorly-examined history of commercial content moderation. [[Matias2025-px]] generalizes the same insight to evaluation writ large, arguing that AI systems are sociotechnical artifacts whose reliability depends on social and organizational layers that technical metrics routinely miss. Where Gillespie's essay diagnoses the problem—opaque, market-driven practices becoming entrenched before anyone studies them—Matias proposes a remedy: building public and lived-experience participation directly into the scientific apparatus of evaluation. Read together, they frame red-teaming and evaluation as two faces of the same unresolved question: who gets to define harm, and whose knowledge counts as evidence of it.

## Whose labor, whose values: the hidden workforce of safety

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] does the most to make visible the people who actually perform safety work. By tracing red-teaming's institutional ancestry to outsourced content moderation, the paper shows how harm categories, iterative internal value-setting, and labor arbitrage are being reproduced almost unexamined—this time inside AI companies that set their own definitions of harm with little outside accountability. The essay's most distinctive contribution is its attention to psychological toll: red-teamers are asked to inhabit adversarial, transgressive personas, and this imaginative labor carries real risk of secondary trauma and moral injury, risks obscured by rhetoric about the eventual automation of the task. This labor-centered lens complicates the more aspirational participatory vision in [[Matias2025-px]]: broadening who contributes to evaluation—whether through volunteer red-teaming events or community-based data labeling—is not simply a matter of epistemic inclusion but also one of working conditions, consent, and exposure to harm. Matias's own discussion of contributory citizen-science models acknowledges this tension, noting that such models raise questions of consent and treatment even as they produce more robust data. The two papers thus triangulate a shared concern: expanding the circle of people involved in safety evaluation is necessary, but doing so without attending to labor protections simply redistributes the costs already visible in Gillespie's account of red-teaming.

## Participation as better science, not just legitimacy

[[Matias2025-px]] pushes the argument a step further by insisting that public involvement is not a political concession to be traded against scientific rigor but a condition for achieving it. The paper's five-stage breakdown of evaluation—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation—gives analytic structure to a claim Gillespie's essay only gestures toward: that outsiders bring situated knowledge capable of catching failures invisible to designers working from inside a single organizational vantage point. The case studies are pointed rather than illustrative: community reanalysis of the Allegheny Family Screening Tool surfaced a bias in bipartite ranking that the original AUC-based evaluation missed, and community labeling of police complaint records uncovered sexual-violation allegations hidden by official coding schemes. These examples do real argumentative work against the common objection—echoed in Gillespie's critique of internal red-teaming—that affected communities lack the expertise to meaningfully participate in technical assessment. Matias's answer is that the opposite is often true: professional evaluators lack the lived expertise needed to see what communities can see.

## Public squares as both site and instrument of participatory evaluation

[[Goldberg2026-eb]] extends this logic outward from individual model evaluation to the infrastructure of public discourse itself. Its four families of AI-enabled technologies—collective dialogue systems, bridging systems, community-driven moderation, and proof-of-humanity systems—can be read as concrete instantiations of the participatory methods Matias calls for, applied at platform scale. Collective dialogue systems in particular occupy exactly the methodological space Matias stakes out: tools positioned between surveys and focus groups that promise scalable, inclusive feedback but, as the paper candidly notes, still depend heavily on human labor for facilitation, moderation, and sensemaking—labor whose conditions echo Gillespie's concerns about burnout and inadequate institutional support, here surfacing among volunteer community moderators rather than contracted red-teamers. Goldberg's paper also supplies the democratic-theory vocabulary—Habermas's public sphere, Rawls's public reason, Landemore's open democracy—that gives normative weight to Matias's more methodological argument: participatory evaluation is not only better science but a precondition for democratically legitimate AI governance. Where Gillespie and Matias focus on evaluating models, Goldberg asks what it means to design entire discursive environments so that evaluation and participation are structurally built in, rather than bolted on after deployment.

## Formalizing the social: a theoretical complement

[[Ng2026-og]] sits at a different register—formal and quantitative rather than ethnographic or policy-oriented—but it reinforces the same underlying thesis from the direction of system design. Its central argument, that agentic AI behavior emerges from multi-agent interaction structures rather than individual capability and therefore requires social theory as a "structural prior," is the modeling analogue of the sociotechnical claims made elsewhere in this cluster. Just as Matias argues that evaluation metrics focused on isolated model performance miss the organizational and social layers that determine real-world reliability, Ng argues that alignment techniques such as RLHF or DPO address only dyadic user-operator problems and cannot substitute for population-level analysis of how agents co-evolve within interaction networks. The empirical grounding in the MoltBook dataset—showing topology-dependent variance, bidirectional agent-network coupling, and endogenous distributional drift among tens of thousands of interacting agents—gives formal teeth to an intuition shared across the other three papers: that safety-relevant behavior is an emergent property of social systems, not a fixed attribute inspectable in a single model or a single evaluation session. In that sense, Ng's framework can be read as asking what a MASS-aware successor to current red-teaming and evaluation practice would need to formally represent—network position, strategic heterogeneity, distributional drift—if the sociotechnical commitments argued for qualitatively by Gillespie, Matias, and Goldberg were to be built into the mathematics of agentic system design itself.

## The arc

Taken together, the four papers trace a progression from diagnosis to method to infrastructure to formal theory. Gillespie identifies the problem: safety practice has been treated as a technical black box, obscuring the labor and values embedded within it. Matias proposes the remedy at the level of method: participatory science can make evaluation itself more rigorous, not merely more legitimate. Goldberg scales that remedy to the level of public infrastructure, imagining digital public squares co-designed with the same participatory commitments. And Ng supplies a formal vocabulary—still nascent, still largely untested against these more humanistic concerns—for representing the social structures that all three other papers insist cannot be abstracted away. The unresolved tension running through the set is between inclusion and extraction: every mechanism for widening participation, from red-teaming to community moderation to citizen evaluation, risks reproducing the very labor precarity and value asymmetries it is meant to correct unless governance and protection are designed in from the start.
