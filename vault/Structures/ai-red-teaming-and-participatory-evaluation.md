# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## Reframing Safety Work as Sociotechnical Practice

The founding move shared across this cluster is a refusal to treat AI safety evaluation—red-teaming, auditing, transparency disclosure—as a purely technical exercise. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes this argument most directly, insisting that red-teaming is inseparable from the value judgments, labor arrangements, and psychological costs that structure it. The essay draws an explicit historical analogy to commercial content moderation: just as platforms once outsourced the definition and adjudication of harm to opaque, market-driven labor systems, generative AI companies are now doing the same with red-teaming, letting internal teams define harm categories without input from the diverse publics affected by them. [[Unknown2025-qj]] extends this framing empirically, showing through interviews and observation at public red-teaming events that what counts as a "vulnerability" or "harm" is not a neutral technical fact but is shaped by institutional context, participant expertise, and the framing of "public interest" itself. Together these two papers establish the volume's core thesis: the *content* of safety evaluation—what gets probed, by whom, and against what conception of harm—is a site of contestation, not a solved engineering problem.

## Labor, Harm, and the Limits of Internal Expertise

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] pushes this further by centering the people who perform red-teaming labor. It documents how red-teaming is increasingly outsourced to crowdworkers and third-party vendors, reproducing the precarity, weak protections, and psychological toll (secondary trauma, moral injury) long documented in content moderation work. Crucially, the paper argues that internal red-teamers—even well-resourced ones—typically lack the sociocultural and linguistic range needed to anticipate harms experienced by marginalized or non-dominant communities, a structural limitation that no amount of technical sophistication can fix. This diagnosis dovetails with [[Unknown2025-qj]]'s finding that public-facing red-teaming events surface considerations that internal, expert-only exercises miss entirely, suggesting that participation is not just an ethical nicety but an epistemic necessity: without it, red-teaming systematically underrepresents the harms that matter most to those outside the design team.

## Publics as Co-Producers of Scientific Validity

[[Matias2025-px]] generalizes this insight from red-teaming to AI evaluation writ large, arguing that public involvement improves not merely the legitimacy of AI science but its rigor. Where [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] focus on adversarial probing, Matias maps five stages of quantitative evaluation—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation—at which lived-experience expertise corrects errors that purely technical evaluation misses. The case studies (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool's hidden bias, Chicago police complaint recoding, participatory Reddit experiments) function as concrete instances of the abstract failure mode that Gillespie and the red-teaming study describe more generally: internally defined metrics and harm categories quietly encode assumptions that only affected communities can detect. Read together, these three papers converge on a shared prescription—treat the public not as a downstream audience for AI safety claims but as an upstream contributor to defining and testing them—while differing in scope: Matias offers a methodological toolkit drawn from participatory science broadly, while Gillespie and the red-teaming study focus on the specific labor and institutional politics of adversarial testing.

## Transparency as a Sociotechnical Design Problem

[[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] extends the volume's sociotechnical lens to a different safety mechanism—disclosure—and arrives at a structurally similar conclusion: the *content* of what is revealed matters far more than the fact of revelation itself. Its experimental finding that labeling a chatbot as AI does almost nothing to blunt persuasion, while disclosing its persuasive *intent* roughly halves it, echoes the broader theme that safety interventions designed around superficial technical facts ("this is AI-generated") miss the deeper sociotechnical reality of what the system is trying to do and to whom. This resonates with Gillespie's and the red-teaming study's concern that harm categories set by designers—here, regulators modeling transparency after the EU AI Act's identity-disclosure requirement—can be poorly matched to how harm is actually produced and experienced by users. Where the other three papers argue for bringing publics into the *evaluative* process, Rauchfleisch's work suggests a complementary lesson for *disclosure* regimes: meaningful transparency, like meaningful red-teaming, requires attending to the sociotechnical mechanics of influence and harm rather than settling for procedural markers that look rigorous but do little protective work.

## An Emerging Research Agenda

Taken as a set, these papers chart an arc from diagnosis to method to empirical test. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] diagnose red-teaming's opacity and call for coordinated, interdisciplinary study before its current market-driven form hardens into unquestioned practice. [[Matias2025-px]] supplies a positive methodological program—participatory science techniques adapted to AI evaluation—that could operationalize that call. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] demonstrates, with experimental precision, why getting the sociotechnical details right (intent versus identity, harm versus label) has measurable consequences for whether a safety intervention actually protects anyone. The throughline is a shared skepticism toward safety practices that are technically legible but socially thin, and a shared insistence that rigor and public accountability in AI safety are not competing goals but mutually constitutive ones.
