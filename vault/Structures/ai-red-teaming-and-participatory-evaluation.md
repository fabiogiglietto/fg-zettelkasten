# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The Sociotechnical Turn in AI Safety Practice

Both papers converge on a single, consequential move: refusing to let "AI safety" remain a narrowly technical category. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Matias2025-px]] each insist that the practices meant to certify AI systems as safe—red-teaming and evaluation, respectively—are themselves social institutions, staffed by people, embedded in organizations, and shaped by whose knowledge counts. Where they differ is in which half of this sociotechnical bargain they foreground: [[Gillespie2026-aa]] is preoccupied with the *labor* that safety work extracts and conceals, while [[Matias2025-px]] is preoccupied with the *epistemics* that public participation can repair. Read together, they sketch a fuller picture of AI safety as a field that currently under-theorizes both the workers who perform it and the publics who could improve it.

## Red-Teaming as Ghost Work

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] builds its argument by analogy to content moderation: a practice that began as an improvisational, internally defined harm-mitigation activity and calcified, without public deliberation, into outsourced, traumatizing, poorly governed labor. The essay's central worry is that red-teaming is retracing this path in real time—harm categories set unilaterally by companies, work pushed onto vendors and crowdworkers, and psychological costs (secondary trauma, moral injury) treated as incidental rather than as a predictable consequence of asking people to inhabit adversarial, transgressive personas. Crucially, the paper frames red-teaming's *value judgments*—whose definition of harm counts, whose sociocultural fluency is represented—as inseparable from its *labor arrangements*: the people doing the probing are rarely the people most exposed to the harms being probed for, and the institutional opacity around both NDAs and vendor contracts makes this invisible to outside scrutiny. The call to action is for an empirical, interdisciplinary research program that studies red-teaming before its "market-driven form" hardens into unexamined infrastructure, much as content moderation's exploitative patterns became normalized before critique caught up.

## Evaluation as Participatory Science

[[Matias2025-px]] takes the complementary route: rather than asking how safety work exploits labor, it asks how safety work could be made *scientifically better* by including the people with lived experience of the systems under evaluation. Its argument is explicitly not that participation is merely a legitimacy exercise—it claims participation improves rigor at identifiable stages of evaluation (equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation), each illustrated with cases where community involvement caught flaws that professional evaluators missed (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, Chicago police complaint recoding, Reddit field experiments on recommender behavior). This is a more constructive, methods-forward companion to [[Gillespie2026-aa]]'s diagnostic critique: if red-teaming's problem is that internal, proprietary value-setting produces blind spots, [[Matias2025-px]] offers a positive program—participatory science methods borrowed from citizen science and medical ethics—for correcting exactly that kind of blind spot.

## A Shared Diagnosis, Divergent Remedies

Despite their different emphases, the two papers share a structural diagnosis: AI safety evaluation currently concentrates authority—over what counts as harm, what counts as valid measurement—in the hands of developers and designers who do not reflect the diversity of affected populations, and who have incentives (commercial, reputational) to keep that authority internal. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] treats this as a labor-exploitation and occupational-health problem requiring external research oversight and better worker protections; [[Matias2025-px]] treats it as a methodological deficiency requiring the formal incorporation of lived-experience expertise into the scientific apparatus of evaluation itself. Neither position is naive about costs: [[Gillespie2026-aa]] acknowledges that volunteer, community-based red-teaming (e.g., DEFCON-style events) risks extractive reliance on marginalized participants, while [[Matias2025-px]] directly engages skeptics' concerns about the generality, subjectivity, and scalability of participatory methods, arguing these are addressable rather than disqualifying.

Taken together, these two papers suggest a research agenda for the field: AI safety cannot be adequately evaluated, audited, or red-teamed without confronting *who* performs the work, under what conditions, and *whose* knowledge is allowed to shape what "safety" even means. They imply that the current trajectory—opaque, internally governed, labor-exploitative—is neither scientifically optimal nor ethically sustainable, and that correcting course requires treating public involvement and worker welfare not as peripheral governance concerns but as constitutive of what good safety science looks like.
