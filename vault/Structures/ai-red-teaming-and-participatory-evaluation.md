# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The sociotechnical turn in AI safety evaluation

A central claim unites this cluster: red-teaming, evaluation, and the broader apparatus of "safety testing" for AI are not neutral technical procedures but social practices—constituted by labor arrangements, value judgments, legal exposure, and institutional politics. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] states this thesis most directly, arguing that red-teaming has been normalized as a governance centerpiece while remaining empirically opaque: nobody outside the companies conducting it really knows who does it, under what conditions, or according to whose values. By tracing red-teaming's genealogy through the history of commercial content moderation, the paper reframes it as "ghost work"—outsourced, precarious, and psychologically costly—rather than a clean adversarial benchmark. [[Matias2025-px]] arrives at a structurally similar conclusion from a different angle: AI systems are sociotechnical artifacts, so evaluations that ignore the social and organizational layers around them are not just ethically deficient but *scientifically* incomplete. Both papers thus reject a widespread assumption in ML culture—that rigor and participation are in tension—and instead argue that public involvement and attention to labor are preconditions for valid, trustworthy evaluation.

### From critique to method: participatory evaluation as better science

[[Matias2025-px]] operationalizes this claim by identifying five evaluation stages—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation—where lived-experience expertise measurably improves scientific quality, not merely legitimacy. The case studies it marshals (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, Chicago police complaint relabeling, Reddit fact-checking experiments) function as existence proofs that affected communities catch failures that credentialed evaluators, working from the inside, structurally cannot see. This is a direct rebuttal to the implicit model in much of industry evaluation practice, where [[Gillespie2026-aa]] shows internal red-teamers—constrained by NDAs, incentives, and limited diversity of lived experience—define harm categories largely on their own terms. Read together, the two papers suggest a shift from red-teaming as an internal, proprietary stress-test to evaluation as a civic-epistemic process requiring the same methodological apparatus (consent, compensation, validity checks) long developed in participatory and citizen science.

### The labor and legal substrate of evaluation work

Where [[Matias2025-px]] emphasizes what public participation adds epistemically, [[Gillespie2026-aa]] foregrounds what it costs the people doing it: red-teamers suffer secondary trauma and moral injury from sustained adversarial and transgressive imagination, with support structures (EAPs, opt-outs) inconsistently available and undermined by surveillance and NDA culture. [[Park2026-tr]] extends this labor-and-risk lens into an adjacent but structurally parallel domain: the legal jeopardy faced by security and social-computing researchers whose public-interest probing of systems—functionally a form of red-teaming—exposes them to prosecution under overbroad computer-crime and IP statutes. Its findings on chilling effects, vulnerability stockpiling, and the precarity of relying on pro bono counsel make concrete what [[Gillespie2026-aa]] gestures at abstractly: that the people doing adversarial evaluation of sociotechnical systems bear real psychological, financial, and legal costs that current institutional arrangements neither recognize nor adequately mitigate. Together they argue that any serious research agenda on evaluation practice must treat labor protection and legal reform as first-order safety concerns, not afterthoughts.

### Evaluation failure modes at the level of discourse and persuasion

Several papers test what happens when evaluation and transparency mechanisms meet real human audiences rather than abstract benchmarks. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] demonstrates that frontier AI systems now out-persuade even elite trained human persuaders, with the advantage traceable to information throughput rather than charisma—a finding with direct bearing on what red-teaming and evaluation need to probe (manipulation capability, not just toxic content). [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] follows naturally, showing that the obvious policy response—AI-identity disclosure, as in the EU AI Act—does little to blunt this persuasive power, whereas disclosing *intent* roughly halves it. This is itself a sociotechnical finding: it shows transparency is not a fixed technical feature to be checked off but a design choice whose social meaning (what it reveals, to whom, against what expectation) determines its protective value. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] situates these micro-level findings within a macro-institutional vision, arguing that LLM-enabled collective dialogue, bridging, and moderation systems could reshape digital public squares for better or worse—but only if AI augments rather than replaces human deliberation, and only if community moderators (themselves an underprotected labor category, echoing [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Park2026-tr]]) are resourced rather than displaced.

### Automation as a looming counter-pressure

[[Jayaram2026-wd]] stands slightly apart in subject matter—automated scientific peer review rather than red-teaming or chatbot persuasion—but it crystallizes a tension running under the whole topic: the temptation to resolve scaling problems (too many papers, too much content to moderate, too much red-teaming needed) by substituting agentic AI evaluators for human ones. Its reported gains in detection accuracy are real, but the paper's own four-level autonomy taxonomy, and its hallucination caveats, underline exactly the point [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes against premature automation claims in red-teaming: human judgment, labor, and oversight do not disappear when evaluation is automated, they are redistributed and often obscured. Read against [[Matias2025-px]]'s and [[Goldberg2026-eb]]'s arguments for augmentation rather than replacement, PAT exemplifies both the promise and the risk of this topic's central tension—whether AI safety evaluation evolves as an accountable, publicly engaged sociotechnical practice, or as an increasingly opaque, automated, and labor-erasing one.
