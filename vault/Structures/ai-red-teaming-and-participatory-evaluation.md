# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## From Technical Procedure to Sociotechnical Practice

The animating move across this cluster is a reframing: safety evaluation practices that are usually presented as technical—red-teaming, benchmarking, auditing—are recast as social institutions with their own labor arrangements, value commitments, and political economies. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes this argument most directly, tracing red-teaming's rapid normalization as a governance and policy touchstone despite the public's near-total ignorance of who performs it, under what conditions, and according to whose values. The essay's central historical move is to read red-teaming through the lens of commercial content moderation: both practices emerged as ad hoc, internally defined, iteratively expanded categories of harm; both were rapidly outsourced to precarious crowdworkers; and both impose a distinctive psychological burden—secondary trauma, moral injury—on workers asked to inhabit adversarial or harm-seeking postures. [[Unknown2025-qj]] extends this sociotechnical framing empirically, using interviews and observation at public red-teaming events to show that red-teaming's meaning is not fixed by its cybersecurity and military origins but is actively negotiated by institutional context, participant expertise, and competing notions of "public interest." Read together, these two papers establish that red-teaming is less a stable method than a contested site where harms get defined, and where that definitional power is currently concentrated among AI developers rather than the publics affected by the systems.

## Participation as Epistemic Improvement, Not Just Legitimation

A second thread pushes back against the idea that public involvement in AI evaluation is merely a democratic nicety layered atop "real" science. [[Matias2025-px]] argues the opposite: participatory methods can materially improve the *rigor* of evaluation itself, not just its perceived legitimacy. By decomposing quantitative AI evaluation into five stages—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation—the paper shows concrete mechanisms by which lived-experience expertise catches errors that purely technical evaluation misses, citing cases like the HRDAG/ACLU reanalysis of the Allegheny Family Screening Tool and community relabeling of Chicago police complaint data. This is a direct epistemic complement to [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]]: where those papers diagnose the narrowness of who currently defines harm in red-teaming (internal teams lacking sociocultural and linguistic breadth), [[Matias2025-px]] supplies the positive case for why broadening participation is not a compromise on scientific validity but a condition for it. Together they suggest a shared claim: AI systems are sociotechnical artifacts, so their evaluation—whether adversarial probing or statistical measurement—cannot be adequately performed by technical experts working in isolation from the populations the systems affect.

## Labor, Precarity, and the Limits of Volunteerism

Underneath the participatory optimism runs a harder-edged concern about who actually does this work and under what conditions. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] is explicit that red-teaming labor is increasingly organized through outsourcing and crowdwork, replicating the labor arbitrage and weak protections documented in content moderation, and that even well-intentioned volunteer or public red-teaming events (echoed in [[Unknown2025-qj]]'s account of public red-teaming convenings) risk extractive reliance on marginalized communities whose insight is solicited but whose labor and exposure to harm go under-supported. This tempers the participatory science argument of [[Matias2025-px]]: broadening who evaluates AI is scientifically valuable, but the "who" cannot be treated as a costless resource. The two papers implicitly call for the same missing infrastructure—coordinated, empirically grounded research and institutional support—but from different angles, one centered on worker well-being and governance, the other on evaluation methodology and public epistemics.

## Structural Threats to an Independent Evidence Base

[[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] supplies the political-economic context that makes public and participatory approaches urgent rather than optional. It documents how the tech industry shapes the very possibility of independent evaluation—suppressing internal findings, restricting data access, funding research asymmetrically, and engaging in "performative" collaborations that constrain design and publishability—drawing explicit parallels to tobacco and fossil-fuel science. This paper functions as a structural diagnosis underlying the concerns raised elsewhere in the cluster: if companies control access to red-teaming results, evaluation data, and the definition of harm categories (as [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] describe for red-teaming, and as [[Matias2025-px]] implies for evaluation more broadly), then calls for public involvement are also, necessarily, calls to break industry's near-monopoly on the evidence base about its own systems. Without the institutional reforms [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] proposes—mandated data access, stronger disclosure norms, journal-level safeguards—participatory evaluation risks remaining dependent on the goodwill and selective transparency of the companies being evaluated.

## Toward Public Infrastructures for Deliberation and Oversight

[[Goldberg2026-eb]] extends the arc from evaluation of AI systems to AI's role in *constituting* the public sphere itself, arguing that LLMs could reshape digital public squares toward more participatory and deliberative forms while also threatening to entrench division, dilute human agency, or produce illegitimate synthetic participation. Though focused on discourse infrastructure rather than red-teaming or evaluation per se, it shares this cluster's core commitment: that safety- and legitimacy-relevant properties of AI systems—here, whether AI-mediated deliberation is inclusive, trustworthy, and genuinely participatory—cannot be assessed or engineered by technologists alone, and require sociotechnical design attentive to power, access, and institutional capacity (echoing the burnout and under-resourcing of community moderators, a labor concern paralleling [[Gillespie2026-aa]]'s account of red-teaming labor). Taken as a whole, the five papers trace a coherent arc: from diagnosing that safety practices like red-teaming are sociotechnical rather than technical ([[Gillespie2026-aa]], [[Unknown2025-qj]]), through demonstrating that public participation strengthens rather than dilutes the science of evaluation ([[Matias2025-px]]), to confronting the structural industry power that constrains independent evaluation in the first place ([[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]]), and finally toward imagining what genuinely participatory AI-mediated public institutions might look like ([[Goldberg2026-eb]]).
