# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## Red-Teaming as Labor, Not Just Technique

The anchor claim running through this cluster is that red-teaming and evaluation are not neutral technical procedures but social arrangements with histories, workers, and value judgments baked in. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes this case most explicitly by tracing red-teaming's institutional trajectory against the earlier history of commercial content moderation: the same pattern of internally defined harm categories, outsourced and precarious labor, and neglected worker well-being is reappearing, largely unexamined, in AI safety work. The paper's key intervention is to insist that *who* red-teams, under what labor conditions, and according to whose values, is inseparable from what red-teaming can discover—internal teams lacking sociocultural and linguistic diversity will simply miss classes of harm that affected communities would catch. [[Unknown2025-qj]] extends this empirically, using interviews and observation at public red-teaming events to show that red-teaming's meaning shifts depending on institutional context: a practice born in cybersecurity and military threat-modeling is being repurposed as a public-interest accountability mechanism, but only unevenly, and its adaptation to generative-AI harms (as opposed to system intrusion) remains incomplete. Together these two papers frame red-teaming as an unsettled, still-forming institution—one whose current market-driven, opaque shape is contingent rather than natural, and therefore still open to redesign around labor protections and public participation before it hardens.

## Participatory Science as an Argument About Rigor, Not Just Legitimacy

[[Matias2025-px]] supplies the methodological counterpart to this critique: rather than treating public involvement as a concession to democratic optics, it argues that lived-experience participation improves the *scientific* quality of AI evaluation itself. By decomposing evaluation into equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation, the paper shows concrete points where technical evaluators systematically lack the situated knowledge to notice what they are missing—echoed in real cases like the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis and community relabeling of police complaint data, both of which surfaced harms invisible to the original quantitative frameworks. This directly operationalizes what [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] gesture toward: if red-teaming and evaluation depend on whose harms get counted, participatory science methods are not an add-on to safety evaluation but a corrective for a specific kind of measurement blindness. Read together, these three papers argue for treating AI safety evaluation as continuous with participatory and citizen science traditions—with attention to consent, compensation, and epistemic contribution—rather than as an engineering checklist administered by credentialed insiders.

## Testing the Stakes: Public Experiments as Evaluation Practice

Two papers in this set are less about red-teaming institutions per se and more about what large-scale, public-facing evaluation actually looks like when it is done rigorously and involves real people at scale. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] demonstrates, via nearly 19,000 conversations, that frontier models can out-persuade even elite, incentivized human experts, and that no individual human matches AI performance—a finding that only becomes visible through large public experiments rather than internal red-teaming exercises confined to a handful of testers. This is itself a sociotechnical evaluation method: the paper's evidentiary weight comes precisely from its scale and its use of real behavioral stakes (charitable donations), illustrating the kind of "public involvement improves the science" logic that [[Matias2025-px]] argues for in the abstract. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] takes the next step, using a comparably public, preregistered experiment to test whether transparency interventions (identity labels vs. intent disclosure) actually mitigate the persuasive harms that papers like Hackenburg's identify. Its finding—that disclosing *what an AI is trying to do* works where disclosing *what it is* does not—is a direct empirical contribution to the value judgments that [[Gillespie2026-aa]] warns are usually made unilaterally by developers rather than tested against public response. Both papers model an evaluation paradigm in which the public is not merely a source of harms to be discovered by red-teamers, but a participant subject whose actual responses to interventions must be measured before governance mechanisms (like the EU AI Act's disclosure requirements) can be trusted.

## The Automation Counter-Current

[[Jayaram2026-wd]] sits in productive tension with the rest of the cluster. Where Gillespie et al. explicitly warn that framing red-teaming as amenable to automation "distracts from urgent labor... concerns about the people currently doing this work," Jayaram's Paper Assistant Tool is precisely an attempt to automate a structurally similar form of expert evaluation—scientific peer review—via agentic, inference-scaled pipelines, motivated by the claim that human review "cannot scale." Its four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review (from author tool to full automation) is worth reading against the sociotechnical framing elsewhere in this set: it treats evaluation capacity as fundamentally a throughput problem to be solved by better orchestration of models, rather than as a practice entangled with labor conditions, value judgments, and situated expertise. The pilot deployments at STOC and ICML, with authors reporting genuine substantive catches alongside persistent hallucination and grounding failures, suggest the tension is not fully resolved: automation can extend evaluative capacity, but the residual errors and disagreements are exactly the sort of judgment calls that [[Matias2025-px]] and [[Gillespie2026-aa]] argue require situated human (and public) involvement rather than pure automation.

## Synthesis

Across the six papers, a shared arc emerges: red-teaming and evaluation are being institutionalized rapidly and largely invisibly, in ways that reproduce known pathologies of platform labor ([[Gillespie2026-aa]]); public and participatory framings offer both a normative corrective and, per [[Matias2025-px]], a genuine improvement to evaluative rigor; empirical field sites ([[Unknown2025-qj]]) show this reframing is already underway but unevenly institutionalized; and large public experiments ([[Hackenburg2026-ud]], [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]]) demonstrate both the high stakes of capabilities that internal evaluation might miss and the value of testing governance interventions against real public behavior rather than assumption. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] stands as the live counter-model—an automation-first approach to scaling evaluation that this literature would push to ask: automated for whom, verified against whose values, and with what account of the human judgment it displaces or obscures?
