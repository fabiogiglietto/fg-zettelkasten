# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The sociotechnical turn in AI safety practice

Both papers filed here converge on a single contention: that the two pillars of contemporary AI safety work—red-teaming and evaluation—have been mischaracterized as technical procedures when they are, in fact, sociotechnical systems embedded in labor relations, institutional politics, and contested value judgments. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Matias2025-px]] approach this claim from different angles—one genealogical and labor-focused, the other methodological and participatory—but together they stake out a shared argument: that the current trajectory of AI safety governance, left to internal corporate control, will reproduce the opacity, extraction, and epistemic narrowness seen in earlier platform-governance regimes, and that the remedy is deliberate, structured public and worker involvement.

## Red-teaming as inherited labor practice

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] builds its case historically, drawing a direct analogy to commercial content moderation. Just as moderation was normalized as an opaque, outsourced, market-driven practice before its harms to workers and its hidden value politics were well understood, red-teaming is being "rapidly normalized" as a core AI safety ritual without public knowledge of who performs it, under what arrangements, or according to whose definitions of harm. The paper's distinctive contribution is to foreground labor and psychological cost: red-teamers, whether internal employees, outsourced vendors, or volunteer participants at events like DEFCON, face secondary trauma, moral injury, and precarity from the adversarial, transgressive imaginative work the role demands—harms compounded by NDAs, surveillance, and inconsistent well-being supports. Crucially, Gillespie et al. reject the techno-solutionist deflection that automation will soon eliminate this labor; rather, automation threatens to further obscure the human work and human cost underlying red-teaming, making the current moment urgent for empirical, interdisciplinary study before practices calcify into unaccountable norms.

## Evaluation as public epistemic practice

[[Matias2025-px]] approaches the same underlying problem—whose values and knowledge count in judging AI systems—from the standpoint of evaluation methodology rather than labor history. Where Gillespie et al. ask who bears the cost of probing AI systems, Matias et al. ask who is epistemically positioned to know whether an evaluation is even measuring the right thing. Their central move is to reframe public involvement not as a legitimacy exercise or political concession but as a scientific necessity: lived-experience expertise supplies situated knowledge unavailable to professional evaluators, and this knowledge measurably improves rigor at identifiable stages of evaluation—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation. The case studies (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, Chicago police complaint relabeling, Reddit fact-checking experiments) each demonstrate how purely technical evaluation missed harms or dynamics that participatory re-analysis surfaced, directly paralleling Gillespie et al.'s claim that internally defined harm categories, set by designers unrepresentative of affected populations, are epistemically and ethically insufficient.

## A shared diagnosis, complementary remedies

Read together, the two papers trace an arc from diagnosis to prescription across the same underlying concern. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] diagnoses the problem at the level of who performs safety labor and under what conditions, warning that outsourcing and volunteerism can become extractive reliance on already-marginalized communities even while appearing participatory. [[Matias2025-px]] complements this by showing how, if structured well through established participatory-science methods, public involvement can overcome the very objections (subjectivity, cost, unreliability) that have historically justified excluding affected communities from evaluation design. Where Gillespie et al. call for a coordinated empirical research agenda treating red-teaming as a labor and public-health issue, Matias et al. supply a positive methodological template—five evaluation stages amenable to cocreation—that could be read as partially answering that call for red-teaming itself, since red-teaming is one site where equipoise, measurement, and interpretation are all contested. Neither paper treats "making AI safety more public" as merely a matter of transparency or disclosure; both insist that the quality and validity of safety knowledge itself depends on broadening who participates in producing it, and on taking seriously the human costs and situated expertise of those who do.
