# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The Sociotechnical Turn in AI Safety Practice

A common move unites these three papers: each insists that a practice usually described in technical language—red-teaming, evaluation, benchmarking—is actually organized by people, institutions, and value choices, and that treating it as a purely technical procedure hides the very things that determine whether it works. [[Gillespie2026-aa]], [[Unknown2025-qj]], and [[Matias2025-px]] together sketch an emerging research agenda: understand AI safety work as labor, as participation, and as science simultaneously, rather than as a checklist to be automated or standardized away.

## Red-Teaming as Labor and Value System

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] supplies the critical genealogy. By drawing an explicit parallel to commercial content moderation, the paper argues that red-teaming is following the same arc: rapid normalization as an industry-defined safety ritual, internal and proprietary determination of what counts as "harm," outsourcing to precarious crowdworkers and vendors, and a near-total silence on the psychological toll—secondary trauma, moral injury—borne by the people asked to adversarially imagine the worst uses of a system. Crucially, the paper reframes red-teaming's core epistemic problem as a labor problem: internal red-teamers, bound by NDAs and corporate incentive structures, lack the sociocultural and linguistic diversity needed to surface the harms that matter to differently situated publics. This sets up a tension that the other two papers try to resolve from different angles—if internal, proprietary red-teaming is structurally too narrow, what should replace or supplement it?

## Widening Participation, Testing Its Limits

[[Unknown2025-qj]] answers empirically, studying red-teaming events that explicitly open the practice to public participants rather than confining it to in-house teams or paid contractors. Based on interviews and observation at public red-teaming events, the paper shows that framing and institutional context are not incidental—they actively shape what counts as a vulnerability worth surfacing, echoing Gillespie's point about value judgments being embedded rather than neutral. Public-facing events surface considerations that internal, cybersecurity-derived adversarial methods routinely miss, suggesting that democratizing participation is not just an ethical add-on but changes the substantive content of what gets identified as harm. Yet the paper is careful not to romanticize this: adversarial methods borrowed from military and cybersecurity traditions require real adaptation, and the practice remains highly variable across organizational contexts—an unevenness that maps onto Gillespie's warning about volunteer/event-based red-teaming (e.g., DEFCON-style events) risking extractive reliance on marginalized participants without offering scalable, sustained support.

## From Public Interest to Better Science

[[Matias2025-px]] pushes the argument furthest, reframing public involvement not as a legitimacy exercise or harm-surfacing exercise but as a mechanism for improving the *scientific rigor* of AI evaluation itself. Where Gillespie diagnoses a labor and ethics problem and Unknown2025-qj documents public red-teaming as an accountability practice, Matias2025-px offers a methodological architecture: five stages of evaluation—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation—where lived-experience expertise supplies situated knowledge that professional evaluators structurally lack. The case studies (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, Chicago police complaint labeling, Reddit field experiments) function as proof that participatory involvement isn't merely additive but corrective: cocreated evaluation caught biases and mischaracterizations that expert-only, purely technical evaluation missed entirely. This directly substantiates Gillespie's claim that internally-defined value judgments are epistemically impoverished, while also answering a challenge implicit in Unknown2025-qj's fieldwork—how to make participatory harm-identification methodologically rigorous rather than ad hoc or merely symbolic.

## A Shared Arc

Read together, the three papers trace an arc from diagnosis to intervention. Gillespie names the problem—red-teaming as an opaque, labor-intensive, value-laden practice modeled on troubling precedent—and calls for an interdisciplinary empirical research network. Unknown2025-qj investigates one proposed remedy, public red-teaming events, showing both their promise for surfacing overlooked harms and their institutional messiness. Matias2025-px generalizes the underlying principle into a methodological framework applicable beyond red-teaming to AI evaluation writ large, arguing that participatory rigor is not opposed to scientific rigor but constitutive of it. The throughline across all three is a refusal to let "safety evaluation" remain a black box defined solely by AI companies' internal incentives—insisting instead that who does this work, under what conditions, and with whose knowledge, is inseparable from whether the resulting safety claims can be trusted at all.
