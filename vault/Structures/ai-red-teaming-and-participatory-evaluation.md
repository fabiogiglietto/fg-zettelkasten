# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## From Technical Procedure to Sociotechnical Practice

The three papers filed here share a foundational move: they refuse to treat AI safety evaluation as a self-contained technical exercise and instead insist on its irreducibly sociotechnical character. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes this argument sharpest for red-teaming, positioning it alongside content moderation as a practice constituted by labor arrangements, embedded value judgments, and human costs — not just algorithms and benchmarks. [[Matias2025-px]] generalizes the claim to AI evaluation writ large, arguing that reliability, contextual performance, and security depend on organizational and social layers that purely technical evaluations systematically miss. [[Unknown2025-qj]] applies this frame empirically to red-teaming events, showing how institutional context and participant composition shape what even *counts* as a harm worth surfacing. Read together, they establish that "safety" is co-produced by who evaluates, under what conditions, and answering to whose definitions of harm.

## Who Does the Work, and at What Cost

A second thread concerns the political economy and human costs of evaluation labor. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] pushes this hardest, drawing explicit parallels to commercial content moderation: outsourcing to third-party vendors, crowdworker precarity, NDA-constrained silence, and the distinctive psychological toll — secondary trauma, moral injury — of adversarial imagination on demand. This concern reframes red-teaming as a public health issue as much as a security one. [[Unknown2025-qj]] partially answers by looking at public events where volunteers rather than outsourced workers do the probing, but Gillespie and colleagues raise a warning that runs through this cluster: broadening participation can slide into extractive reliance on marginalized communities. The papers thus share an ambivalence about volunteer and event-based models — democratizing in aspiration, potentially exploitative in practice.

## Public Involvement as Epistemic, Not Merely Political

Where [[Gillespie2026-aa]] emphasizes what is hidden and harmful about current arrangements, [[Matias2025-px]] articulates the constructive counterpart: lived-experience expertise materially improves the *science* of evaluation, not just its legitimacy. The five-stage framework — equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation — supplies a methodological vocabulary for what [[Unknown2025-qj]] observes ethnographically, namely that public-facing red-teaming surfaces vulnerabilities invisible to internal teams. Concrete cases (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, community recoding of Chicago police complaints) show participation catching errors that professional evaluators missed. This inverts a familiar defensive posture in ML circles: participation is not a threat to rigor but a condition for it.

## Institutional Framing and the Contest Over "Harm"

All three papers converge on a governance worry: if AI companies define harms internally, the value judgments constituting safety are quietly proprietary. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] frames this as a diversity-of-affected-users problem compounded by NDAs and corporate incentives; [[Unknown2025-qj]] shows empirically that institutional framing shapes what red-teamers even look for; [[Matias2025-px]] mobilizes equipoise from medical ethics as a mechanism for allowing adversarial stakeholders to commit to shared evidentiary processes. The line of argument across the cluster is that public-interest evaluation requires not just adding participants but redesigning the institutions that scope, commission, and interpret evaluation results.

## Open Questions and Points of Tension

The papers do not fully agree on scale and mechanism. [[Matias2025-px]] is optimistic that established participatory-science methods can absorb objections about cost, pace, and reliability, whereas [[Gillespie2026-aa]] is more skeptical about whether current market-driven arrangements will accommodate such methods before opacity becomes entrenched. [[Unknown2025-qj]] occupies a middle empirical position, documenting variability across events without resolving whether public red-teaming can scale as accountability infrastructure or remains a symbolic supplement to industry practice. A research agenda emerging from this cluster would need to connect participatory-science methodology (Matias et al.) to labor and well-being protections (Gillespie et al.) and to the institutional design questions foregrounded by the public red-teaming study — a genuinely interdisciplinary program that none of the three papers alone completes.
