# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The reframing: safety practices as sociotechnical labor, not technical procedure

The organizing move across this cluster is a rejection of the premise that AI safety evaluation—whether red-teaming, benchmarking, or transparency disclosure—is a neutral technical exercise that can be optimized independently of the people, institutions, and values that shape it. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes this argument most directly by tracing red-teaming's rapid institutionalization as a governance-legible safety ritual, then showing how thin the public's actual knowledge is of who performs it, under what labor conditions, and according to whose values. Its explicit move is genealogical: red-teaming is following the same arc as commercial content moderation—internally defined harm categories, outsourcing to precarious crowdworkers and vendors, and a structural neglect of the psychological toll (secondary trauma, moral injury) borne by the humans doing adversarial, transgressive imaginative labor. This lineage matters because it reframes red-teaming not as a discrete technical checkpoint but as a labor system with the same extractive dynamics as "ghost work," where automation rhetoric threatens to further obscure rather than resolve the human costs.

[[Unknown2025-qj]] extends this sociotechnical framing empirically, moving from origin-story critique to grounded fieldwork: 26 interviews and observation across three public red-teaming events. Where [[Gillespie2026-aa]] worries about the opacity and precarity of red-teaming labor, [[Unknown2025-qj]] asks what happens when red-teaming is deliberately opened to publics rather than confined to internal teams or paid vendors—finding that public-facing events surface harms and considerations that internal, cybersecurity-descended red-teaming methods are not built to catch. The two papers thus triangulate the same object from opposite ends: one diagnosing the risks of the market-driven status quo (outsourced, NDA-bound, psychologically costly), the other examining an alternative institutional form (open, participatory, publicly convened) and showing that institutional context and framing determine what even counts as a "vulnerability" worth flagging. Together they establish that red-teaming's meaning is not fixed by its cybersecurity ancestry but constructed anew by who is invited in and how their labor is organized, protected, or exploited.

## From critique to method: participatory science as an evaluation upgrade

[[Matias2025-px]] pushes this line of argument from diagnosis toward methodology. Rather than arguing that public involvement is merely an ethical or legitimacy-conferring add-on, it makes the stronger scientific claim that participatory methods—drawn from citizen science and participatory research traditions—improve the *rigor* of AI evaluation itself, addressing failure modes that purely technical metrics miss. Its five-stage framework (equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation) gives concrete shape to what [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] gesture toward more abstractly: lived-experience expertise is not just a moral corrective to insider blind spots but a source of situated knowledge that changes what gets measured and how it is interpreted—illustrated by cases like the reanalysis of the Allegheny Family Screening Tool, where community-informed reanalysis surfaced bias an AUC-based evaluation had missed. This paper effectively answers the "so what" implicit in the red-teaming critiques: if internal, proprietary red-teaming reproduces narrow value judgments (as [[Gillespie2026-aa]] warns) and if public red-teaming events surface different harms (as [[Unknown2025-qj]] finds), then participatory science offers the methodological infrastructure to make that inclusion systematic and scientifically defensible rather than ad hoc.

## Testing what "meaningful" public-facing safety actually requires

[[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] sits at a slight remove from the labor-and-participation focus of the other three, but it belongs to the same broader argument: that safety interventions aimed at the public must be evaluated empirically for whether they actually work, not merely for whether they satisfy a regulatory template. Its finding that identity disclosure (the EU AI Act's preferred mechanism) does nothing to reduce persuasive harm, while intent disclosure roughly halves it, is a cautionary empirical illustration of exactly the gap [[Matias2025-px]] worries about—evaluation regimes that look rigorous on paper (a compliance-driven label) but fail the sociotechnical reality of how people actually process AI-mediated influence. In that sense it operationalizes the collection's central claim from a different angle: transparency, like red-teaming, is a public-facing safety practice whose design choices carry hidden value judgments (what counts as adequate disclosure) and needs the same participatory, empirically grounded scrutiny urged elsewhere in this set, rather than being treated as a solved technical checkbox.

## The arc

Read together, the four papers trace a progression: first, an indictment of red-teaming's current opaque, labor-extractive, market-driven form ([[Gillespie2026-aa]]); second, an empirical look at what happens when that form is opened to public participation, revealing both promise and institutional contingency ([[Unknown2025-qj]]); third, a methodological argument for why and how public involvement should be built into the scientific core of AI evaluation rather than bolted on ([[Matias2025-px]]); and finally, a concrete demonstration of the stakes of getting public-facing safety design wrong, even in a domain (persuasion disclosure) far from red-teaming's original cybersecurity roots ([[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]]). The throughline is a shared insistence that AI safety evaluation—whatever its specific form—is inescapably social: it encodes labor arrangements, value judgments, and epistemic assumptions about whose knowledge counts, and its legitimacy and rigor both depend on making those dimensions visible and contestable rather than settled behind NDAs, vendor contracts, or compliance checkboxes.
