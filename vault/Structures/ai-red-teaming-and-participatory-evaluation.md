# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## From Technical Procedure to Sociotechnical Practice

The animating move across this cluster is a reframing: AI safety work—red-teaming, evaluation, benchmarking—is not a neutral technical procedure but a social practice embedded in labor arrangements, institutional politics, and contested value judgments. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes this case most directly, arguing that red-teaming's rapid normalization as *the* central safety practice has outpaced any public understanding of who performs it, under what conditions, and according to whose definitions of harm. The paper's key intervention is historical: it maps red-teaming onto the trajectory of commercial content moderation, predicting (and warning against) a similar pattern of outsourced labor, internally-defined harm categories, and neglected worker welfare becoming quietly entrenched before scrutiny catches up. [[Unknown2025-qj]] picks up this sociotechnical framing empirically, using interviews and observation at public red-teaming events to show that red-teaming's cybersecurity and military origins are being stretched and adapted—sometimes awkwardly—to accommodate the more diffuse, public-interest harms distinctive to generative AI. Together these two papers establish the core claim of the topic: the *how* and *who* of red-teaming shape *what* counts as a vulnerability, and that shaping is currently happening with almost no public visibility or accountability.

## Labor, Harm, and the Human Cost of Safety Work

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] pushes further than critique of institutional opacity into an argument about embodied cost: red-teamers, like content moderators before them, absorb psychological injury—secondary trauma, moral injury, PTSD-like symptoms—as a structural byproduct of being asked to inhabit adversarial, transgressive personas on demand. This labor lens is largely absent from [[Unknown2025-qj]] and [[Matias2025-px]], which focus more on epistemic and participatory questions than on occupational health, but it supplies an essential counterweight: calls to "bring the public in" to safety work cannot be naive about the conditions of that participation. Volunteer and event-based red-teaming, which [[Unknown2025-qj]] treats as a promising democratizing mechanism, is flagged by [[Gillespie2026-aa]] as potentially extractive—leaning on marginalized communities' unpaid expertise while claiming the mantle of public interest. The tension between these two papers is productive rather than contradictory: it marks out a live question the field has not resolved—how to expand who participates in adversarial probing of AI systems without reproducing the labor arbitrage and burnout dynamics of the content-moderation economy that preceded it.

## Participation as Scientific Method, Not Political Concession

[[Matias2025-px]] broadens the argument from red-teaming specifically to AI evaluation generally, and makes the sharpest normative claim in the set: public involvement is not a legitimacy-enhancing add-on but a way of making evaluation *more scientifically rigorous*. Drawing on participatory science across healthcare, policing, and child welfare, the paper identifies five evaluation stages—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation—where lived-experience expertise catches what purely technical evaluation misses (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, which surfaced bias invisible to an AUC-based audit, is the paper's paradigm case). This reframes the concerns raised by [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] in epistemic rather than only ethical terms: it is not just fairer to include diverse red-teamers and evaluators, it produces better science, because internal teams structurally lack the situated knowledge needed to anticipate harms outside their own experience. Read together, these three papers converge on a shared diagnosis—AI companies defining harm categories internally, using homogenous in-house teams, is both an equity problem and a validity problem—while [[Matias2025-px]] supplies the methodological vocabulary (equipoise, cocreated measurement, community debriefing) that [[Gillespie2026-aa]]'s call for a "coordinated research network" gestures toward but does not itself develop.

## Infrastructures for Public Deliberation

[[Goldberg2026-eb]] extends the participatory logic outward from evaluation of specific AI systems to the broader question of what participatory infrastructure for AI-mediated public life could look like. Its four technology families—collective dialogue systems, bridging systems, community-driven moderation, proof-of-humanity—can be read as an attempt to operationalize, at platform scale, the kind of inclusive, deliberative process that [[Matias2025-px]] argues is scientifically necessary and that [[Unknown2025-qj]] observes emerging in ad hoc form at public red-teaming events. The paper's insistence that LLMs should *augment* rather than substitute for human deliberation, and its concern that synthetic participation risks diluting public agency even while improving representativeness, echoes the labor concerns of [[Gillespie2026-aa]]: technology that promises to scale up public involvement can just as easily obscure or displace the human judgment it depends on. Its account of community moderator burnout and inadequate tooling is, in effect, the content-moderation analogy from [[Gillespie2026-aa]] revisited from the infrastructure side rather than the red-teaming side—reinforcing that participatory promises need durable institutional and labor support, not just better interfaces.

## The Political Economy Behind the Call for Participation

[[Hepp2026-oi]] supplies necessary context for why these calls for public and sociotechnical framing are needed in the first place. Its ethnography of Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring"—the discursive thickenings around AGI arrival, job loss, and geopolitical competition circulating through EA- and Rationalist-adjacent communities—describes the very milieu in which harm categories, safety priorities, and existential-risk framings that structure red-teaming and evaluation get informally set, well upstream of any public involvement. Where [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Matias2025-px]] argue that value judgments about harm are currently made too narrowly by AI labs, [[Hepp2026-oi]] shows empirically who those narrow circles are and how they present deeply political long-termist commitments as apolitical technical common sense. This closes the arc of the topic: the case for treating red-teaming and evaluation as sociotechnical and participatory is not abstract proceduralism but a direct response to a concentrated, self-legitimating imaginative landscape whose "quiet" agenda-setting otherwise stands in for genuine public involvement.
