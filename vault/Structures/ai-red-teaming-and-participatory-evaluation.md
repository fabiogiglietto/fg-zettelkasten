# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The sociotechnical turn in AI safety evaluation

A common move unites these three papers: each takes a practice that AI companies and policymakers have treated as a technical checklist item—red-teaming, benchmarking, evaluation—and insists it be re-described as a social process with politics, labor, and epistemics of its own. [[Gillespie2026-aa]], [[Unknown2025-qj]], and [[Matias2025-px]] together sketch an emerging research program: safety evaluation cannot be adequately understood, let alone improved, without attending to who does the evaluating, under what conditions, with what values, and with what stake in the outcome.

## Red-teaming as labor and value system

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] supplies the most sustained diagnosis of red-teaming's hidden social infrastructure. Its central analogy—red-teaming as the "next content moderation"—does real argumentative work: just as commercial content moderation was quietly outsourced, its harm taxonomies set unilaterally by platforms, and its psychological costs offloaded onto precarious crowdworkers, red-teaming is following the same trajectory. The paper's contribution is to name three dimensions that a purely technical framing erases: the value judgments embedded in deciding what counts as a "harm" worth probing (currently made internally and proprietarily by developers unrepresentative of affected publics); the labor arrangements—outsourcing, NDAs, crowdworker precarity, extractive reliance on volunteer/marginalized participants at events like DEFCON—that organize who actually does the probing; and the psychological toll (secondary trauma, moral injury) of adversarial and transgressive imaginative labor that goes largely unsupported. Crucially, Gillespie et al. resist the techno-solutionist deflection that automation will soon make these concerns moot, arguing instead that automation obscures rather than eliminates the human labor underneath.

[[Unknown2025-qj]] can be read as an empirical companion to this diagnosis, moving from analogy to fieldwork. Based on interviews and observation at public red-teaming events, it pushes on exactly the institutional variable Gillespie et al. flag as decisive: framing. Where red-teaming happens, who convenes it, and what institutional interests shape the event determine what counts as a vulnerability at all—the same adversarial toolkit borrowed from cybersecurity and military testing produces different findings depending on whether it is deployed internally, commercially, or as a public-interest exercise. This paper's distinctive contribution is to take seriously the possibility that red-teaming's origins in cybersecurity/military contexts are not neutral technical heritage but a framing legacy that must be actively reworked if red-teaming is to serve public rather than narrowly organizational interests. Read alongside [[Gillespie2026-aa]], it suggests that public-facing events are simultaneously a partial remedy to the internal-values problem and a site that reproduces the extractive-participation problem Gillespie et al. warn about.

## From critique to method: participation as scientific improvement

[[Matias2025-px]] shifts the register from labor and harm-critique to methodology, and in doing so completes the arc: if red-teaming's central problem is that narrow, unaccountable actors define what harms and vulnerabilities matter, then the constructive response is to build public and lived-experience participation directly into the scientific architecture of evaluation. Matias et al. decline to treat participation as a merely political or legitimacy-conferring add-on—the paper's most provocative claim is that participatory involvement improves the *rigor* of AI evaluation itself, not just its acceptability. This reframes the stakes established by the other two papers: where [[Gillespie2026-aa]] worries about who bears the costs of evaluation labor and [[Unknown2025-qj]] shows how institutional framing shapes what counts as harm, [[Matias2025-px]] offers a structural account of *where in the evaluation pipeline* participation matters—equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation—and marshals case studies (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, Chicago police complaint relabeling, Reddit fact-checking experiments) to show lived-experience expertise catching flaws that credentialed technical evaluation missed entirely.

Taken together, the three papers trace a single line of inquiry from problem to practice to prescription. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] establishes that red-teaming's opacity and market-driven form conceal an exploitative and value-laden labor system urgently in need of empirical study. [[Unknown2025-qj]] investigates that system in situ, showing how institutional framing determines what public-interest red-teaming can actually accomplish. [[Matias2025-px]] then generalizes the underlying insight beyond red-teaming to AI evaluation writ large, offering participatory science as a positive methodology rather than only a critical lens. None of the papers treats participation or labor consideration as incidental to safety practice; each insists that the sociotechnical dimensions—whose values, whose labor, whose knowledge—are constitutive of whether an evaluation can be trusted at all.
