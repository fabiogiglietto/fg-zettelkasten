# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The sociotechnical turn in safety evaluation

The organizing claim across this cluster is that evaluating AI systems—whether through adversarial red-teaming or formal scientific testing—is never a purely technical act. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] makes this case most directly for red-teaming: probing a model for harmful outputs is not just an engineering procedure but a practice saturated with value judgments about what counts as harm, who gets to decide, and whose labor absorbs the cost of finding out. [[Matias2025-px]] generalizes the argument to AI evaluation writ large, insisting that reliability, performance, and security claims about AI systems cannot be assessed without attending to the social and organizational layers in which the system is embedded. Both papers reject a model of evaluation as a closed technical loop between developers and their own benchmarks, and both locate the missing ingredient in the same place: the people outside the lab who are affected by, or expert in, the harms being tested for.

## Labor, harm, and the hidden workers of evaluation

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] grounds this claim historically, tracing red-teaming's trajectory against the earlier normalization of commercial content moderation. The parallel is substantive, not decorative: both practices emerged as ad hoc, internally defined, rapidly scaled labor systems; both outsource psychologically costly work to vendors and crowdworkers with weak protections; and both leave the definition of "harm" to designers who do not reflect the diversity of people affected by their systems. The paper's distinctive contribution is to foreground the embodied cost of this work—secondary trauma, moral injury, the demand that workers inhabit adversarial personas—and to warn that framing automation as an imminent solution obscures rather than resolves the labor and governance problems already in play. This is a corrective to techno-solutionist narratives that treat red-teaming as a pipeline stage to be optimized rather than a workforce to be protected.

## Participation as scientific method, not political concession

[[Matias2025-px]] extends the sociotechnical diagnosis into a methodological program. Where [[Gillespie2026-aa]] worries about who absorbs the costs of evaluation, Matias et al. argue that public and lived-experience participation actually improves the *epistemics* of evaluation—its validity, generalizability, and capacity to surface errors invisible to professional evaluators. Their five-stage framework (equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation) gives structural specificity to a claim that in [[Gillespie2026-aa]] remains more diagnostic than prescriptive: it says not just that affected communities *should* be involved, but precisely *where* in the evaluation pipeline their involvement changes scientific outcomes. The Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis and the Chicago police-complaint relabeling cases function as proof-of-concept rebuttals to the common worry that participatory methods trade rigor for inclusion—here inclusion is what produced the rigor, catching biases and miscoded harms that purely technical metrics (AUC, single-category official coding) had missed. Read together, these two papers reframe the red-teaming/evaluation apparatus as something that is currently broken in a specific, correctable way: it treats affected publics as subjects of evaluation rather than as sources of situated expertise necessary to do the evaluation well.

## The automation counter-current

[[Jayaram2026-wd]] sits at a tangent to this program and is useful mainly as a foil. It describes PAT, an agentic system for automating a cognate form of evaluation—scientific peer review—built explicitly to address a scaling problem (submission volume outpacing human reviewer capacity) rather than a participation or value-adequacy problem. Its four-level taxonomy of AI roles in review (author tool, reviewer tool, supporting reviewer, total automation) treats evaluation as a capacity-allocation question to be solved by inference-scaled compute, with success measured in detection accuracy and author satisfaction rather than in whose values or lived experience inform the judgments being automated. This is instructive by contrast: it is precisely the trajectory [[Gillespie2026-aa]] warns against when it cautions that automation claims in red-teaming "distract from urgent labor, well-being, and governance concerns," and it embodies the technical-evaluation-as-closed-loop model that [[Matias2025-px]] argues is scientifically insufficient even on its own terms. Juxtaposed against the other two papers, PAT's pilot at STOC and ICML illustrates how readily the discourse of safety and quality evaluation can be absorbed into pure throughput optimization unless deliberately counterbalanced by sociotechnical and participatory framings.

## The arc

Taken together, the three papers trace a tension rather than a consensus: [[Gillespie2026-aa]] names the labor and value problem in current red-teaming practice; [[Matias2025-px]] proposes a constructive, methodologically grounded response—participatory science as a route to *better* evaluation, not just fairer process; and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] demonstrates the gravitational pull toward automated, technically-defined evaluation that the first two papers are implicitly arguing against. The structure note's throughline is a call to keep evaluation's sociotechnical character—its labor, its values, its publics—visible and load-bearing, precisely as evaluation infrastructure scales and the temptation to treat it as a solvable engineering problem grows strongest.
