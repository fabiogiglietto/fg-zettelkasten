# Public and Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Safety Evaluation

## The sociotechnical turn in AI safety practice

The organizing claim across this cluster is that the core practices of AI safety—red-teaming, evaluation, transparency disclosure—cannot be adequately understood, designed, or governed as narrowly technical procedures. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] states this most directly: red-teaming is not a neutral adversarial test but a sociotechnical system built from value judgments about what counts as harm, labor arrangements that determine who does the probing, and psychological costs borne by workers who must inhabit transgressive personas. [[Matias2025-px]] makes the parallel argument for evaluation more broadly, insisting that reliability, contextual performance, and validity are not purely technical properties of a model but emerge from the social and organizational layers in which AI systems are embedded. Read together, these two papers reframe "safety" work as continuous with older debates about invisible labor and situated knowledge in sociotechnical systems, rather than as a novel technical subfield invented by frontier AI labs.

## Labor, opacity, and whose values count

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] draws its evidentiary weight from an explicit analogy to the history of commercial content moderation: internal, proprietary definitions of harm; outsourcing to vendors and crowdworkers; inconsistent, NDA-constrained well-being protections; and a public that is asked to trust a process it cannot see. The paper's warning is that automation narratives ("red-teaming will soon be handled by AI") function to obscure rather than resolve these labor and governance problems. [[Matias2025-px]] offers a constructive counterpart to this critique: rather than treating outside participation as a threat to rigor or a mere legitimacy exercise, it argues that lived-experience expertise measurably improves evaluation science itself, citing cases—the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, community relabeling of Chicago police complaints, participatory field experiments on recommender systems—where affected communities surfaced flaws that closed, developer-controlled evaluation missed. Its five-stage framework (equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, interpretation) gives Gillespie's diagnosis a methodological answer: the same value-laden judgments that currently sit hidden inside corporate red-teaming protocols are exactly the junctures where structured public involvement adds scientific, not just political, value.

## Structural barriers to independent evaluation

[[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] supplies the political-economic context that explains why red-teaming and evaluation have developed in the opaque, market-driven form both [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Matias2025-px]] worry about. Its account of industry control over data, funding, and access—modeled explicitly on tobacco- and fossil-fuel-style agnotology—shows that the asymmetries Gillespie identifies in red-teaming (internal definitions of harm, NDA constraints, outsourced and unaccountable labor) are instances of a more general pattern: companies can suppress inconvenient internal research, fund selectively, and use "open science" norms strategically against independent researchers who lack comparable data access. This paper sharpens the stakes of Matias's participatory proposal: public and lived-experience involvement in evaluation is not simply a nice-to-have improvement to method, but a potential counterweight to a research ecosystem where companies otherwise remain the primary—and structurally compromised—narrators of their own systems' safety.

## Theoretical grounding: agency as social status, not technical property

[[Anicker2024-vp]] operates at a more foundational register but supplies conceptual scaffolding for the whole cluster's central move. By treating agency as a socially granted and monitored license rather than an inherent property of a system, it reframes the question "is this system safe/does it act reliably?" as inseparable from social practices of attribution, trust, and surveillance. This resonates with Gillespie's and Matias's insistence that evaluation is not a matter of reading off intrinsic technical facts but of negotiating what abilities and risks a system will be socially recognized as having—work that is necessarily distributed across labor, institutions, and publics rather than settled once inside a lab.

## A concrete test case: transparency as sociotechnical design

[[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] provides an empirical demonstration of what happens when this sociotechnical dimension is ignored in favor of a narrowly technical compliance checkbox. Testing EU AI Act–style identity disclosure against disclosure of persuasive intent, the study finds that merely labeling a chatbot as AI does essentially nothing to reduce its persuasive power, while revealing what the system is trying to do roughly halves persuasion by activating recipients' persuasion knowledge. The paper's core argument—that meaningful transparency must address purpose and intent, not just machine identity—is a direct instance of the broader thesis running through this topic: safety-relevant interventions designed as technical/procedural fixes (a label, a disclosure statement, an internal red-team sign-off) can fail exactly where they ignore the social and interpretive work that recipients, workers, or publics must do to make such interventions meaningful.

## The arc

Taken together, these papers trace an arc from diagnosis to remedy to grounding to test. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] diagnose why current red-teaming and evaluation practices are opaque, value-laden, and structurally tilted toward industry control. [[Matias2025-px]] proposes participatory science as a remedy that improves rigor rather than merely appeasing publics. [[Anicker2024-vp]] offers a theoretical vocabulary—agency as socially licensed status—for why such practices are inescapably social rather than purely technical. And [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows empirically what is lost when safety-adjacent interventions (transparency disclosures) are designed without attending to that social dimension. The collective upshot is a call for treating red-teaming, evaluation, and disclosure as public, participatory, and labor-conscious institutions-in-formation, before their current ad hoc, company-controlled form hardens into unquestioned infrastructure.
