# Cross-National Digital Campaign Analysis

## Two anchoring cases: Germany 2021 and Brazil's electoral crises

This cluster of notes traces an expansion outward from the Italian-centered work on platform data (visible in the shared DNA with [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]]) into a genuinely comparative research agenda, organized around two recurring empirical anchors — the German 2021 federal election and the successive Brazilian electoral crises of 2018, 2022, and 2024 — and around a growing infrastructural preoccupation with what can even be studied given platform access regimes.

The German thread is methodological as much as substantive. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs against traditional computer-vision pipelines for reading candidate visibility on Instagram during the 2021 campaign, while [[Schulte2026-df]] pushes the 2025 federal election into a full multi-platform (X + TikTok) infrastructural study, arguing that party-specific platform choices and weakly correlated text/voice/facial channels make single-platform designs insufficient — and that the deeper problem is the absence of reusable research infrastructure, not a shortage of case studies. [[Rothut2026-or]] extends the German case chronologically and thematically, tracing how the Querdenken protest movement on Telegram became a conduit for mainstreaming far-right and conspiracist content, embedding German publics in an explicitly transnational (Austrian, Italian-language, QAnon) network.

Brazil supplies the richest and most recurrent case, followed across multiple elections and methods. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] examines how parliamentarians battled for epistemic authority during the Manaus COVID crisis; [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] audits irregular Facebook political ads in the 2018 election; [[Kansaon2025-id]] traces coordinated WhatsApp activity from the 2022 election into the January 8, 2023 insurrection; and [[Ventura2025-sw]] examines WhatsApp deactivation effects on misinformation exposure. A pair of panel-survey studies — [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] — together show how belief in electoral fraud narratives corrodes both political tolerance and trust in results, while mainstream news use (even among Bolsonaro voters) acts as a countervailing, trust-sustaining force. [[Ventura2026-yc]] closes this arc with the 2024 X ban, showing how platform-level state intervention itself produces durable partisan sorting — a finding that reframes "misinformation" research as inseparable from questions of platform governance.

## Coordinated inauthentic behavior as a comparative object

A second throughline treats coordinated inauthentic behavior (CIB) not as a US-specific pathology but as a phenomenon requiring cross-regime comparison and methodological scrutiny. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] argues that Serbia's SNS-linked networks represent a subtler, harder-to-detect form of digital authoritarianism than centralized bot farms, drawing on non-English secondary sources typically absent from Anglophone scholarship. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes Philippine influence operations as political brokerage — organizational infrastructure rather than isolated propaganda — based on interviews with operation insiders. [[Oprea2025-lf]] documents hyperactive-user amplification across all major Romanian parties in the 2024 EU elections. Meanwhile [[Pante2025-pq]] and [[Appel2026-qr]] apply methodological correctives to earlier, more sensational claims: the former re-tests inter-state coordination claims against control datasets, the latter shows that Meta-identified deceptive networks reached tens of millions but that most exposure was indirect (via reshares) and that engagement effects mostly evaporate once pre-exposure user traits are controlled. [[Kim2026-wg]] extends this rigor backward to 2016, using direct exposure measurement to demonstrate geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising with real turnout effects — a rare case where microtargeting harms are causally substantiated rather than merely inferred.

## Cross-national misinformation, populism, and the WEIRD critique

A more explicitly comparative-theoretical cluster interrogates whether misinformation dynamics travel across contexts at all. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames this as an editorial mandate: existing misinformation research overwhelmingly reflects WEIRD democracies, and the special issue it introduces (which several of these notes belong to) seeks correctives. [[Nenno2025-xa]] operationalizes this empirically, finding that "newsworthiness" markers of flagged misinformation vary meaningfully between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the US and Brazil forming a distinctive polarized-election cluster. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] scales this comparative ambition to 26 countries and 32 million parliamentarian tweets, showing that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing — only their conjunction, radical-right populism, does. [[Zhu2026-tn]] performs a parallel six-country network analysis of alternative-mainstream audience overlap, showing that the marginality of alternative media is not intrinsic but shaped by national media systems and populist parties' institutional access. [[Iris2026-pg]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] extend the radical-right lens into visibility and visual strategy: the former finds systematic media over-visibility of radical-right actors across five countries in the 2024 EU elections, the latter shows how the Sweden Democrats tailor securitizing visual rhetoric platform-by-platform, weaponizing TikTok memes built from non-Swedish footage. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] sits above this cluster as a programmatic call: polarization research, like misinformation research, needs integration across disciplines and geography rather than continued US-centrism.

## Platform access as the shared infrastructural constraint

Running underneath nearly all these studies is a second-order concern: what can be known given platforms' data regimes. [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Lukito2026-nb]], and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] together chart this terrain systematically — from the DSA's reshaping of API access, to a 72-tool taxonomy revealing a "price, proficiency, or permission" problem that structurally disadvantages non-Western researchers, to the historical arc from Twitter's "Wild West" through the Academic API's "Golden Age" to the post-Musk paywall, with the DSA's Article 40 offering a fragile regulatory corrective. [[Jurg2025-ur]] applies this lens empirically, auditing YouTube's inconsistent "News Funding Notice" labeling and opaque removal practices across EU languages during the 2024 European elections. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Allcott2025-jb]] extend the infrastructural theme to advertising specifically — the former probing how delivery algorithms themselves (not just advertiser targeting) shape political ad reach and cost, the latter reporting a large Meta-collaboration field experiment finding no detectable attitudinal effects of six weeks of ad removal, a null result that itself depends on unusually good platform access. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] round out this methodological lineage, the latter explicitly building a European (German-French-Italian) counterpoint to the US-centric Guess et al. newsfeed studies that this whole cluster implicitly answers.

## Emergent frontiers: AI, propaganda, and narrative

A final, more recent set of papers signals where this research agenda is heading next. [[Lin2025-xp]] tests LLM persuasion experimentally across four national contexts (US, Canada, Poland, plus a US ballot measure), finding conversational AI persuasion effects exceeding traditional advertising and a troubling asymmetry in factual accuracy favoring left-leaning candidates. [[Richter2026-bt]] historicizes the pre-GenAI stakeholder landscape in US and German AI discourse, showing diverging institutionalization (Germany) versus individualization around tech influencers (US) that plausibly shaped each country's subsequent AI governance. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Makeev2026-ma]] extend the narrative and propaganda lens beyond coordination metrics toward meaning-making itself — the former theorizing "deep stories" as collaborative sensemaking around US election denial, the latter modeling the dual domestic/foreign propaganda objectives of authoritarian wartime communication in the Russo-Ukrainian case. Finally, [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] offers a comparatively calmer counterpoint: even in the low-polarization Scandinavian democracies, populist parties' outsized ability to provoke "Angry" reactions — and anger's mobilizing effect on shares — replicates the affective-amplification dynamics documented in more polarized contexts, suggesting that the mechanisms uncovered elsewhere in this topic are not artifacts of extreme political conflict alone, but structural features of platformed political communication itself.
