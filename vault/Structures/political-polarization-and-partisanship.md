# Political Polarization and Partisanship on Social Media

## Insularity and Partisan News Ecologies: The Italian Baseline

The Italian strand of this literature supplies the conceptual scaffolding for much of what follows. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] establishes, through the 2018 Italian election, that populist-aligned audiences (M5S, the League) cultivate more "insular" news diets than mainstream-party supporters, and—crucially—that insularity does not simply mean silence toward the outside: the Five Star Movement's supporters selectively amplify favorable coverage while mobilizing to contest unfavorable coverage, turning Facebook's comment/share economy into a strategic weapon. This early attention to *how* partisan communities metabolize news, rather than merely *whether* they are exposed to it, anticipates a recurring tension in the corpus between structural insularity and active cross-partisan engagement. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] complements this empirically grounded work with a theoretical reframing of "fake news" itself, arguing—via second-order cybernetics and Chadwick's hybrid media system—that scholars should study propagators and propagation cascades rather than creator intent, since the same content moves through disinformation, misinformation, and re-legitimation cycles depending on who relays it. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] extends this hybrid-systems lens to the infrastructural level, cataloguing how blurred organizational boundaries, microtargeted advertising, and strategic amplification complicate any straightforward measurement of "exposure" in political communication research—a methodological worry that resurfaces throughout the corpus. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] applies a similar logic to science communication, showing that UK newspapers sort scientists holding rival COVID-19 policy views along partisan lines even though the scientists themselves are not fringe figures, suggesting that partisan "use" of expertise, not experts' own bias, drives politicized science coverage. Together with the older case of [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] on the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag, this cluster establishes an early, methodologically pluralistic template—combining network, content, and discursive analysis—that the rest of the collection largely inherits.

## Affective Polarization and Elite Strategy in Latin America

The Brazilian case studies extend this hybrid-media logic to a more acutely polarized, crisis-driven setting. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during the Manaus COVID collapse—opposition legislators mimicking journalistic form to borrow credibility, Bolsonaro allies attacking the press and claiming unmediated state authority, and local Amazonas deputies sidestepping polarization via brokerage narratives. This three-way typology of legitimation strategies dovetails with [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s US-focused account of a "logic of connective faction," in which ideologically extreme elites build symbiotic ties to hyper-partisan media ecosystems that reward and radicalize them—a dynamic visible again in [[Rossini2026-jn]], where belief in Brazilian electoral fraud misinformation, often channeled through messaging apps, measurably deepens political intolerance over the 2022 campaign, reframing misinformation as an amplifier of pre-existing cleavages (antipetismo) rather than a standalone cause. [[Marino2026-he]]'s three-year longitudinal analysis of pro-Bolsonaro Facebook networks pushes back on the assumption that such communities are stable echo chambers: affective engagement toward Bolsonaro and Lula fluctuates sharply around events (the January 2023 coup attempt above all), with rising internal debate and even "brigading" incursions by adversaries complicating the sealed-community picture. Finally, [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents a structural, platform-level version of Brazilian polarization: X's 2024 nationwide ban produced a "sorting ratchet" in which conservative users disproportionately circumvented the ban while liberal users went silent, durably shifting the platform's ideological composition even after the ban was lifted—showing how state-level platform governance can itself become a polarizing force, a theme echoed by [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]]'s work on Community Notes governance more generally.

## German-Language Contexts: Conspiracy, Slant, and Hostile Perceptions

The German-speaking cases sharpen the corpus's attention to media-predisposition interactions. [[Adam2026-tz]]'s panel-plus-web-tracking study of COVID conspiracy beliefs in Germany and German-speaking Switzerland finds both contagion (alternative media exposure fuels belief) and mitigation (mainstream media exposure suppresses it) effects, but shows that populist and mistrustful citizens selectively engage with the former and avoid or "counterargue" against the latter—a finding that formalizes the selective-exposure logic underlying insularity elsewhere in the corpus. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] extends the hostile-media-effect tradition into misinformation perception across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland, showing a robust "hostile misinformation effect" whereby partisans believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by falsehood—an effect amplified by political interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation, and linked to declining media trust. [[Schemer2026-mh]], although US-focused, speaks directly to a methodological concern that pervades comparative European work: using specification-curve analysis across 504 operationalizations of media slant and polarization, it shows that measurement choices (particularly audience- versus content-based partisan-slant scores) drive the *magnitude* though not the *direction* of observed polarization effects, with a persistent conservative-side asymmetry that mirrors debates about partisan misinformation asymmetries found in [[Renault2025-uh]]. Read together, these papers suggest that "the media effect" on polarization is less a fixed quantity than an artifact of predispositions, media ecology, and measurement decisions interacting—an argument that [[Hartmann2025-px]] and [[van-Eck2026-xg]] generalize into full-blown critiques of the field's methodological heterogeneity.

## Echo Chambers and Filter Bubbles: An Unsettled Empirical Core

A substantial cluster interrogates the foundational echo-chamber/filter-bubble hypothesis directly. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the touchstone null(ish) result: on Facebook, individual click choices suppress cross-cutting exposure more than the News Feed algorithm does, complicating strong filter-bubble claims while still documenting asymmetric ideological sorting. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 studies argues that much of the field's disagreement about whether echo chambers even exist stems from incommensurable conceptualizations and operationalizations rather than divergent realities—a diagnosis reinforced by [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s Reddit study, which finds minimal cross-ideological overlap across users, sources, and discourse even among nominally similar subreddits, challenging r/politics's presumed role as a cross-ideological "town square" and reframing polarization as a "sedimented" precondition rather than a measurable outcome. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates the picture further by showing that source-level (domain) partisanship scores mask substantial story-level heterogeneity—individual stories from "moderate" outlets can reach starkly divergent partisan audiences—implying that networked curation, not simple source selection, structures online information diets. [[Brown2026-br]]'s YouTube audit design disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects and finds only mild, largely user-driven echo chambers, strong content "rabbit holes," but no evidence of radicalization pathways, alongside a platform-wide algorithmic tilt toward moderately conservative content. [[Zhu2026-tn]] internationalizes this debate, showing that alternative-mainstream audience overlap in six European countries depends less on fixed "alternativeness" than on media-system type and populist parties' institutional access—with Sweden's isolated alternative enclave emerging specifically from political marginalization rather than platform structure. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] gesture toward similar cross-platform variability, underscoring that no single-platform finding (especially from Twitter/X) can be assumed to generalize.

## Algorithms, Bots, and the Architecture of Amplification

A parallel cluster asks whether platform *design*, rather than user choice, actively manufactures polarization. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X finds that switching to the algorithmic "For You" feed causally shifts users rightward on several issues by promoting conservative and activist content while demoting traditional media—and, tellingly, that these effects persist after the algorithm is switched off because users retain the conservative accounts they were induced to follow, reconciling this finding with Meta's earlier null results. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment during the 2024 US election similarly finds that engagement-based feeds amplify intergroup, moralized, emotional content and distort perceived social *norms* of political dialogue (rather than directly polarizing attitudes)—and shows that a custom "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate these effects without sacrificing platform enjoyment, offering one of the corpus's few interventionist, design-based responses to polarization. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] extends the amplification question to non-human actors, showing social bots functioning as agenda-builders that shape campaign sentiment (especially negative tone) more than press or public discourse, motivating a revision of classical agenda-building theory for human-computer information ecosystems. Kim2026-wg rounds out this cluster with a sobering demonstration that targeted digital ads—including Russian IRA suppression campaigns—produced measurable, geographically and racially concentrated turnout depression in 2016, showing that "average treatment effect" null findings in prior work obscured highly consequential heterogeneous effects.

## Elite Misinformation, Moderation, and the Politics of Fact-Checking

A distinct thread interrogates elite behavior and the governance systems meant to police it. [[Tai2026-qk]] investigates how institutional role and ideology condition elected officials' misinformation sharing, complementing [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s account of factional radicalization. [[Eady2025-vm]] offers a methodological bridge, showing that ideologically extreme legislators—not ordinary citizens—drive the most polarized elite news sharing, and that reduced electoral competition predicts more extreme sharing, linking legislative behavior to electoral incentive structures. On the platform-governance side, [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] jointly interrogate X's Community Notes system: the former shows Republicans are flagged 2.3 times more often than Democrats even under a crowdsourced, cross-partisan-agreement design (undercutting claims of fact-checker bias), while the latter argues this bridging-based design *structurally* undermoderates polarizing content by construction, with acute risks for electoral integrity as the system is exported globally—a concern also voiced in [[Gattermann2025-yx]]'s finding that far-right electoral performance shapes European voters' disinformation concerns. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]'s billion-post diffusion-tree analysis of Facebook during the 2020 US election shows misinformation exhibiting distinctive viral reach concentrated among specific platform actors, while [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows how the very term "fake news" has been weaponized symmetrically by left- and right-wing alternative outlets to redraw Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance—turning a would-be diagnostic term into another instrument of polarization.

## Narrative, Framing, and the Rhetoric of Division

Several papers shift analytic attention from exposure and networks to the discursive and narrative substance of polarized communication. [[Elfes2026-jb]] introduces "narrative polarisation," using an LLM-operationalized Actantial Model on Israeli-Palestinian YouTube content to show that videos propagate highly polarized role-assignments while comments partially harmonize surface narratives—even as deeper "narrative motifs" preserve partisan differences, suggesting polarization operates between narratives as much as between people. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop complementary sensemaking frameworks for US election-integrity rumors: the former shows how quote-tweet framing actions transform even accurate evidence into misleading narratives, while the latter's "deep storytelling" concept demonstrates that 2022 election-fraud rumors relied on audiences filling in meaning from the pre-established 2020 "deep story," making single-post analysis of misinformation analytically insufficient. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes this narrative turn into identity theory, showing that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities are sustained less by platform-specific echo chambers than by cross-platform symbolic boundary work—reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label and converting personal hardship into collective, affectively bonded opposition to a "mainstream other." Methodological contributions from [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] aim to make such framing dynamics tractable at scale via multi-modal and unsupervised computational pipelines, while Gaisbauer2025-px and [[Lee2026-je]] extend the measurement question further—critiquing oversimplified unidimensional partisanship scores and demonstrating, respectively, that news circulation requires multi-level (story/outlet/content) cartography and that LLMs can infer political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical text, raising fresh privacy concerns about the granularity at which partisanship can now be detected.

## Affect, Engagement, and the Feedback Loops of Emotion

A related set of studies zeroes in on the emotional mechanics that convert partisan identity into engagement. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]'s Scandinavian election study shows populist parties disproportionately eliciting "Angry" Facebook reactions that in turn predict more sharing, while "Love" reactions predict less—an asymmetry corroborated by [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review of Facebook Reactions research and by [[Larsson2026-ro]]'s decade-long study of Norway's Progress Party, where negative sentiment posts (especially under Sylvi Listhaug) consistently drive more shares and comments than positive content, even as likes follow an opposite, positivity-driven logic. [[Lieu2025-nl]]'s experimental work on climate misinformation adds a content-specific dimension to this affective picture, showing that attacks on climate *solutions* (rather than the underlying science) are perceived as most credible and most politically polarizing—implying that depolarization and misinformation interventions may need to target specific rhetorical content categories rather than fallacious form alone. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] complicates the causal story further: media narratives about digital election interference in the UK do not increase polarization on average, but *do* produce asymmetric affective polarization among those who see their opposing party benefit—showing that discourse *about* interference can itself become a polarizing vector independent of whether interference campaigns succeed.

## Toward Depolarization and Global Equity: Normative and Meta-Level Reflections

The collection closes on papers that step back from case-specific findings to ask what the field's amassed evidence is actually good for. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s PRISMA review of 89 depolarization studies finds recommendations for reducing polarization are largely disconnected from the empirical evidence researchers themselves produce, resting instead on implicit deliberative-democratic ideals—a warning that resonates with [[Esau2025-tf]]'s theoretical proposal of "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as normative antidotes to "destructive polarization," and with [[Oswald2025-km]]'s observation that the visible, highly produced sliver of online political discourse used to study (and combat) polarization is itself a distorted, unrepresentative "tip of the iceberg" relative to a largely silent public. [[Wang2026-ub]]'s account of academic Twitter's failed migration to Mastodon illustrates a related structural point: even highly motivated, well-connected communities struggle to escape entrenched platform ecologies, suggesting that partisan and professional sorting are sticky properties of platforms as much as of ideology. Finally, [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] issues the collection's most explicit methodological corrective, arguing that polarization research remains disciplinarily fragmented and geographically skewed toward the United States—a critique this very structure note's Italian, German, and Latin American throughlines are implicitly organized to answer, even as the imbalance in available studies (US and Brazil dominating over comparably rich European or Global South cases) shows how much integrative work remains.
