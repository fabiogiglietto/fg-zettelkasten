# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The problem: authenticity as performance, not property

Each paper in this cluster begins from a shared suspicion: that questions like "is this creator sincere?" or "is this account a bot?" or "is this joke cruel?" are the wrong questions to ask of platform behaviour. [[Volpe2026-um]] makes this most explicit by refusing to adjudicate whether Italian second-hand-fashion micro-influencers "really" care about sustainability or are merely branding themselves; instead it treats care-talk and self-branding as *intertwined* responses to the dual precarity of unstable creative labour markets and relentless algorithmic demands for content. [[Graham2026-fb]] pushes the same move onto the human/bot boundary, arguing that the binary distinction between authentic human posting and inauthentic automated posting is analytically unstable, and that what Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon reveals is a spectrum of imitative, community-legible practices rather than a technical fact to be detected. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] extends this logic to civility itself: Filipino Gen Z "dogshows" on TikTok are neither simply cyberbullying nor simply harmless humor but a culturally negotiated performance whose meaning is settled collaboratively by influencers and audiences, within the affordances TikTok offers for spreadable, legal-but-harmful content. Across all three, authenticity, sincerity, and civility are recast as *situated performances* calibrated to platform demands, not stable properties residing in a person's intentions.

## Care and branding as survival strategy

[[Volpe2026-um]] supplies the clearest theoretical vocabulary for this move: micro-influencers do not perform community care *instead of* branding themselves, they perform community care *as* a branding strategy that also happens to reduce the cognitive and emotional cost of constant content production. Second-hand fashion is chosen not for ideological reasons but because it is a familiar, low-cost niche that satisfies platform demands for consistency while generating reputational capital that can be cashed out later as freelance work or a career pivot. This reframes "ethical" content as a survival tactic under conditions of structural precarity — a claim that resonates with [[Graham2026-fb]]'s finding that Reddit "botting," lacking any monetisation or personal-branding infrastructure, is motivated instead by playful in-group signalling within participatory culture. In both cases, the performance is legible and rewarded by a *particular platform's* incentive structure: Instagram/TikTok reward niche consistency and emotional resonance; Reddit rewards meme "freshness" and community-recognised repetition. Authenticity work, in other words, is not free-floating self-expression but a response tailored to what each platform's economy of attention actually pays out.

## Incivility as another currency of participation

[[Cabbuag2024-me]] extends the survival/participation frame into darker territory. If care and imitation can be understood as platform-adapted performances, so too can incivility: "dogshows" function as a form of humor-labour that Gen Z influencers and their audiences co-produce, blurring entertainment, cyberbullying, and hate speech into something collectively "accepted" within a specific cultural and platform context. This paper is important to the arc because it resists treating platform-shaped performance as necessarily benign or merely economically rational — TikTok's affordances actively amplify legal-but-harmful humor, showing that the same logic of culturally negotiated performance that explains ethical branding in [[Volpe2026-um]] and playful mimicry in [[Graham2026-fb]] can also explain normalized cruelty. Taken together, these three papers argue that platform-specific performance is agnostic between "good" and "bad" content: what matters is how legibly a practice satisfies platform-vernacular expectations of participation, not whether it maps onto prior moral categories of sincerity or malice.

## A baseline for what performance has replaced

[[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] and [[Marwick2026-ss]] function less as case studies of platform-specific performance and more as historical/comparative anchors that let the other three papers' claims register as *change* rather than as timeless media logic. Farci's concept of "networked intimacy" on early-2010s Facebook — showing rather than telling, tagging, liking, the expectation of mutual understanding — describes a collaborative, low-stakes performance of closeness that still depended on an audience's cooperative interpretive labour, much as today's care-branding or bot-mimicry depends on community uptake. But Farci's practices were oriented toward friendship maintenance within bounded, semi-private networks, not toward algorithmic visibility or monetisable reputation. Marwick's autoethnographic essay on LiveJournal sharpens this contrast further, arguing that earlier platforms afforded intimacy, vulnerability, and reciprocity precisely because their political economy did not yet demand constant, algorithmically legible output. Read alongside [[Volpe2026-um]], [[Graham2026-fb]], and [[Cabbuag2024-me]], these two papers suggest that what has changed is not the fact that online selves are always performed — Farci's "showing rather than telling" already assumed that — but the *conditions* performance must now satisfy: monetisation, algorithmic amplification, and survival within precarious creative economies, which push performance toward branding, imitation, and even incivility as adaptive strategies.

## Toward a shared analytic vocabulary

Read as a set, these five papers converge on a claim that could anchor future notes in this structure: authenticity, care, and civility online are best understood as *culturally negotiated, platform-specific performances* whose meaning is settled by communities and infrastructures rather than by any essential property of the performer. [[Volpe2026-um]] locates this performance in the branding of ethical care, [[Graham2026-fb]] in the blurred human/bot boundary, and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] in the normalization of harmful humor — three registers of the same underlying dynamic, in which platform affordances and economic precarity jointly determine what performances are survivable or rewarded. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] and [[Marwick2026-ss]] supply the historical depth needed to see this not as an eternal feature of networked life but as an intensification tied to the platform economy's specific demands for scale, virality, and constant output — the very conditions whose absence, in Marwick's telling, once made a different, more reciprocal kind of intimacy possible.
