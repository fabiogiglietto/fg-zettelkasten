# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## Authenticity as Performance, Not Property

The papers gathered here converge on a single reframing: authenticity, care, and civility online are not essential qualities that users either possess or fake, but situated performances negotiated against the specific demands of a platform's economy, algorithm, and audience. [[Volpe2026-um]], [[Graham2026-fb]], and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] form the empirical core of this argument, each taking a practice that looks, on its face, either virtuous (ethical consumption), suspicious (bot-like spam), or malicious (cyberbullying-adjacent humor), and showing that it is better understood as a culturally legible survival strategy shaped by platform affordances. The move across all three is the same: refuse the binary of sincere/insincere or human/automated, and instead ask what work the performance does for users trying to remain visible, legitimate, or safe within a given infrastructure.

## Branding Care: Micro-Influencers and the Economics of Sincerity

[[Volpe2026-um]] anchors this cluster by showing how Italian micro-influencers' second-hand fashion content operates simultaneously as genuine community orientation and as reputational labor — not because creators are cynically performing sustainability, but because care and branding are structurally inseparable under conditions of dual precarity (unstable creative labor markets, relentless algorithmic demands for niche-consistent output). This dovetails with [[Cotter2019-js]], which, despite its different object (platformized tattoo work on Instagram), documents an analogous dynamic: professionals "play the platform's game," reshaping their bodies of practice, workspaces, and unpaid labor time to satisfy algorithmic visibility, blurring the line between professional identity and platform-conditioned self-presentation. [[Divon2026-wx]] extends this logic into the political domain, showing that even ostensibly apolitical lifestyle influencers broker state-aligned narratives during crises as a visibility strategy — nationalism and patriotism become, like Volpe's ethical care, "flexible repertoires of practice" activated for algorithmic survival rather than stable convictions. Across these three, performance of value (ethical, professional, patriotic) is less an expression of belief than a currency traded for visibility.

## Imitation, Botting, and the Human/Machine Blur

[[Graham2026-fb]] pushes the authenticity question to its logical extreme by examining Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon, arguing that the perceived human/bot distinction collapses once posting is understood as a spectrum of imitative, platform-vernacular practices rather than a technical fact. Authenticity here becomes something the community itself negotiates and polices — through upvotes, counter-subreddits, and in-group irony — rather than something verifiable by inspecting an account's substrate. This insight reframes [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s account of "ConspiracyTok," where creators construct legitimacy through populist knowledge production — curated evidence, deep lore, personal testimony — that mimics institutional epistemic authority while explicitly rejecting it. In both cases, what counts as credible or authentic is performed through recognizable genre conventions rather than verified through any ground truth. [[Tang2026-gu]]'s study of Chinese "AI slop" creators radicalizes this further: here, virality itself is reverse-engineered and industrialized through generative AI and matrix-account systems, destabilizing the very notion of an authentic "creator" and making explicit what Graham's and Marwick's subjects do more improvisationally — treating authenticity as a manageable production variable rather than a personal attribute.

## Incivility, Humor, and Shared Platform Vernaculars

[[Cabbuag2024-me]] extends the argument to the register of harm, showing how Filipino Gen Z TikTok "dogshows" normalize legal-but-harmful humor through collaborative negotiation between influencers and audiences — incivility is not simply tolerated but actively co-produced as entertainment, again troubling any fixed line between playful performance and malice. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] offers an instructive parallel from a different political register: in Canadian TikTok immigration discourse, pro- and anti-immigration creators deploy the platform's affordances (humor, non-original audio, community Toks) in symmetrical ways, suggesting a shared vernacular that can carry both inclusive and xenophobic content with equal fluency. Together these studies suggest that TikTok's memetic, performative architecture is affectively and formally agnostic — the same toolkit of humor, mimicry, and audio remix sustains solidarity, hostility, and ambiguous cruelty alike, which is precisely why incivility and care can emerge from structurally identical performative moves.

## The Affective Infrastructure Beneath the Performance

Underlying all of this is a question of how users come to understand and feel the platform conditions they are performing against. [[Bucher2017-qh]] supplies the foundational theoretical vocabulary: the "algorithmic imaginary" describes how users, lacking access to actual code, develop affectively charged beliefs about what algorithms want, and adjust their self-presentation — timing, wording, imagery — accordingly. This is the affective substrate that makes Volpe's niche-caring, Cotter's platform-adapted tattooing, and Marwick's conspiracist intimacy-building legible as responses to a perceived (if not fully knowable) algorithmic demand. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] shows an earlier, pre-TikTok instance of the same logic on Facebook: "networked intimacy" is achieved not through private disclosure but through publicly legible, collaboratively performed practices (tagging, implicit sharing, liking) that depend on an audience's cooperative interpretation — intimacy as choreography rather than confession, long before influencer economies made this explicit. [[Marwick2026-ss]] closes the loop critically, arguing from personal history that earlier platforms like LiveJournal afforded a qualitatively different, less performatively managed intimacy, and that today's extractive, algorithmically optimized environments foreclose vulnerability in favor of exactly the kind of curated, audience-aware performance the other papers document. Read against the collection, Marwick's essay functions as an elegy for what performing authenticity has had to become.

## Structural Conditions: When Platforms Foreclose or Reward Performance

Finally, two papers demonstrate that these dynamics are not limited to individual creators but condition collective and institutional behavior. [[Wang2026-ub]] shows that even a highly motivated cohort of academic Twitter users could not sustain community on Mastodon absent the right structural supports (discoverable profiles, field-specific servers, diverse engagement) — legitimacy and belonging, like influencer authenticity, are platform-specific achievements that do not transfer automatically. [[Lukito2026-il]] shows the same principle operating for right-leaning media outlets, which curate distinct content packages for Twitter versus Truth Social, tailoring self-presentation to each platform's affordances and audience rather than maintaining a single "authentic" institutional voice. Taken together with the influencer- and user-level studies above, these papers suggest that performing authenticity under platform demands is a general condition of contemporary networked life — individual, institutional, human, and bot-adjacent alike — in which legitimacy is less a fixed property than an ongoing, platform-specific negotiation.
