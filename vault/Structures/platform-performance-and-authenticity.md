# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The Question Behind the Question: Is Authenticity Even the Right Frame?

Every paper filed here begins from the same provocation: what looks like sincerity or insincerity, care or exploitation, humanity or automation, civility or cruelty, is not a stable property of the person or the post but an effect of the platform infrastructure that demands and rewards certain performances. [[Volpe2026-um]] opens the set most directly, asking whether Italian micro-influencers' second-hand fashion content reflects "genuine" ethical commitment or strategic branding — and then dissolving the question itself. Care and self-branding, Volpe argues, are not opposed but intertwined responses to a dual precarity of unstable labour markets and relentless algorithmic production demands; second-hand fashion is chosen less for ideological reasons than because it is a familiar, low-cost niche that lets creators sustain visibility without burning out. The ethical narrative is real *and* strategic at once — a survival tactic rather than either fraud or pure conviction.

## Authenticity as Cultural Negotiation, Not Ontology

[[Graham2026-fb]] radicalizes this move by applying it to the human/bot boundary itself. Studying Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" mushroom-emoji spam, the paper shows that a phenomenon widely perceived as a bot invasion was mostly manual, imitative posting embedded in participatory culture — yet whether the accounts were "really" bots mattered less to the community than whether the posts were culturally fresh and fun. Authenticity, Graham argues, is a *culturally negotiated performance* evaluated by the community, not a technical or moral fact about authorship. This is the clearest theoretical statement of what unites the whole cluster: legitimacy is conferred by audiences and vernaculars, not verified by origin.

[[Marwick2025-ov]] extends the same logic to conspiracy content on TikTok. ConspiracyTok creators — disproportionately young, non-White, and/or female, defying the stereotype of the isolated white male conspiracist — build legitimacy through a "populist knowledge production" that mimics journalistic and academic evidentiary form (screenshots, deep lore, curated visual proof) while explicitly rejecting institutional epistemic authority. Their "generous epistemology" is neither simply sincere truth-seeking nor cynical manipulation; it is a genre-specific performance of credibility that can simultaneously voice marginalized standpoints and launder disinformation. Like Volpe's micro-influencers and Graham's Reddit botters, these creators are navigating platform-specific demands for intimacy, evidence, and identity-legibility, and the resulting content cannot be sorted into a clean authentic/inauthentic binary.

## When Performance Curdles into Harm

[[Cabbuag2024-me]] pushes the argument toward its darker edge. Filipino Gen Z TikTok "dogshows" show that the same logic of platform-rewarded performance can normalize "legal-but-harmful" incivility as a shared social practice between influencers and audiences, blurring entertainment, cyberbullying, and hate speech. Read alongside [[Graham2026-fb]] and Marwism2025-ov (sic — [[Marwick2025-ov]]), this suggests a throughline: platform affordances do not merely permit performance, they actively amplify whichever performative register — care, evidentiary conspiracism, or cruelty — best satisfies engagement and in-group signalling. The moral valence of the performance shifts, but the underlying mechanism (audience-conferred legitimacy, algorithmically rewarded intensity) stays constant.

## The Lost Alternative: Intimacy Before the Demand for Performance

Two papers supply historical and comparative counterpoint rather than case studies of platform-driven performance, and together they sharpen what is distinctive about the contemporary moment. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] documents "networked intimacy" on Facebook as a *collaborative* rather than purely individual disclosure practice — showing rather than telling, tagging, liking — that depends on a friendship network's cooperation and that revives community-like qualities of friendship rather than corroding them narcissistically. Crucially, this intimacy is public and performative too, but it is oriented toward relational maintenance rather than algorithmic survival or reputational capital.

[[Marwick2026-ss]] makes the contrast explicit and personal: reflecting on LiveJournal in the early 2000s, Marwick argues that earlier internet infrastructures supported reciprocal, vulnerable, intimate writing that today's extractive, surveillance-driven, virality-optimized platforms actively foreclose. Read against the other five papers, this essay reframes "performing authenticity under platform demands" as a historically specific problem — one produced by particular political-economic choices (advertising, algorithmic optimization, consolidation) rather than an inevitable feature of networked life. It suggests that the very need to strategically perform care, evidence, or edginess that Volpe, Graham, Marwick (2025), and Cabbuag document is itself a symptom of platform infrastructures optimized for scale and profit rather than for the kind of small-scale reciprocity Farci's Facebook users and Marwick's LiveJournal once enjoyed.

## Toward a Shared Argument

Taken together, these six papers refuse to treat authenticity, care, or civility as fixed dispositions that platforms merely reveal or corrupt. Instead they trace a common arc: platform affordances issue demands (for constant content, for evidentiary legitimacy, for engagement-driving humor, for legible intimacy), and users — from Italian fashion micro-influencers to Reddit's fungi-posters to Filipino Gen Z comedians to ConspiracyTok creators to ordinary Facebook friends — respond with culturally specific, community-validated performances that blend sincerity and strategy inseparably. What varies is not the presence of performance but its content and consequence: care work that doubles as reputational labour, imitation that reads as participatory play, evidentiary aesthetics that can radicalize or empower, humor that can bond or brutalize. [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] remind us that this dynamic is neither natural nor unchangeable — it is the residue of infrastructural choices — while the other four papers show, across strikingly different platforms and cultures, just how consistently users have learned to survive, and even thrive, within it.
