# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The Core Triad: Branding Care, Imitating Bots, Performing Incivility

Three papers anchor this topic by each taking a distinct platform-specific performance and refusing to read it as either sincere or cynical. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Italian micro-influencers branding second-hand fashion as community care and ethical advocacy, but argues this moralized self-presentation is inseparable from the mundane economics of content production: care-talk is also reputational labour that lets creators meet algorithmic demands cheaply while building portfolios for precarious creative careers. [[Graham2026-fb]] takes the opposite pole — humans imitating bots rather than bots imitating humans — in its study of Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" mushroom-emoji phenomenon, arguing that the human/bot binary itself collapses under ethnographic scrutiny and that what matters to the community is not ontological status but cultural freshness and in-group signalling. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] completes the triad by examining Filipino Gen Z "dogshows," where legal-but-harmful humor is collaboratively normalized by influencers and audiences as an accepted genre rather than simple cruelty. Read together, these three papers establish the volume's central move: authenticity, bot-ness, and civility are not fixed properties of actors or content but negotiated, community-validated performances shaped by what each platform rewards.

## Platform Vernaculars and the Shared Language of Performance

A second cluster extends this argument into more overtly political terrain, showing that performative strategies travel across ideological and even regime divides because they are anchored in shared platform grammar rather than shared belief. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds that pro- and anti-immigration TikTok creators in Canada deploy the same affordances — trending audio, humor, community hashtags — in symmetrical ways, suggesting a vernacular that cuts across stance. [[Marwick2025-ov]] similarly shows that ConspiracyTok creators, disproportionately young, non-White, and female, borrow the aesthetics of journalism and scholarship (visual evidence, "deep lore," close reading) to construct a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinformation-prone — again a performance of legitimacy keyed to platform form rather than a stable property of truth or deceit. [[Divon2026-wx]] pushes this furthest by showing ostensibly apolitical lifestyle influencers in Israel and China adopting state-aligned "visibility strategies" (glamorization, patriotism, derogation) during crises, arguing that nationalism itself becomes a situational repertoire of practice rather than fixed ideology. Across this cluster, the throughline from the core triad persists: performances of knowledge, patriotism, or stance are survival/participatory strategies calibrated to what makes content visible, not expressions of underlying conviction.

## Labor, Precarity, and the Economics of Performance

Several papers make explicit what is implicit elsewhere: that performing authenticity is often indistinguishable from platform labour. [[Volpe2026-um]] frames micro-influencer care-branding as a response to "dual precarity" in both labour markets and platform infrastructures. [[Cotter2019-js]], examining tattoo artists on Instagram, extends this logic to a profession that predates platformization, arguing that artists must "play the platform's game" — reshaping workspaces, procedures, and unpaid hours — to remain visible, blurring work and leisure in ways structurally similar to influencer self-branding. [[Tang2026-gu]] radicalizes this trajectory by describing Chinese "AI slop" creators who treat virality itself as an engineered, industrialized output via matrix accounts and systematic benchmarking — performance here is stripped of any pretense of spontaneity and becomes pure platform-optimized labour. Together these papers suggest a spectrum from Volpe's affectively-framed care labour to Tang's fully industrialized content production, with authenticity performances occupying variable points of sincerity-labour entanglement depending on how formalized the production process becomes.

## Intimacy, Algorithms, and the Felt Experience of Platforms

Underlying all these performances is a shared infrastructural condition: users act under felt, often opaque algorithmic pressure. [[Bucher2017-qh]]'s concept of the "algorithmic imaginary" supplies the affective groundwork for this topic, showing that Facebook users develop optimization tactics and emotional responses to algorithms they cannot see, and that this imagined relationship recursively shapes both behavior and system. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] offers an earlier, platform-specific precedent for performative negotiation of authenticity, showing how Italian Facebook users collaboratively perform "networked intimacy" through tagging, implicit sharing, and liking — public practices that depend on an audience's cooperative interpretation rather than private disclosure. Read against the TikTok- and Reddit-focused papers, Farci's and Bucher's work suggests that the platform-conditioned performance of authentic selfhood long predates the influencer economy, and that algorithmic opacity itself is a constant generative pressure pushing users toward strategic, legible self-presentation.

## Audience Reception and Folk Theories of Authenticity

A final strand turns from producers to audiences, asking how ordinary users themselves theorize the authenticity of what they consume. [[Li2026-zv]] analyzes Reddit discourse about "AI slop," finding that users construct a fuzzy, contested folk category defined less by technical origin than by a relational "uncanny of provenance" — mirroring [[Graham2026-fb]]'s finding that Reddit communities cared little whether fungi accounts were technically bots, caring instead about cultural resonance. Both papers converge on a methodological point that anchors the whole topic: authenticity judgments are made communally, inconsistently, and often irrespective of ground-truth authorship, reinforcing the collection's overarching argument that performing authenticity under platform demands is a culturally negotiated survival and participatory strategy — alternately care, mimicry, incivility, patriotism, or industrialized content production — rather than a fixed marker of sincerity or malice.
