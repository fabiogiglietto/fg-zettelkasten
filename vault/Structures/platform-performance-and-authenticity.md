# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The Problem with Sincerity as a Metric

A persistent move across this literature is the refusal to ask "is this authentic?" as though authenticity were a binary property waiting to be detected. Instead, each study treats authenticity, care, and civility as *performances calibrated to platform demands* — repertoires that users deploy, negotiate, and are evaluated on by their communities, rather than stable traits of sincerity or deception residing in individuals. [[Graham2026-fb]] states this position most explicitly: its study of "fungi" accounts spamming mushroom emojis on Reddit shows that the human/bot binary itself collapses under ethnographic scrutiny, since community members cared less about the ontological status of an account than about its "freshness" and cultural relevance. Authenticity, the paper argues, is "culturally negotiated" rather than technically verifiable — a claim that could stand as the thesis for the whole topic cluster.

## Care as Branding, Branding as Survival

[[Volpe2026-um]] extends this logic to the register of ethics. Italian micro-influencers who frame second-hand fashion content as community care and sustainability advocacy are not simply dissembling commercial self-interest behind altruistic language, nor are they purely sincere stewards of slow-fashion values. The paper insists that care and self-branding are *intertwined*, not opposed: niche content about second-hand fashion is operationally efficient for meeting algorithmic demands for constant output, and the rhetoric of community curation simultaneously builds reputational capital that creators need to survive dual precarity (unstable labour markets and relentless platform infrastructures). This reframes "ethical" influencer content not as hypocrisy but as a survival strategy — a theme that recurs, in starker form, in [[Tang2026-gu]], where Chinese "AI slop" creators dispense with any pretense of personal expression altogether, engineering virality through benchmarking and matrix-account systems. Read alongside Volpe, Tang's study suggests a spectrum: from care-branding that still gestures at authenticity-as-value, to a fully industrialized creative labour in which the question of sincerity drops out entirely in favor of systematized output. Platform demands, in other words, don't just distort authenticity — they produce new genres of labour in which authenticity-talk is itself a tool.

## Incivility and Legitimacy as Platform Vernaculars

The same logic of culturally negotiated performance appears on the darker end of the spectrum. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] documents how Filipino Gen Z TikTok influencers and audiences co-produce "dogshows" — legal-but-harmful humor that blurs entertainment, cyberbullying, and hate speech — as a *normalized and collaboratively accepted* practice rather than simple malice or platform failure. This resonates with [[Graham2026-fb]]'s point that Reddit's "stopanusfungi" community paradoxically rewarded the very behavior it claimed to oppose: in both cases, communities police and simultaneously indulge boundary-transgressing content, suggesting that incivility and botting alike function as in-group signaling systems whose meaning is settled locally, not by external legal or technical standards.

[[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates this further by showing how legitimacy itself is performed through platform-native evidentiary conventions. ConspiracyTok creators — disproportionately young, non-white, and/or female, against stereotype — build "populist knowledge production" through visual evidence, greenscreen curation, and identity markers (AAVE, natural hairstyles, conspiritual aesthetics) that interpellate specific audiences and manufacture intimacy. This is not fabrication so much as a vernacular epistemology adapted to TikTok's affordances, one that can simultaneously validate marginalized standpoints and launder disinformation. Legitimacy, like authenticity and civility, turns out to be assembled from platform-specific materials rather than possessed outright.

## Collective Identity as a Boundary-Work Performance

[[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shifts the unit of analysis from individual creators to the discursive "we" built in conspiracy communities across six platforms, including TikTok, Reddit, and Facebook. Participants in "Great Reset" discourse reclaim the stigmatized label "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor and transmute personal grievance into collective, affectively bonded identity — a performance of authenticity-as-critical-thinking that does real relational work regardless of its factual content. Notably, Grusauskaite finds this boundary work strikingly *consistent across platforms*, which complicates the emphasis elsewhere in this cluster on platform-specific affordances: it suggests that while the vocabulary of authenticity performance is shaped by platform vernaculars (mushroom emojis, dogshow humor, greenscreen evidence), the underlying sociological function — securing legitimacy and belonging under conditions of precarity, stigma, or institutional distrust — may be more general.

## Older Infrastructures, Different Intimacies

[[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] and [[Marwick2026-ss]] push the inquiry backward in platform history, asking what collaborative, intimacy-building performance looked like before the current demands of algorithmic virality and influencer branding took hold. Farci's Italian Facebook users develop a repertoire of "networked intimacy" — showing rather than telling, tagging, implicit sharing — that depends on the cooperative legibility of a friendship network rather than on performing for an algorithm or an anonymous public. Marwick's autoethnographic account of LiveJournal goes further, arguing that earlier platform architectures actively enabled reciprocal vulnerability and care that today's platforms foreclose. Read against [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Tang2026-gu]], these pieces suggest a historical arc: as platforms shifted from small, filtered, reciprocal networks toward attention-economy infrastructures oriented to virality and monetization, authenticity performance shifted correspondingly — from mutual, low-stakes relational signaling toward branded, labour-intensive, algorithmically legible display. The "survival strategy" framing that dominates the contemporary studies in this cluster is, in this light, itself a symptom of a particular political-economic infrastructure, not a timeless feature of online self-presentation.

## Synthesis

Taken together, these eight papers converge on a shared methodological and theoretical stance: authenticity, care, civility, and legitimacy online are not fixed properties to be sorted into sincere/insincere or human/bot bins, but situated performances whose meaning is settled by platform vernaculars and community evaluation. Whether the register is ethical branding ([[Volpe2026-um]]), imitative posting ([[Graham2026-fb]]), normalized incivility ([[Cabbuag2024-me]]), populist epistemology ([[Marwick2025-ov]]), collective boundary work ([[Grusauskaite2026-po]]), engineered virality ([[Tang2026-gu]]), or older forms of collaborative intimacy ([[Farci2017-2a88c00e]], [[Marwick2026-ss]]), the recurring argument is that these are *platform-specific survival or participatory strategies* — adaptive responses to infrastructural demands, precarity, and stigma — rather than evidence of fixed individual sincerity or malice.
