# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The Performance Imperative

Across this cluster of papers, authenticity emerges not as a psychological state to be verified but as a *practice* — something users do, adapt, and stage in response to the specific affordances and incentive structures of the platforms they inhabit. [[Volpe2026-um]] provides the clearest articulation of this argument: Italian micro-influencers' performances of ethical care around second-hand fashion are neither pure ideological commitment nor cynical marketing, but a survival strategy that fuses reputational labour with genuine community orientation under conditions of dual precarity — unstable creative labour markets and relentless algorithmic demands for content. The paper's refusal to adjudicate sincerity is programmatic for the whole topic: care and branding are "intertwined," and the analytic task is to trace how platform infrastructures reward certain performances of virtue while making others economically unviable.

## Beyond the Authentic/Inauthentic Binary

[[Graham2026-fb]] pushes this logic furthest by dissolving the human/bot distinction itself. The "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon on Reddit shows that what read as an automated invasion was largely human imitation of bot-like repetition, embraced by the very communities that claimed to oppose it. Authenticity here is a *community-negotiated performance* rather than a technical property — Reddit's r/stopanusfungi upvoted the accounts it professed to resist, valuing "freshness" of the meme over ontological status. This resonates with [[Marwick2025-ov]], where ConspiracyTok creators similarly blur boundaries — between evidence and aesthetic, between marginalized standpoint epistemology and disinformation vector — through a "generous epistemology" that mimics academic and journalistic legitimacy while explicitly rejecting institutional authority. Both papers insist that legitimacy is manufactured through recognizable genre conventions (platform vernaculars, visual evidence tropes) rather than verified through correspondence to any underlying truth about the poster's identity or intent.

## Incivility and Toxicity as Platform-Shaped Performance

The same interpretive move — treating apparently negative or transgressive behavior as culturally patterned performance rather than individual pathology — structures [[Cabbuag2024-me]] and [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]]. Filipino Gen Z "dogshows" on TikTok normalize legal-but-harmful humor as a collaborative genre between influencers and audiences, echoing Graham's point that communities actively sustain the very practices they might officially condemn. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] complicates the picture by showing that influencer status itself is an infrastructural mechanism: the same credibility that legitimizes ethical branding in Volpe's account can, when attached to misinformation, convert parasocial trust into collective antagonism against third parties (typically brands) rather than the influencer. Read together, these two papers suggest that the "performance" lens cuts both ways — the same relational capital that lets micro-influencers stage care can, in other platform contexts, launder toxicity or incivility as entertainment.

## A Longer Genealogy: Intimacy as Collaborative Disclosure

[[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] supplies historical depth to this argument, showing that the public, negotiated performance of private life predates the influencer economy. Italian Facebook users' repertoire of "networked intimacy" — showing rather than telling, tagging, expecting mutual understanding — demonstrates that self-disclosure on social platforms has long depended on the cooperative labour of one's network, not on any unmediated baring of the self. This reframes the topic's central claim in continuity rather than rupture: performing selfhood publicly, with an audience's complicity, is a structural feature of networked sociality, not a recent corruption introduced by influencer capitalism or algorithmic feeds.

## The Political-Economic Backdrop

The urgency behind these performative strategies is thrown into relief by three essays that diagnose the platform environment itself as increasingly hostile to reciprocal, low-stakes sociality. [[Boyd2026-op]] argues that "social" media has become "parasocial" media, structurally rewarding one-directional attention to curated influencer content over peer exchange — precisely the economy that makes Volpe's micro-influencers' branding-as-care a rational adaptation and that supplies the asymmetric trust Di-Domenico shows curdling into toxicity. [[Marwick2026-ss]] offers a personal, historical counterpoint, mourning the reciprocity and vulnerability once possible on platforms like LiveJournal and framing today's extractive, surveilled environment as a contingent political-economic outcome rather than technological destiny. [[Wang2026-ub]] extends this diagnosis empirically: even highly motivated academic users failed to sustain a migration to Mastodon, suggesting that alternative infrastructures cannot simply recreate lost sociality once platform incentives, discoverability, and habituated audiences are absent. Together these three papers frame the survival strategies documented elsewhere in the topic not as free cultural invention but as adaptations forced by a narrowing set of platform-sanctioned modes of being visible, cared-for, and legitimate — underscoring the collection's overarching claim that authenticity, care, and incivility alike are performative currencies minted under specific, and specifically constraining, platform conditions.
