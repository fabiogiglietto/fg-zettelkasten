# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## Care, Branding, and the Labour of Survival

The anchor case for this topic is [[Volpe2026-um]], which asks whether Italian micro-influencers' second-hand fashion content reflects genuine ethical commitment or strategic self-branding — and refuses the binary. Volpe shows that care and commerce are not opposed but intertwined: creators frame their activity as community service and sustainability advocacy while that very framing functions as reputational labour that builds legitimacy, trust, and a portfolio for future (often non-influencer) careers. The analytical move is important for the whole topic: authenticity performances are not read as sincere or cynical but as *survival strategies* under dual precarity — unstable labour markets and relentless platform demands for content, niche-coherence, and emotional resonance. This framing sets the terms for every other paper in the set, which can be read as variations on the same question applied to different platforms, genres, and stakes.

[[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] offers a useful genealogical precursor. Writing on Facebook "networked intimacy," Farci shows that even ostensibly private self-disclosure was never simply sincere expression but a *collaborative, public performance* requiring the cooperation of one's network — showing rather than telling, tagging, liking, implicit sharing. Long before influencer economies made branding explicit, ordinary users were already managing authenticity as a relational, audience-dependent accomplishment. Read alongside Volpe, this suggests that "performing authenticity under platform demands" is not a novel pathology of influencer culture but an intensification of practices users have always used to negotiate the public/private boundary — now disciplined by algorithmic visibility requirements and monetisation logics that Facebook-era intimacy did not yet carry.

## Authenticity Beyond the Human/Bot Binary

[[Graham2026-fb]] pushes the argument furthest theoretically. Its case study of Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" mushroom-spam accounts dismantles the assumption that authenticity is a technical property distinguishing humans from bots. Botting, the authors show, is a spectrum of imitative, repetitive posting — manual, semi-automated, fully automated — evaluated by communities not on the basis of *who* or *what* produced it but on its cultural "freshness" and fit with platform vernacular. Crucially, the platform without strong monetisation or personal-branding affordances (unlike Instagram or TikTok) produces a different incentive structure: botting here is playful in-group signalling rather than aspirational labour. This complements Volpe's argument from the opposite direction — where Instagram/TikTok's affordances reward sustained, emotionally resonant authenticity-performance tied to reputation and income, Reddit's architecture rewards imitation and meme-propagation for its own sake. Both are platform-specific negotiations of legitimacy rather than expressions of fixed sincerity or deception.

[[Dehghan2026-sy]] extends this materialist sensibility to a different register: political discourse rather than individual self-presentation. Its finding that cross-ideological interaction on political Reddit is minimal even between discursively similar subreddits, and that moderation regimes visibly shape permissible speech styles, reinforces the broader claim running through this topic — that what looks like organic community behaviour (civility, hostility, identity-formation) is in fact structured by platform and moderation "entanglements." Dehghan's "sedimentation of polarisation" is the macro-level analogue to Graham's micro-level point: performances (of oppositional identity, of civility norms) are pre-structured by infrastructural conditions as much as by individual intent.

## Incivility, Humor, and Normalized Harm on TikTok

[[Cabbuag2024-me]] brings the question of legitimacy to its most ethically fraught case: Filipino Gen Z "dogshows," where influencers and audiences collaboratively normalize legal-but-harmful humor that blurs entertainment, cyberbullying, and hate speech. As with Volpe's care-branding, incivility here is not simply individual malice but a *negotiated, culturally specific practice* amplified by TikTok's affordances — a vernacular performance whose acceptability is continually tested and ratified by audience response, much as Graham's Reddit communities playfully indulge rather than police bot-like behaviour. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates this picture by showing how performative legitimacy is constructed discursively rather than just behaviourally: ConspiracyTok creators — disproportionately young, non-White, and/or female — build "populist knowledge production" through curated visual evidence, identity markers, and intimacy cues, rejecting institutional epistemic authority while mimicking its forms. The "generous epistemology" Marwick describes functions analogously to Volpe's care-branding: a genuinely resonant, sometimes empowering practice that is simultaneously a vector for harm, irreducible to a sincerity/manipulation binary.

[[Hollingshead2026-vx]] adds an important qualifier: TikTok's affordances are deployed with striking symmetry across ideological positions on immigration discourse, suggesting a shared platform vernacular — humor, non-original audio, community Toks — that enables both inclusive and xenophobic performances using the same toolkit. This resonates with the topic's central claim: the affordances themselves are morally neutral infrastructures that different communities inflect toward care, incivility, conspiracy, or solidarity depending on genre norms and audience expectations, not fixed technical determinism.

## Visibility as Currency: Brokerage and Engineered Virality

[[Divon2026-wx]] generalizes the "survival strategy" logic to political crisis contexts, showing how ostensibly apolitical lifestyle influencers on TikTok and Douyin become "brokers of the state," mobilizing patriotism and glamorization as *visibility resources* rather than stable ideological commitments. This reframes nationalism itself as a performative repertoire activated situationally — directly paralleling Volpe's and Graham's insistence that performed positions (care, patriotism, bot-identity) are tactical responses to platform-conditioned visibility games rather than expressions of fixed belief. [[Tang2026-gu]] pushes this logic to its most industrialized extreme: Chinese "AI slop" creators who reverse-engineer viral formats and run matrix-account systems treat virality itself as an engineerable output, dissolving any remaining notion of spontaneous or sincere creative authorship into systematized production labour.

## The Shadow of the Old Internet

[[Marwick2026-ss]] serves as a reflective counterpoint to the entire cluster. Marwick's autoethnographic account of LiveJournal-era intimacy argues that earlier, smaller-scale infrastructures enabled vulnerability and reciprocity that today's platforms structurally foreclose — not because users have become less sincere, but because political-economic design choices (surveillance, virality optimization, scale) changed what performances are rewarded. Read against the rest of the topic, this essay supplies the historical baseline against which Volpe's branded care, Graham's imitative botting, Cabbuag's normalized incivility, and Divon's and Tang's engineered visibility can be understood not as decline from some prior authentic state, but as the latest iteration of users continually re-negotiating legitimacy, intimacy, and care within whatever infrastructural demands confront them. Together, these papers argue that authenticity online was never a fixed property to begin with — only ever a culturally and infrastructurally contingent accomplishment, whose terms shift with each new platform regime.
