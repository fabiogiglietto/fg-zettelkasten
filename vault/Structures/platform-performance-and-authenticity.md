# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The Problem of Authenticity as Performance, Not Property

The papers gathered here converge on a single methodological and theoretical wager: authenticity, care, and legitimacy online are not essential qualities that users either possess or lack, but situated performances negotiated against the specific affordances, incentive structures, and moral economies of particular platforms. Rather than asking whether a micro-influencer's sustainability talk is "real," whether a Reddit poster is "actually" a bot, or whether a TikTok joke is "genuinely" malicious, this literature asks what work such performances do for users trying to survive, gain visibility, or claim legitimacy within infrastructures that were never built with their flourishing in mind.

## Care as Branding, Branding as Care

[[Volpe2026-um]] offers the clearest articulation of this logic: Italian micro-influencers frame second-hand fashion content as community care, sustainability advocacy, and curatorial expertise, but this framing is simultaneously reputational labour responding to dual precarity — unstable creative labour markets and relentless platform demands for algorithmically legible, niche-consistent output. Volpe's insight that care and self-branding are "intertwined" rather than opposed reframes a question that often gets asked in binary moralizing terms (sincere vs. exploitative) as a question about survival strategy. This same logic of performed legitimacy recurs, in a very different register, in [[Divon2026-wx]], where lifestyle and fitness influencers in Israel and China become situational "political brokers" during crises, mobilizing state-aligned narratives not out of settled ideological conviction but because the state temporarily becomes a resource for sustaining visibility. Nationalism and patriotism, Divon argues, function as repertoires activated for strategic advantage rather than stable belief — a structural analogue to Volpe's creators activating "care" as a repertoire for algorithmic survival. [[Tang2026-gu]] pushes this logic to its most industrialized extreme: Chinese "AI slop" creators treat virality itself as an engineerable output, formalizing benchmarking, format-testing, and matrix-account production into a systematized labour process that further dissolves any stable line between authored creativity and manufactured content.

## Imitation, Incivility, and the Collapse of Human/Machine, Sincere/Malicious Binaries

[[Graham2026-fb]] extends this performative framing to the question of bot authenticity itself. Analyzing Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" mushroom-emoji phenomenon, the paper shows that the human/bot binary is analytically unstable: most participation was human-driven, imitative, and playfully in-group, yet communities treated the ontological status of posters as secondary to cultural "freshness" and meme relevance. Authenticity here is a community-evaluated norm, not a technical fact — a conclusion that directly echoes Volpe's point that what matters is less some essential truth about motive than how a performance is received and legitimated by its audience. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] locates a structurally similar dynamic in TikTok's Filipino "dogshow" phenomenon, where Gen Z influencers and audiences collaboratively normalize legal-but-harmful humour that blurs entertainment, cyberbullying, and hate speech; incivility, like botting and care-branding, is a negotiated, culturally specific practice shaped by platform affordances rather than a fixed property of malicious intent. [[Marwick2025-ov]] rounds out this cluster by showing how ConspiracyTok creators — disproportionately young, non-White, and/or female — construct epistemic legitimacy through "populist knowledge production," borrowing the visual grammar of journalism and scholarship while rejecting institutional authority, in a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering for marginalized standpoints and a vector for disinformation. Across Volpe, Graham, Cabbuag, and Marwick2025-ov, the same move recurs: practices that look, from outside, like sincerity, deception, hostility, or credibility are better understood as platform-specific vernaculars of participation.

## Collective Identity as Negotiated Boundary Work

A related strand treats the *collective* rather than individual dimension of this negotiation. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows how "Great Reset" conspiracy communities across six platforms perform symbolic boundary work, reclaiming the stigmatized "conspiracy theorist" label and converting personal hardship into shared "deep stories" that bind an otherwise ideologically diverse diagonal coalition. This is authenticity-work at the level of group identity: legitimacy is produced interactionally, through affective solidarity and boundary drawing against a "mainstream other," rather than delivered by content or platform architecture alone. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offers an earlier, narrower instance of the same dynamic — the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie hashtag as a site where dissenting users had to construct discursive strategies to perform legitimate disagreement without being read as endorsing violence, illustrating how even brief, crisis-driven hashtag practices require careful management of perceived sincerity and moral standing. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]'s account of "networked intimacy" on Facebook — showing, tagging, implicit sharing, liking — demonstrates that even ordinary relational maintenance requires collaborative, audience-dependent performance work to manage the public/private boundary, suggesting that performed authenticity is not confined to influencer or political contexts but is a general condition of networked sociality.

## Platforms as Moral Arbiters

If users' performances are shaped by platform demands, several papers examine how platforms themselves produce and police the authenticity/manipulation boundary. [[Petre2019-zh]] is the pivot point here: platforms and press discourse construct "gaming the system" as a moralized category — using metaphors of organic growth, crime, and cheating — that legitimates platform power while obscuring that the boundary between legitimate strategy and illegitimate manipulation is nebulous and serves platforms' material interests. Crucially, platforms valorize their own automation and scale while condemning identical practices in creators, a double standard Petre calls "platform paternalism." This directly illuminates why Volpe's creators must *perform* care rather than simply monetize visibility outright, why Graham's communities treat bot/human distinctions as culturally negotiable rather than policeable, and why Tang's AI slop creators must systematize virality discreetly rather than openly admit to "gaming" it. [[Bucher2017-qh]]'s concept of the "algorithmic imaginary" supplies the affective substrate beneath these negotiations: users' feelings about what algorithms want, reward, and punish are themselves generative, shaping the optimization tactics, caution, and self-presentation strategies that produce exactly the kind of culturally legible authenticity performances this literature documents. Reading Bucher alongside Petre clarifies that performed authenticity is not simply a user-side adaptation but a recursive loop between platform moralizing and user response.

## The Longer Arc: From Reciprocal Sociality to Parasocial Performance

Finally, a trio of reflective, essayistic pieces situates these case studies within a longer historical arc. [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Boyd2026-op]], both written for *Social Media + Society*'s anniversary issue, argue that the "social" in social media has been displaced by extractive, scroll-oriented, influencer-dominated architectures that reward parasocial attention over reciprocal exchange — a transformation both authors insist was contingent on political-economic choices, not technological inevitability. Read against the rest of this corpus, these essays supply the macro-level condition that makes the micro-level performances (Volpe's care-branding, Graham's botting, Cabbuag's dogshows, Divon's brokerage, Tang's engineered virality) necessary in the first place: if platforms no longer reward authentic reciprocity but instead reward algorithmically legible, emotionally resonant, high-frequency content, then performing care, legitimacy, intimacy, or edginess becomes a rational — even for Petre's embattled creators, defensive — survival strategy rather than evidence of personal insincerity. Farci's 2017 study of networked intimacy, written before this "parasocial" shift fully crystallized, can be read as a snapshot of the reciprocal, audience-collaborative intimacy that Marwick and boyd argue has since been crowded out by platform consolidation and algorithmic curation.

## Synthesis

Taken together, these fourteen papers trace an arc from historical loss (the reciprocal, intimate "old internet" of Marwick and boyd) through present-day adaptation (Volpe's care-branding, Graham's imitative botting, Cabbuag's normalized incivility, Divon's political brokerage, Marwick2025-ov's populist epistemology, Tang's engineered virality) to the structural and discursive conditions that make such adaptations necessary (Petre's platform paternalism, Bucher's algorithmic imaginary, Cotter's visibility game) and the collective identity-work that sustains them (Grusauskaite, Giglietto, Farci). The throughline is a refusal of binary judgment — sincere/strategic, human/bot, civil/uncivil, authentic/manipulative — in favour of a culturally and infrastructurally situated account of how users perform legitimacy under conditions not of their own making.
