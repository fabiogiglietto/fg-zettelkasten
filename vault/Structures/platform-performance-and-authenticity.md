# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

These three papers, spanning fashion micro-influencing, Reddit meme culture, and Filipino TikTok humor, converge on a single methodological and theoretical wager: that authenticity, sincerity, and even civility are not intrinsic properties of an actor's intent but negotiated performances that emerge from the friction between platform affordances and cultural practice. Each paper resists a moralizing binary — genuine vs. strategic, human vs. bot, humor vs. harm — in favour of describing how users *do things* with these categories to survive, belong, or gain standing within a specific platform ecology.

### Care as Strategy, Strategy as Care

[[Volpe2026-um]] opens the set by dismantling the opposition between ethical commitment and self-branding. Italian micro-influencers curating second-hand fashion content are shown to select this niche less from ideological conviction than because it is operationally efficient — a "low-cost" content vein that satisfies algorithmic demands for constant, emotionally resonant posting. Yet the paper refuses to read this as cynical: care and sustainability narratives are not covering stories for branding but are *constitutively entangled* with it, both functioning as responses to the dual precarity of unstable creative labour markets and relentless platform infrastructures. Authenticity here is less a property of the influencer's inner state than an effect of performing consistency, niche expertise, and community service under conditions of reputational competition. Crucially, most participants do not even claim influencer identity; they treat visibility as a portfolio-building strategy toward more stable careers, reframing platform performance as professional survival rather than aspirational fame-seeking.

### Authenticity as a Culturally Negotiated Property, Not a Technical One

[[Graham2026-fb]] extends this deconstruction from *ethical* authenticity to *ontological* authenticity — the human/bot divide. Through the case of Reddit's mushroom-emoji "fungi" phenomenon, the paper shows that a seemingly automated invasion was in fact predominantly human imitation, spreading through in-group signalling and playful performance of bot-like repetition. What matters to the Reddit community is not whether an account is "really" a bot but whether its behaviour is culturally legible and "fresh" enough to sustain the meme's participatory life. This directly parallels Volpe's finding that Instagram audiences respond to the *performance* of care rather than verifying its sincerity: in both cases, authenticity functions as a communally policed vernacular rather than a fixed technical or psychological fact. Graham's argument that "botting" is best understood as a spectrum of imitative practices shaped by platform vernaculars, rather than a deceptive technology, generalizes Volpe's insight that self-branding and care performances are graded, context-sensitive negotiations rather than binary states of sincerity or fraud. Both papers argue that governance or judgment of these practices (whether ethical scrutiny of influencers or bot-detection systems) fails when it seeks essentialist categories instead of attending to community norms and platform-specific incentive structures.

### From Sincerity to Civility: Extending the Argument to Harm

[[Cabbuag2024-me]] pushes the same logic into ethically fraught territory, examining how TikTok's "dogshow" phenomenon among Filipino Gen Z influencers normalizes "legal-but-harmful" humor that blurs cyberbullying, hate speech, and entertainment. Here the negotiated-performance framework is tested against its limits: unlike Volpe's ethical branding or Graham's playful mimicry, dogshows involve audiences and influencers *collaboratively* producing incivility as an accepted social practice, amplified by TikTok's specific affordances for rapid, humor-coded virality. The paper implicitly asks whether the same interpretive generosity — reading performance as survival strategy or participatory culture rather than fixed malice — can or should be extended to incivility. Its answer is cautious: it affirms the shared theoretical move (incivility as *performed and negotiated*, not simply given) while flagging that platform-specific amplification can turn negotiated performance into harm, particularly for non-Western creator communities operating under distinct, understudied moderation regimes.

### The Arc: Performance as Platform-Specific Survival

Read together, the three papers trace an arc from benign to troubling instantiations of the same underlying claim: that platform demands force users into performative repertoires — of care, of humanness, of civility — that are legible only within particular cultural and infrastructural contexts. [[Volpe2026-um]] establishes performance as adaptive labour under precarity; [[Graham2026-fb]] shows how the same logic destabilizes ontological categories (human/bot) that underpin assumptions about sincerity; and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] demonstrates how these negotiated performances can shade into normalized harm when amplified by affordances rewarding humor and virality over care. Collectively, they argue against treating authenticity, automation, or civility as fixed moral or technical properties, insisting instead that these are effects of ongoing negotiations between users, audiences, and the platforms that structure their visibility, livelihood, and belonging.
