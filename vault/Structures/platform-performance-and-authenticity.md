# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The common thread: authenticity as performance, not essence

Across these five papers, a single argument recurs in different registers: authenticity, care, sincerity, and even "bot-ness" are not fixed properties of a person or a post but situated performances calibrated to the demands of a specific platform. [[Volpe2026-um]], [[Graham2026-fb]], and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] each take a practice that looks, on its surface, either suspiciously commercial, suspiciously fake, or straightforwardly malicious — ethical influencer branding, repetitive "bot-like" posting, and normalized incivility — and argue that the binary of authentic/inauthentic or benign/malicious misses what is actually happening: users are negotiating legitimacy within culturally and infrastructurally specific vernaculars. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] and [[Marwick2026-ss]] extend this logic backward in time and inward toward intimacy, showing that even "private" self-disclosure has always been a collaborative, audience-dependent performance, and that the platform infrastructure itself determines whether such performances can register as sincere at all.

## Care and ethics as branding under precarity

[[Volpe2026-um]] offers the clearest articulation of the "survival strategy" thesis: Italian micro-influencers frame second-hand fashion content as community care, sustainability advocacy, and ethical curation, yet this framing is inseparable from the reputational labour needed to survive algorithmic demands for constant, niche-consistent output. Volpe's move is to refuse the question "is this genuine or branded?" and instead show that care and branding are structurally intertwined responses to dual precarity — unstable creative labour markets and relentless platform infrastructures. The ethical performance is not a mask over commercial intent; it *is* the strategy by which creators generate trust, visibility, and eventually exit routes into more stable work. This reframes "authenticity" not as a psychological state but as a currency minted under specific algorithmic conditions.

## Imitation, repetition, and the bot/human boundary

[[Graham2026-fb]] pushes the same logic into a different register: rather than care-branding, it examines repetitive, apparently automated posting (Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" mushroom-emoji phenomenon) and shows that what reads as bot invasion is largely human imitative participation, sustained by in-group signalling rather than fame-seeking or monetisation — because Reddit, unlike Instagram or TikTok, lacks personal-branding affordances. This is a crucial counterpoint to Volpe: on a platform without visibility labour incentives, performative repetition serves playful communal belonging rather than reputational capital. Graham's central claim — that authenticity is "culturally negotiated" and community-evaluated rather than an ontological fact about human versus machine authorship — generalizes the structure note's organizing argument: the question is never "is this real?" but "what cultural and infrastructural logic makes this legible as acceptable participation?"

## Incivility as platform-shaped performance

[[Cabbuag2024-me]] applies the same reframing to behaviour usually coded as straightforwardly harmful. Filipino Gen Z TikTok "dogshows" involve influencers and audiences co-producing legal-but-harmful humour that blurs entertainment, cyberbullying, and hate speech. Rather than treating this as individual malice, the paper (echoing Matamoros-Fernández et al.'s "legal-but-harmful" framework) shows incivility being normalized as a *collaborative, culturally specific social practice* amplified by TikTok's affordances. Read alongside [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Graham2026-fb]], this suggests a shared analytic move across the topic: performances that look ethically troubling (care-washing, bot-mimicry, cruelty-as-comedy) are better understood as platform-specific survival or participatory strategies, legible within their own cultural economy, than as expressions of fixed sincerity or villainy.

## Intimacy as collaborative disclosure — and its platform conditions

[[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] supplies the foundational mechanism underlying all these performances: on Facebook, intimacy itself is never purely private but achieved through *collaborative disclosure* — showing rather than telling, tagging, implicit sharing, liking — practices that depend on friends' cooperation to encode and decode meaning within public space. This paper demonstrates that the blurring of public/private, sincere/staged is not a recent platform pathology but a structural feature of networked self-presentation as such, predating influencer economies and bot cultures alike. It supplies the theoretical scaffolding (collaborative, audience-dependent performance of closeness) that Volpe's "performed care" and Graham's "performed participation" later specialize into commercial and participatory-culture variants respectively.

[[Marwick2026-ss]] closes the arc with a historicizing and normative counterpoint. Marwick's autoethnographic reflection on LiveJournal argues that earlier platform infrastructures *supported* the kind of reciprocal, vulnerable intimacy that Farci describes, whereas contemporary platforms' political economy of surveillance, scale, and algorithmic optimization forecloses it — pushing users toward the more defensive, strategic, and performative modes documented in Volpe, Graham, and Cabbuag. Where the other four papers analyze how users adapt and perform legitimacy *within* current platform demands, Marwick asks what has been lost in that adaptation, and insists that the current landscape of performed authenticity is a contingent product of design and governance choices, not a technological or even a human inevitability.

## The arc of the topic

Together these papers trace a progression: from the basic infrastructural fact that online intimacy and disclosure are always collaboratively and publicly performed ([[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]), through contemporary case studies of how specific platform affordances shape what performances are rewarded — ethical branding on Instagram/TikTok ([[Volpe2026-um]]), imitative participation on Reddit ([[Graham2026-fb]]), normalized incivility on TikTok ([[Cabbuag2024-me]]) — to a reflective, critical vantage point asking whether the platforms themselves could be redesigned to make more humane, less strategically defensive performances possible again ([[Marwick2026-ss]]). The throughline is a refusal to adjudicate sincerity or malice as fixed properties of users, insisting instead that care, authenticity, bot-ness, and civility are all culturally and infrastructurally produced — and that understanding them requires attending closely to the specific platform demands users are negotiating.
