# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The performative turn: authenticity as strategy, not essence

The anchor claim threading this topic is that authenticity, care, and civility online are not fixed dispositions of users but situated performances calibrated to what a given platform rewards. [[Volpe2026-um]] makes this explicit: Italian micro-influencers' second-hand fashion content reads as ethical advocacy and community care, but Volpe reframes it as a survival strategy against dual precarity — unstable creative labour markets and the relentless algorithmic demand for niche-consistent, emotionally resonant content. Care and self-branding are not opposites here but a single adaptive repertoire. [[Graham2026-fb]] extends the same logic to a very different register: Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" mushroom-emoji spam is neither simply human nor simply bot, and the paper argues that the entire human/bot binary is the wrong lens. What matters to the community is not the ontological status of the poster but whether the repetition is culturally "fresh" and legible as in-group play — authenticity is adjudicated collectively, not detected technically. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] pushes the argument into darker territory, showing how Filipino Gen Z TikTok "dogshows" normalize legal-but-harmful humor as an accepted, collaboratively negotiated register between influencers and audiences, blurring entertainment and cyberbullying. Read together, these three papers establish the topic's thesis: performative practices that look variously virtuous, bot-like, or cruel are better understood as platform-specific survival or participatory strategies than as evidence of sincerity or malice.

## Infrastructures that script the performance

A second cluster explains *why* these performances take the shape they do by foregrounding platform architecture itself. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes TikTok's shift from "networked" to "clustered" publics — audiences assembled by algorithmic inference of watch-time rather than by explicit social ties — which helps explain why Volpe's micro-influencers and Cabbuag's dogshow creators must perform legibly to an anonymous, interest-based crowd rather than a known social network. [[Bucher2017-qh]]'s "algorithmic imaginary" supplies the affective mechanism underlying this adaptation: users do not need to know how an algorithm works to feel its judgments, and they develop folk theories and tactics (timing, phrasing, image choice) to be recognized favorably by it — the same optimizing logic that drives micro-influencers' niche consistency and Reddit botters' imitative posting. [[Cotter2019-js]], despite its ostensible subject of tattoo artists, makes a structurally parallel argument about Instagram as a non-human agent that "makes do" with users' labour, forcing even pre-digital professions into platformized, algorithm-feeding routines — a labour-precarity story that rhymes closely with Volpe's micro-influencers treating visibility as reputational capital rather than an end in itself. Together these three papers supply the infrastructural vocabulary — clustered publics, algorithmic imaginaries, the visibility game — for understanding performance as something co-produced with the platform, not staged against a neutral backdrop.

## Negotiating legitimacy with and before an audience

A third strand examines how audiences themselves participate in authenticating or de-authenticating these performances, extending the negotiation beyond the performer. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] offers an important precursor logic from Facebook: "networked intimacy" is achieved collaboratively, requiring the cooperation of one's friend network through tagging, implicit sharing, and liking — intimacy, like care or authenticity, is never a solitary accomplishment but a publicly brokered one. [[Wedel2026-ix]] shows audiences applying a similarly negotiated standard to judge influencers' informativeness: expertise and topical focus, not institutional credentials, confer legitimacy, and platform context (TikTok as "guilty pleasure" versus YouTube as self-selected) recalibrates what counts as trustworthy. This audience-side lens clarifies why Graham's Reddit community can tolerate and even upvote "fungi" accounts in the very subreddit organized to oppose them: legitimacy is conferred through communal enjoyment and recognition, not rule violation per se.

## Scaling up: performance as political and infrastructural phenomenon

The topic's final arc extends the performativity thesis beyond individual creators to political and systemic registers, testing the limits of "survival strategy" as an explanatory frame. [[Divon2026-wx]] documents influencers — including ostensibly apolitical fitness and lifestyle creators — brokering state-aligned narratives during crises in Israel and China, treating "the state" itself as a strategic visibility resource; nationalism here functions as a situational repertoire rather than stable belief, directly echoing this topic's refusal to treat performed sincerity as fixed character. [[Lukito2026-il]] shows an analogous logic at the institutional level, with right-leaning media outlets curating distinct content packages for Twitter versus Truth Social, confirming that platform-specific self-presentation is a strategic adaptation that operates at organizational as well as individual scale. [[Tuters2026-vo]] supplies a cautionary complement: it shows how patterned, platform-structured circulation (the "Memeplex") can be misread by *audiences* as evidence of coherent intentional coordination — the mirror image of the dynamic under study here, in which what is actually distributed, infrastructurally shaped behavior gets collapsed into judgments of sincerity or conspiracy. Taken together, this expanding arc — from micro-influencers curating care, to Reddit's ambiguous bots, to TikTok's normalized incivility, to political brokerage and cross-platform curation — traces a consistent argument: what looks like individual authenticity, deception, or malice is, across contexts, better explained as the patterned residue of users and institutions learning to survive, participate in, and be legible within platform-specific demands.
