# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The Performance Problem: Why "Authentic" and "Fake" Are the Wrong Categories

Across these six papers, a shared analytical move recurs: each refuses the binary opposition between sincere/authentic behaviour and calculated/inauthentic performance, replacing it with an account of situated, platform-shaped negotiation. Whether the object is a micro-influencer's sustainability narrative, a Reddit account's mushroom-emoji spam, or a TikTok teenager's cruel joke, the papers converge on treating authenticity, care, and civility not as fixed dispositions but as culturally legible performances that users learn to produce because platforms demand them.

[[Volpe2026-um]] makes this case most explicitly for the influencer economy: Italian second-hand-fashion creators frame their content as ethical advocacy and community care, but this framing is inseparable from the "reputational labour" needed to survive algorithmic visibility regimes. The paper's key move is refusing to ask whether the care is "real"—instead it shows how care-talk and self-branding are structurally fused, both responses to the dual precarity of gig-style creative labour and relentless platform production demands. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] extends this logic to a very different affective register: Filipino Gen Z TikTok "dogshows" normalize legal-but-harmful humor as a collaboratively negotiated practice between influencers and audiences, not simply cruelty or trolling but a culturally specific genre that platform affordances actively amplify. [[Marwick2025-ov]] adds a third variant—conspiracy TikTok creators borrow academic and journalistic aesthetics ("populist knowledge production") to perform evidentiary legitimacy, constructing intimacy and trust with viewers through identity markers and curated visual "proof." In all three cases, what looks like a fixed moral property (caring, civil, truthful) turns out to be a genre-specific performance calibrated to what the platform rewards.

## Authenticity as Cultural Negotiation, Not Ontological Fact

[[Graham2026-fb]] pushes this argument to its most abstract and provocative point, arguing that the human/bot distinction itself is analytically unstable. Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon—read by outsiders as a bot invasion—turns out to be largely human imitative posting embedded in participatory culture; crucially, the community itself treats the question of bot-vs-human as secondary to whether the practice is culturally "fresh" or relevant. This directly generalizes the logic underlying [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]]: authenticity is something audiences *evaluate and confer* through community norms, not a property intrinsic to the poster or the content. Where micro-influencers are judged by whether their care performance feels appropriately niche and consistent, and conspiracy creators are judged by whether their evidentiary style feels sufficiently "researched," Reddit users judge fungi-posting by its meme-cultural timing—all are instances of what [[Graham2026-fb]] calls a "culturally negotiated performance," evaluated against platform vernaculars rather than against a sincerity/insincerity axis.

## Platform Affordances as the Shaping Force

What ties the case studies together is the insistence that these performances are not free-floating cultural choices but responses to specific infrastructural demands: Instagram/TikTok's algorithmic appetite for constant, niche-consistent content ([[Volpe2026-um]]); TikTok's reward structure for provocative, high-engagement humor ([[Cabbuag2024-me]]) and for visually "evidenced" intimacy ([[Marwick2025-ov]]); Reddit's lack of monetization affordances, which redirects botting toward playful in-group signalling rather than fame-seeking ([[Graham2026-fb]]). The comparison across platforms is itself analytically productive: the same underlying tension—how do users produce legible, legitimate selves under infrastructural constraint—yields different performative genres depending on what each platform rewards and permits.

[[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] offers an earlier, Facebook-specific precedent for this line of thought, showing that intimacy itself is achieved through public, collaborative, network-dependent practices (tagging, implicit sharing, liking) rather than private disclosure. Although older and focused on friendship rather than influencer labour or incivility, it establishes the foundational insight this cluster builds on: that platform affordances reconfigure the public/private boundary such that even the most intimate-seeming acts are performances legible only within a networked, audience-dependent frame. The throughline from Facebook tagging to TikTok evidentiary aesthetics to Reddit meme-posting is the same: legitimacy and closeness are produced relationally and infrastructurally, not simply expressed.

## A Counterpoint: What Is Lost When Everything Becomes Performance

[[Marwick2026-ss]] stands somewhat apart from the other five, functioning less as an empirical case study and more as an elegiac counterpoint that supplies the cluster's implicit stakes. Marwick's autoethnographic account of LiveJournal argues that earlier internet infrastructures enabled *unperformed* vulnerability and reciprocity—intimacy that did not need to be strategically produced for algorithmic legibility—and that today's platforms structurally foreclose this in favor of surveillance and extraction. Read alongside the other papers, this essay reframes the entire cluster's argument: the survival strategies, imitative postings, and normalized incivilities documented elsewhere are not timeless features of human sociality online but historically specific adaptations to a particular, contingent political economy of platforms. Where [[Volpe2026-um]], [[Cabbuag2024-me]], [[Marwick2025-ov]], and [[Graham2026-fb]] treat performative negotiation as a savvy, even admirable, form of agency under constraint, [[Marwick2026-ss]] asks what has been foreclosed by a world in which such constant performance is necessary at all—gesturing toward alternative, smaller-scale infrastructures where care and authenticity might not require branding, imitation, or amplification to be legible.

Taken together, the arc of this topic moves from documenting specific performative genres (care-branding, bot-imitation, dogshow humor, populist epistemology, networked intimacy) toward a shared theoretical claim—that authenticity and civility are platform-contingent, culturally negotiated survival strategies—before closing with a reflective challenge to that very framework, asking whether the naturalization of performance-as-survival forecloses other, less extractive ways of being together online.
