# Performing Authenticity Under Platform Demands

## The authenticity problem reframed

The organizing move across this set of papers is to refuse the question "is this performance sincere or manipulative?" and replace it with "what work does this performance do, given the platform's demands?" [[Volpe2026-um]], [[Graham2026-fb]], and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] form the empirical core: Italian micro-influencers branding second-hand fashion as ethical care, Reddit users imitating bot behaviour in the "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon, and Filipino Gen Z TikTok influencers normalizing "dogshow" incivility. None of these are read as revealing some underlying truth about users' character — greedy self-promoters versus genuine community carers, malicious automatons versus innocent humans, bullies versus comedians. Instead, each paper treats the performance itself as the unit of analysis, asking what cultural and infrastructural conditions make it legible, rewarded, or survivable. [[Graham2026-fb]] states this most explicitly, arguing that "authenticity online is a culturally performed and community-evaluated norm rather than an essential property," a claim that could equally caption [[Volpe2026-um]]'s micro-influencers or [[Cabbuag2024-me]]'s dogshow participants.

## Care, mimicry, and incivility as platform-specific survival strategies

[[Volpe2026-um]] shows how micro-influencers select second-hand fashion not from ideological commitment but because it is a low-cost, familiar niche that satisfies Instagram and TikTok's demand for constant, emotionally resonant output; the resulting discourse of sustainability and community care is simultaneously sincere-feeling and strategically reputational, a response to dual precarity in labour markets and platform infrastructures. [[Graham2026-fb]] extends this logic to a platform with no monetization affordances: on Reddit, where personal branding carries little payoff, repetitive "botting" becomes playful in-group signalling rather than fame-seeking, showing that the same imitative, repetitive posting logic that reads as inauthentic manipulation on Instagram reads as participatory culture on Reddit — the platform's affordance structure, not the behaviour itself, determines its meaning. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] pushes the argument into ethically starker territory: legal-but-harmful humour on TikTok is "performed, accepted, and negotiated" by both influencers and audiences, such that incivility becomes a normalized, collaboratively sustained genre rather than simply individual malice or platform failure. Read together, these three papers propose a spectrum — care-branding, imitative mimicry, incivility — that is unified not by moral valence but by the fact that each is a platform-specific adaptation to the demands of visibility, consistency, and audience engagement.

## Extending the spectrum: populism, nationalism, and political brokerage

[[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Divon2026-wx]] widen this frame beyond "care" and "incivility" to show that performative legitimacy-building is also central to how TikTok creators construct epistemic and political authority. ConspiracyTok creators in [[Marwick2025-ov]] perform "populist knowledge production" — mimicking academic and journalistic evidentiary conventions while rejecting institutional epistemic authority — in ways that are neither simply sincere nor simply disinforming but serve identity-affirming and audience-building functions simultaneously. [[Divon2026-wx]] shows an analogous dynamic in the political domain: ostensibly apolitical lifestyle influencers in Israel and China become "brokers of the state" during crises, not from stable ideological conviction but because the state becomes a situational visibility resource. Nationalism and patriotism, in this account, are "flexible repertoires of practice" rather than fixed beliefs — a direct echo of how [[Volpe2026-um]] treats ethical care and [[Graham2026-fb]] treats bot-like posting as repertoires activated for platform-legible ends rather than expressions of essential selves.

## The infrastructural and discursive conditions of performance

Underlying all these performances is an algorithmic and economic infrastructure that rewards certain forms of visibility and punishes others, and several papers theorize this scaffolding directly. [[Bucher2017-qh]]'s concept of the "algorithmic imaginary" explains why users develop folk theories and affectively charged intuitions about what will be seen — the same affective calculus that drives micro-influencers' niche selection in [[Volpe2026-um]] and dogshow creators' escalating humour in [[Cabbuag2024-me]]. [[Petre2019-zh]] supplies the normative-discursive half of this picture: platforms and press moralize the boundary between legitimate strategy and illegitimate "gaming," a distinction the authors show is nebulous and self-serving rather than principled. This "platform paternalism" helps explain why practices like botting or care-branding are so easily recast as either authentic community service or cynical manipulation depending on who is narrating them — the moral vocabulary itself is a tool of platform power, not a neutral diagnostic. Cotter's account of "playing the visibility game" (filed here as [[Cotter2019-js]]) reinforces this point from the producer's side: visibility work becomes an unpaid, infrastructurally mandated labour that reshapes practice and identity even in professions, like tattooing, that predate platformization, suggesting that the pressure to "perform" for algorithms is not unique to influencer culture but a general condition of platformized work.

## Antecedents and limits: intimacy, migration, and the durability of platform logics

[[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] provides a useful genealogical anchor, showing that the collaborative, audience-dependent performance of authenticity analyzed here has precedent in earlier "networked intimacy" on Facebook, where self-disclosure was never private but always co-produced with one's network through showing, tagging, and liking. This suggests the platform-specific negotiation of sincerity is not new to TikTok or Reddit but a long-running feature of networked publics, intensified rather than invented by algorithmic affordances. [[Wang2026-ub]]'s study of failed academic migration from Twitter to Mastodon serves as a structural counterpoint: it shows what happens when users attempt to exit rather than perform within a platform's demands, finding that even highly motivated, coordinated communities cannot easily reconstitute legitimacy and social capital outside the infrastructural and reputational logics that built them in the first place. Together, these two papers bracket the main argument of this topic: performing authenticity, care, or incivility under platform demands is less a matter of individual sincerity than a structurally conditioned, culturally negotiated practice of surviving — or failing to survive — within specific, materially unequal visibility economies.
