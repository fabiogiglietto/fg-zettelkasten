# Elections & Social Media Campaigns

## The Italian core: from insularity to coordinated manipulation

The programme's spine is a decade of work tracking Italian elections through the lens of Facebook and Twitter. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] establishes the baseline hybrid-media logic even before the 2018–2022 window properly begins, showing how Twitter functioned as a second screen to television talk shows in 2013 — narrow in reach, dominated by opinion and requests for interaction rather than genuine agenda-building. By 2018, [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] reframes the question in terms of partisan insularity: populist-aligned news sources (M5S especially) circulate in more closed sharing networks, and the relationship between insularity and the balance of shares-versus-comments becomes a diagnostic of how attention is strategically managed. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] pushes this insight methodologically, arguing that disinformation research should shift from content- and actor-level detection toward *behavioural* detection of coordinated link sharing — a move that becomes the conceptual anchor for the rest of the Italian thread. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] operationalises this as a living workflow, applied to the 2022 snap election, that continuously refreshes lists of coordinated accounts rather than relying on static blacklists, surfacing everything from partisan hyperpartisan networks to religious-proselytism bot operations hiding behind political clickbait. Around this empirical core, a parallel methods thread tests how large language models can support (rather than replace) this kind of analysis: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks embedding models for clustering Italian political news, and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] confronts the validation problems of LLM-in-the-loop pipelines used across the 2018 and 2022 corpora. The arc closes, for now, with [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], which turns the same infrastructure toward auditing Meta itself — showing that the platform's political-content-reduction policy silently throttled Italian MPs' visibility months before its announced rollout, while extremist accounts adapted by posting more. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] supplies the connective theoretical statement binding these Italian case studies together: blurred organisational boundaries, microtargeted advertising, and strategic amplification are three interacting "big data" pressures that existing methods in political communication cannot easily measure.

## Scaling beyond Italy: cross-national comparative campaigns

The MINE programme's logic — coordinated activity, news visibility, citizen engagement — is increasingly tested outside Italy. The 2021 German federal election supplies [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]], which validates multimodal LLMs against specialist computer-vision pipelines for reading visual self-presentation on Instagram, finding distinct "concentrated visibility" strategies for candidates versus parties. The 2025 German election motivates [[Schulte2026-df]], which is less about substantive findings than about naming the infrastructural bottlenecks (API limits, algorithmic sampling bias, weak cross-modal correlation) that make comparable multi-platform observation so hard to sustain. At the EU level, [[Iris2026-pg]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] both interrogate the 2024 European Parliament elections: the former documents a structural, cross-country overrepresentation of the Radical Right in news coverage relative to polling and results, while the latter asks whether that very electoral performance shapes voters' concerns about disinformation — two sides of the same feedback loop between visibility and legitimacy. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] zooms into a single radical-right actor, the Sweden Democrats, tracing how a securitising EU-election campaign is deliberately re-coded across Facebook, X, and TikTok for different audiences and affective registers.

## Data access as precondition: the DSA and the politics of observability

A second, reflexive strand asks whether any of this research remains possible as platform APIs close. [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys the DSA-mandated data portals platforms have stood up, finding uneven scope and eligibility. [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematises the problem across 72 tools as one of "price, proficiency, or permission," showing that consumption-side data is nearly absent and that access regimes remain WEIRD-centric. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] takes the long view, arguing that the turbulence around DSA Article 40 is not proof of regulatory failure but evidence that binding rules are finally straining entrenched platform power, and that researchers should hold the line rather than retreat. [[Jurg2025-ur]] supplies a concrete audit of what such regulated access does and doesn't reveal, showing inconsistent application of YouTube's "authoritative source" labelling across languages and vague removal notices during the 2024 EP campaign. Read together with [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and [[Munger2025-cz]] — the latter a meta-scientific verdict that even the best-resourced platform-academic collaboration (Meta2020) produced numerically narrow, rapidly decaying findings — this cluster makes a shared argument: the *infrastructure* of observation is now as much an object of study as the campaigns themselves.

## Algorithms as political actors

Where the Italian strand studies coordination among accounts, a parallel body of work asks what platforms' own ranking systems do to political attitudes and visibility. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds that switching US users onto X's "For You" feed causally shifts attitudes rightward and that the effect persists asymmetrically because the algorithm changes who users choose to follow — a persistence mechanism that helps reconcile the largely null results of [[Allcott2025-jb]] and the broader Meta2020 collaboration critiqued by [[Munger2025-cz]]. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] narrows in on the mechanism at the account level, showing that apparent right-leaning advantage in the post-Musk algorithmic feed is better explained by agitating content and proximity to Musk than by ideology per se. [[Brady2026-ln]] intervenes experimentally, testing a "diversified extremity" ranking algorithm on Bluesky and showing that engagement-based feeds distort perceived social norms around political talk — reframing the debate from filter bubbles to algorithm-mediated social learning. [[Simeone2025-vo]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] study the flip side of curation — deplatforming and ad-delivery pricing — with the former showing that suspending a handful of central accounts can flip an entire community's authority structure, and the latter probing how delivery algorithms, not just advertiser targeting, shape the reach and cost of political ads. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] anchors this cluster in scale, mapping how misinformation diffuses disproportionately through a small set of highly-viral Facebook shares during the 2020 US election.

## Coordinated inauthentic behaviour and influence operations, globally

The behavioural-detection logic pioneered in Italy recurs, transformed, across very different information ecosystems. [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapts coordination-detection methods to WhatsApp's closed groups in Brazil, tracing a line from coordinated messaging to real-world mobilisation around the January 8 attacks. [[Oprea2025-lf]] finds hyperactive-user amplification on all major parties' Facebook pages during Romania's 2024 EP campaign, while [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] documents an early, volunteer-driven attempt to audit irregular Facebook ads in Brazil's 2018 election. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations altogether, arguing from Philippine fieldwork that they should be understood as political brokerage rather than isolated disinformation campaigns. On the US side, [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] revisit 2016–2020 interference with much sharper empirical tools than earlier IRA studies: the former shows that most exposure to deceptive Meta networks comes from unaffiliated reshares rather than direct posting, while the latter documents geo-racial targeting of voter-suppression ads and measurable turnout effects invisible to average-treatment-effect designs. Together this cluster argues that "coordination" is a portable diagnostic — as useful for WhatsApp mobs and Kosovar click-farms as for Facebook pages in Emilia-Romagna.

## Disinformation as narrative work: sensemaking, framing, and trust

A CSCW-inflected strand insists that misinformation is less about false facts than about collective interpretive labour. [[Starbird2025-jj]] develops an evidence-frame framework showing that Arizona midterm rumors emerge from politically-charged framing of largely accurate evidence, with quote-tweets doing the framing work. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] extends this temporally, showing how the "deep story" of 2020 election fraud is re-activated in 2022 through minimal, allusive cues rather than fresh explanation. These narrative dynamics have measurable downstream consequences for trust: [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows experimentally that UK media narratives *about* interference asymmetrically raise perceived polarisation depending on which party is framed as beneficiary, while [[Rossini2026-mj]] finds, in Brazil's 2022 election, that belief in electoral misinformation erodes trust in results even as mainstream news use — including among Bolsonaro voters — bolsters it, casting journalism as a source of democratic resilience against elite-driven disinformation.

## Visibility, bias, and media capture

A further set of papers examines how favourable coverage is produced and rewarded rather than simply consumed. [[Balluff2026-bv]] uses a quasi-experimental design to detect the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre" — advertising-for-coverage capture that boosted Kurz's visibility and, more subtly, sharpened attacks on his rivals. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] proposes a "logic of connective faction" whereby Republican factions gain visibility and engagement by tying themselves to hyper-partisan media ecosystems, using the sudden rise of Critical Race Theory as a diagnostic issue. [[Eady2025-vm]] supplies a general-purpose measurement model showing that ideologically extreme legislators dominate polarized news-sharing and that reduced electoral competition predicts more extreme sharing — a structural explanation for why elite visibility skews toward the poles. [[Lukito2026-il]] tracks how right-leaning outlets curate distinct content for Twitter versus Truth Social, illustrating that visibility strategies are now explicitly cross-platform.

## Affect, style, and the new campaign toolkit

Finally, a cluster of papers turns to the emotional and rhetorical registers through which campaigns court engagement, and to the newest persuasive technologies entering that toolkit. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Larsson2026-ro]] both find, across Scandinavian and Norwegian populist Facebook pages, that anger reliably outperforms warmth in generating shares, while "likes" follow a distinct, more positive logic. [[Gaber2022-bk]] historicises this dynamic conceptually, arguing that Brexit- and 2019-UK-election-era "strategic lying" is an evolution of political spin turbocharged by social media's distributed amplification and weak fact-checking purchase. At the technological frontier, [[Lin2025-xp]] shows that brief human–AI persuasion dialogues shift voting intentions across four elections more strongly than traditional ads, largely through evidence rather than manipulation — though right-leaning AI advocacy is measurably less accurate. [[Much2026-gu]] complicates the causal picture for 2024's youth gender gap, showing that non-political "gendered media spaces" like entertainment podcasts carried incidental conservative messaging to young men, though only accounting for a modest share of the shift. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] closes the loop methodologically, arguing that news-sharing research generally needs multi-level (story/outlet/content) and cross-national designs to avoid the oversimplifications that many of the papers above are already working to correct.
