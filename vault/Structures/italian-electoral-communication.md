# Italian Electoral Communication & News Ecosystem

## Mapping an Evolving News Ecosystem

The structure note gathers papers that together constitute the empirical backbone of the MINE research programme: a sustained, multi-method effort to understand how Italian (and, increasingly, European) elections are contested across news media, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and other platforms. Read chronologically, the collection traces an arc from early hybrid-media analyses of Twitter-and-television politics, through the foundational discovery of coordinated inauthentic behaviour around the 2018 and 2019 Italian votes, to a current phase preoccupied with LLM-assisted methodology and the opportunities and frustrations of DSA-regulated data access. Several threads recur: the Italian case as an exemplary "polarized pluralist" testbed, Facebook/Meta as the central (if increasingly opaque) infrastructure of study, and a growing reflexivity about the tools—human-coded, computational, and now generative-AI—used to make sense of it all.

### From Hybrid Media to Coordinated Networks

The earliest work in the corpus, [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], examines the 2013 Italian election through the lens of hybrid media systems, showing how Twitter functioned as a second screen to television talk shows, with participation remaining narrow and dominated by opinion and commentary rather than substantive agenda-building. This hybridity framework is picked up again later in [[Marino2023-9137f448]], which traces how Covid-19 disinformation circulated through "Alternative Influence Networks" that remediate televised and print statements by experts and intellectuals into shareable Facebook content—demonstrating that the TV/social-media interdependence first observed in 2013 persists, and indeed intensifies, in crisis communication.

The programme's signature empirical contribution, however, is the discovery and operationalisation of *coordinated link sharing behaviour*. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] establishes the foundational method, reframing disinformation research away from content- or actor-based detection toward an ecological focus on coordinated collective action during the 2018 and 2019 Italian elections, showing that coordinated networks disproportionately spread problematic domains. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] complements this with a partisanship lens, documenting how insularity of Twitter-based news sources correlates with Facebook sharing/commenting patterns, and identifying the Five Star Movement as a uniquely effective "cyber party" at mobilising asymmetric engagement around positive versus negative coverage. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] then operationalises coordination detection as a living workflow rather than a static snapshot, applying it to the 2022 snap election to surface ideologically, commercially, and religiously motivated networks alike—demonstrating the method's generality beyond narrowly "political" actors.

### Measuring Exposure, Trust, and Engagement at Scale

A second cluster turns from supply-side network detection to demand-side questions of exposure and engagement. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends the Guess et al. US framework to Germany, France, and Italy, finding that untrustworthy URL shares spike in Italian and German election years even as views from untrustworthy sources remain comparatively stable—an important corrective to assumptions that supply shifts translate directly into audience exposure. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] shifts geographic focus to Scandinavia but shares the programme's interest in platform affordances, showing that "Angry" reactions—disproportionately elicited by populist parties—predict sharing more strongly than "Love" reactions, reinforcing a broader finding across this topic that negative, partisan, or populist-coded content travels further on social platforms. [[Darius2026-xl]] generalises this insight to the entire EU: across 27 member states and five platforms, populist radical right (and some far-left populist) parties enjoy outsized audience support, especially on TikTok, even where their posting activity is unremarkable—suggesting the advantage lies in communicative style and algorithmic resonance rather than sheer volume. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] adds a trust dimension to this engagement-centred literature, finding that Italian audiences trust AI-mediated search results more than AI-produced news, hinting at a reconfiguration of where legitimacy is located in the information ecosystem.

Methodological reflection on media capture also enters here: [[Balluff2026-bv]], though set in Austria rather than Italy, speaks directly to this programme's concerns about covert influence over news visibility, using a difference-in-differences design to show how government advertising can cover for a "pay positive to go negative" media-capture dynamic—a mechanism Italian scholars studying party-press parallelism will find familiar. [[Mosca2026-yh]] closes this strand by reframing "fake news" accusations themselves as rhetorical weapons of negative campaigning, proposing a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model that reorients the field from misinformation-as-pathology to misinformation-discourse-as-strategy, within the Italian newspaper context.

### Platform Visibility as a Moving Target

A distinctive and increasingly central concern is how platform policy itself shapes visibility, often invisibly. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] is exemplary: using the Meta Content Library, it shows that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach by 72% well before its officially announced rollout, while extremist accounts compensated through increased posting frequency—revealing both a transparency deficit in platform communication and the uneven, politically consequential effects of ostensibly neutral moderation policies. [[McNally2025-dn]] applies longitudinal time-series methods to Facebook's News Feed algorithm's treatment of Guardian news content, arguing against the "black box" metaphor by demonstrating detectable, lagged effects of documented ranking changes specifically on hard news. [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this auditing orientation to YouTube during the 2024 European elections, finding inconsistent application of "authoritative source" labels and vague removal communications, calling for expanded researcher access via the DSA framework. Together these three papers model a shift in the programme from studying what happens *on* platforms to auditing what platforms themselves *do* to shape what is seen—treating algorithmic policy as itself a historical event to be detected and dated.

### Coordination, Authenticity, and Inauthentic Behaviour Beyond Italy

The coordination-detection paradigm developed for Italy also now travels. [[Oprea2025-lf]] applies an authenticity-matrix approach to hyperactive Facebook sharers during the 2024 Romanian EP campaign, finding substantial shares attributable to inauthentic-seeming accounts across all major parties—echoing [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]]'s and [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]]'s concerns about coordinated amplification, but in a new national context, and underscoring that Meta's restrictive API policies hinder independent verification of such behaviour.

### The Methodological Turn: LLMs, Embeddings, and Validation

A recent and self-reflexive strand addresses how computational methods—especially large language models—should be incorporated into, and validated within, this research programme. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] offers a direct comparative benchmark, showing that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large consistently outperforms the Italian-specific UmBERTo model for clustering Italian political news shared around the 2018 and 2022 elections. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] builds on this embedding work to construct and validate a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline—classification, clustering, and narrative labelling—for the same 2018/2022 Facebook datasets, confronting the problem that expert, not crowdsourced, validation is now necessary since LLMs often exceed low-skilled human annotators. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] extends the LLM-evaluation logic to the visual domain, showing GPT-4o substantially outperforms specialised computer-vision pipelines at identifying candidates and counting people in German Instagram campaign imagery—a methodological result of clear relevance to any future visual analysis of Italian campaign accounts. [[Paci2025-ag]] provides an important corrective within this optimistic turn: testing LLMs on a large corpus of Italian political speech (IMPAQTS), it finds that even the best-performing models fall well short of expert human interpretation when parsing implicatures and presuppositions—suggesting that while LLMs excel at classification, clustering, and visual recognition, they remain limited in grasping the pragmatic, context-laden meaning that is often the whole point of political rhetoric.

### Infrastructure, Access, and the Politics of Data

The final and most forward-looking cluster steps back from specific findings to interrogate the research infrastructure itself. [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys how the DSA has prompted platforms to (re)establish API-based data access, while documenting substantial variation in scope and eligibility across platforms. [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematises this terrain further, cataloguing 72 data-access tools along axes of independent/collaborative collection and production/consumption data, and diagnosing a "price, proficiency, or permission" triad of structural barriers that disproportionately exclude non-Western and under-resourced researchers—while noting the severe underrepresentation of consumption-side (demand) data relative to production-side (supply) data, a gap visible across nearly every paper in this topic that relies on CrowdTangle- or Content-Library-style supply data. [[Schulte2026-df]] makes the infrastructural argument concrete through a case study of the 2025 German election, arguing that the central problem facing multi-platform electoral research is not a shortage of studies but the absence of reusable, standardised research infrastructure for cross-platform observability.

Read together, these infrastructure papers retroactively reframe the entire topic: the detection of coordinated networks ([[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]]), the auditing of platform policy ([[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], [[McNally2025-dn]], [[Jurg2025-ur]]), and the validation of LLM pipelines ([[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]]) are all, in different ways, responses to the same underlying condition—a research ecosystem perpetually negotiating contingent, revocable, and unevenly distributed access to the platforms it studies. The programme's trajectory suggests that future work under this topic will need to treat *data access itself*, alongside news coverage and engagement patterns, as a core object of empirical inquiry in Italian and European electoral communication.
