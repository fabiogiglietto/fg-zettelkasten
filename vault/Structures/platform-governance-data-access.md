# Platform Governance & Researcher Data Access

## The Shape of the Problem: Platform Power Over Knowledge

At the center of this literature sits a structural diagnosis repeated across dozens of papers: platforms are simultaneously the object of study and the sole gatekeeper of the data needed to study them. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this as an unprecedented dependency relative to other regulated industries (pharma, tobacco, food), where independent data generation is at least possible. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its companion empirical study [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantify the consequence: roughly half of high-profile social media research carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small set of repeat-collaborating scientists and editors, producing an estimated ~80% "industrial saturation" of the field and a systematic topical bias toward user-level questions (misinformation sharing) over platform-level dynamics (recommender design, algorithmic governance). [[Munger2025-cz]]'s critique of the Meta2020 partnership and [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]'s earlier methodological diagnosis both argue that even well-resourced, consensual collaborations are structurally incapable of keeping pace with platforms that change faster than peer review — an argument [[Allen2025-ot]] extends by praising platform-independent experimental workarounds (browser-extension LLM reranking) as a necessary adaptation to closed APIs. [[Donovan2025-ws]] and [[Gillespie2010-as]]/[[Gillespie2022-jx]] supply the deeper theoretical vocabulary here: misinformation-at-scale and "reduction" (demotion without removal) are not bugs but structural features of engagement-driven business models, and the very word "platform" is itself discursive cover that obscures curatorial power — a point updated by [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" and [[Hurcombe2025-cs]]'s analysis of how Meta's Newsroom externalizes blame for problematic content onto "inauthentic" outside actors.

## The API Collapse and Its Aftermath

A dense historical thread documents the "APIcalypse" and its long tail. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the post-API age at its inception (Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure); [[Bruns2019-nr]] documents platforms instrumentalizing Cambridge Analytica to shut down critical scholarship while offering token alternatives like Social Science One; [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes two decades of access regimes into five phases ending in today's fragmented mix of pay-to-play, walled-garden, and unofficial methods; and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and Bastos2025-ya narrate Twitter/X's specific arc from "Wild West" to commercial paywall. The empirical cost is measured directly by [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] (a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024) and [[Yang2026-tq]] (a broader plateau and decline in social-media-based social science publishing from 2022-2024 across disciplines). CrowdTangle's shutdown recurs as a flashpoint: [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "Seeing like an API" framework shows how its technical design and governance rules constrained what researchers could even conceive of asking, while [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] demonstrates how a seemingly innocuous methodological choice (the 100-shares threshold) silently encoded platform-wide algorithmic shifts into supposedly cross-national comparative data — a cautionary tale about treating platform-provided data as transparent ground truth. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends the critique to the current "clean room" successor regime (Meta Content Library), arguing it privileges quantitative, code-fluent, well-funded Global North researchers and structurally excludes qualitative and cross-platform work. Tool-building responses include [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats random-sampling infrastructure for YouTube and TikTok, developed explicitly because neither platform's "research API" produces defensible, representative samples — a complaint echoed in API-specific audits like [[Rieder2025-ju]] (YouTube's search endpoint is "forgetful by design," decaying within 20-60 days) and [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] (TikTok's Research API persistently underdelivers despite DSA-driven expansion).

## Article 40 and the DSA as Contested Regulatory Settlement

The regulatory core of this topic is Article 40 of the DSA, treated across many papers as both landmark and unfinished business. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] together frame the current moment (2025-2026) as a genuine but fragile transition from platform philanthropy to legal entitlement — De Vreese and Tromble explicitly argue that observed friction (delays, boilerplate denials, narrow interpretations) should be read as evidence the regulation is finally touching real asymmetries of power, not as proof of its failure. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] situates this within the broader "Wild West to Walled Garden" arc, noting the Commission's 2025 findings that TikTok and Meta breached Article 40(12) obligations — a determination directly relevant to the researcher's own role as JRC/ECAT expert in this process. [[Peters2026-mo]] and [[Annabell2026-ev]] dig into the conceptual substrate of the regulation itself, showing how contested terms — "data quality," "data" as such — are "floating signifiers" that platforms (especially Meta, Booking.com, Amazon, X) strategically narrow during consultation to minimize what they must disclose, while academic and NGO stakeholders push, with partial success, for broader and more rigorous definitions. [[Lukito2026-nb]] catalogs 72 access tools along "price, proficiency, permission" axes, finding consumption-side data severely underprovided relative to production-side data, and argues for regulatory mandates modeled on the DSA as the only durable fix. Sector-specific audits give this regulatory debate empirical teeth: [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data to expose language-based moderation disparities (Twitter/X allocates Spanish/Portuguese/Arabic moderators at just 7-9% of English's rate); [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's authority-signaling and removal communications during EU elections and calls for expanded YouTube Research Program access; and [[Schulte2026-df]] treats multi-platform election research itself as "a research infrastructure problem" requiring standardization, using the 2025 German election as a case study.

## Meta's Political Content Policy as a Live Natural Experiment

A cluster of recent papers uses DSA-enabled access (especially the Meta Content Library and URL Shares Dataset) to empirically interrogate Meta's own governance choices, producing some of the sharpest findings in the corpus. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] documents that Meta's 2021-2025 political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' Facebook reach by up to 72%, began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout, and disproportionately protected extremist accounts' total reach even as mainstream politicians lost visibility — with the policy itself undisclosed in Meta's own transparency reporting. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] complements this with evidence that partisan-aligned content is systematically penalized in the shares-to-views pipeline in ways that spike around known "break the glass" interventions, arguing Facebook is an active curator rather than neutral conduit. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces the discursive shrinking of Meta's problem-naming — from "fake news" to "false news" to "misinformation" to "borderline" — alongside empirical replication showing borderline content's engagement is now overwhelmingly right-wing-sourced, even as Meta's preferred "reach" metric obscures this. [[Holt2026-zq]] examines what the coming Community Notes era actually means by analyzing historical user "False News" reports, finding they track political disagreement rather than factual falsity — a finding with direct bearing on [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]]'s interview-based study of fact-checkers' skepticism toward Community Notes and their "apologia" defense of continued platform partnerships ([[Farkas2026-lr]]), and [[Bouchaud2026-np]]'s formal demonstration that Community Notes' bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing content by design.

## Adjacent Harms, Algorithmic Opacity, and the Wider Governance Stack

Several papers push beyond data-access politics into the substantive harms that opaque platform design enables or fails to catch. [[Karo2026-dn]]'s TikTok jihadist-content typology and [[Vincent_undated-re]]'s cross-platform misinformation measurement both document moderation's practical limits; [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] shows X's recommender system inadvertently encodes users' political ideology as a linear embedding direction, raising profiling concerns under GDPR/DSA alike; [[McNally2025-dn]] demonstrates that Facebook's News Feed, far from being an unknowable black box, produces statistically detectable lagged effects from documented algorithm changes — direct ammunition for systemic-risk auditing under Article 40(4). [[Ahuja2025-ku]] builds a "law-to-design" bridge between HCI dark-patterns scholarship and DSA Article 25 autonomy violations, while [[Crosset2026-mq]] and [[van-Dijck2018-up]] zoom out to theorize content moderation and platformization as forms of security bureaucratization and contested public-value struggles respectively. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] and [[Swartz2026-zb]] extend the governance conversation to adjacent harms — AI-driven financial fraud and the normalization of scam economies — arguing similarly for gatekeeper/platform liability rather than victim-centered remedies. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study both situate platform accountability within the broader democratic-backsliding diagnosis, with experts rating platform governance interventions as marginally more important than individual-level literacy or fact-checking fixes. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] offers a programmatic corrective, urging the field to study platform collective behavior as "democratic infrastructure" rather than chase narrow causal effects — a fitting bookend to [[Goldberg2026-eb]]'s forward-looking survey of LLM-enabled bridging, deliberation, and moderation systems as a possible "second paradigm shift" for digital public squares, and to [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]]'s finding that meaningful AI transparency requires disclosing persuasive *intent*, not merely synthetic identity — a direct challenge to the assumptions underlying EU AI Act Article 50.

## Risk, Labor, and the Human Infrastructure of Oversight

Finally, several papers turn to the people doing this work. [[Park2026-tr]]'s interview study documents the legal chilling effects (CFAA, CMA) researchers face when their methods brush against platform terms of service, with real cases of job loss, prosecution, and abandoned projects — a sobering counterweight to [[Pierri2026-ib]]'s more institutional account of DSA implementation bottlenecks (circular funding problems, vetting delays, resource asymmetries between academic and corporate research). [[Moran2025-qn]] documents parallel upheaval inside platforms' own trust-and-safety professions, suggesting that researcher access struggles are mirrored by an erosion of internal safety capacity. Read together, these papers sketch a single, continuous arc: from the discursive invention of "platforms" as neutral infrastructure ([[Gillespie2010-as]]), through the API collapse and its bibliometric fallout, into today's fraught but consequential DSA settlement — with Article 40 emerging not as an endpoint but as the current, actively contested front line in a decades-long struggle over who gets to know what platforms actually do.
