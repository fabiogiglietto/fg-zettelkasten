# Platform Governance and Research Data Access

# Platform Governance and Research Data Access

## The closing of the open web and the rise of the "post-API age"

The foundational narrative running through this literature is one of enclosure. [[Freelon2024-sc]] provides the canonical periodization—from API "prehistory" through a brief "Golden Age" of academic access to the current fractured landscape of pay-to-play APIs, walled academic gardens, and unofficial workarounds—and this framework is echoed, extended, and re-narrated across dozens of papers filed here. Bastos2025-ya and [[Bruns2026-yv]] eulogize Twitter specifically as a once-uniquely-open research infrastructure whose "flat" network structure enabled a distinct subfield of "Twitter studies," now foreclosed by Musk-era pay-to-play pricing. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies this loss bibliometrically, showing 25% annual growth in Twitter-based scholarship through 2022 followed by stagnation and a 13% decline by 2024—a "eulogy" rendered in citation counts. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] generalizes the pattern beyond Twitter/X, tracing Meta's CrowdTangle shutdown, Reddit's API pricing, and TikTok's restrictions as instances of a single structural shift from "data philanthropy" to commercial paywall, arguing that voluntary, platform-led access is inherently fragile. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review of social science publishing corroborates this at the level of the whole discipline: empirical social media research tripled between 2010 and the early 2020s before plateauing and declining in 2022-2024, precisely as API restrictions bit. Together these pieces establish the master plot: an era of relative openness, concentrated overwhelmingly on Twitter/X and Facebook (a skew Yang shows is badly misaligned with actual platform popularity), giving way to scarcity just as regulatory demand for accountability research intensifies.

## The DSA as regulatory answer—and its friction

Against this backdrop of closure, the EU Digital Services Act's Article 40 emerges as the central regulatory intervention under scrutiny. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] form a cluster of forum-style assessments asking what Article 40 actually changes in practice. The tone across them is cautiously optimistic but unsentimental: [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] argues explicitly that platform pushback, procedural friction, and delay should be read not as regulatory failure but as evidence the law is finally constraining entrenched asymmetries of informational power, urging a "marathon" posture of sustained implementation rather than redesign. [[Pierri2025-hm]], drawing on an 18-month pilot with DG-CONNECT, catalogs concrete bottlenecks—resource asymmetries, a "circular resource problem" disadvantaging early-career researchers, and regulatory blind spots around LLMs—while [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates these frictions within a broader argument that platforms' exclusive control of data makes academic research on social media uniquely vulnerable to industry capture, comparable to tobacco or pharma. [[Peters2026-mo]] adds granularity by showing empirically, via analysis of EU consultation feedback, that "data quality" was a politically contested afterthought largely absent from official DSA text until academic and NGO stakeholders forced its partial inclusion—while Meta strategically inverted the argument to claim access was futile. [[Crosset2026-mq]] and [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pull back to a more theoretical register, the former reframing DSA-style legislation as "security bureaucratisation" that governs circulation rather than content per se, the latter arguing platform research should move beyond narrow causal-effects paradigms toward conceptualizing collective platform behavior as democratic infrastructure.

## Auditing the instruments: do the new tools actually work?

A substantial empirical cluster tests whether DSA-mandated or DSA-adjacent access mechanisms deliver on their promise, and the verdict is largely skeptical. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained limitations despite its DSA-driven 2023 expansion. [[Rieder2025-ju]] conducts a rigorous audit of YouTube's Data API search endpoint, discovering it is "forgetful by design"—a severe temporal decay effect erases findability of videos after 20-60 days regardless of continued public availability, undermining any retrospective systemic-risk research. [[Jurg2025-ur]], auditing YouTube's moderation of the 2024 EU Parliamentary elections, finds inconsistent application of "News Funding Notice" labels and vague removal communications, calling for reconceiving transparency as "observability." [[Tonneau2025-bv]] leverages DSA transparency mandates themselves to expose systematic disparities in human moderator allocation across languages, with Global South languages receiving as little as 7.5% of English's moderator allocation on X. [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] both treat the fragmented tooling landscape as an infrastructure problem in its own right: Schulte's German election case study argues platform observability should be built as reusable infrastructure rather than one-off scraping, while Lukito's taxonomy of 72 access tools identifies a "price, proficiency, or permission" triad of exclusionary barriers and documents the severe underrepresentation of consumption-side (as opposed to production-side) data. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] offers a direct survey of the API/tool landscape in the DSA era as a reference point for this whole cluster.

## Platform-provided datasets as compromised instruments

A related strand interrogates not access mechanisms in the abstract but the specific datasets platforms have historically offered researchers, showing how methodological artifacts of platform engineering can masquerade as social signal. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] is exemplary: the 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset produces near-identical monthly trends across 46 wildly different countries, revealing that a seemingly innocuous privacy-preserving design choice can generate a spurious "global" pattern driven by algorithmic ranking changes rather than genuine political events. [[McNally2025-dn]] similarly uses CrowdTangle-derived Guardian engagement data to show Facebook's News Feed algorithm produces detectable, lagged (19-24 day) effects specifically on hard news—directly challenging the "black box" framing and arguing DSA Article 40(4) offers a legal basis for systemic algorithmic audits. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends this critical use of Meta's Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset to show that partisan alignment dampens, and journalistic quality amplifies, the shares-to-views relationship in ways that fluctuate with known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures—evidence that Facebook is an active curator, not a neutral conduit. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] pushes this further into policy accountability: using the Meta Content Library (itself a DSA-enabled tool), the study finds Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' reach by 72%, began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout, and was never disclosed in DSA compliance reports—a concrete instance of the "transparency deficit" this whole literature worries about, even as the author affirms the underlying research value of DSA-enabled infrastructure.

## Industry capture of the scholarly record

Running parallel to concerns about data access is a distinct but entangled concern about industry influence over what gets published using that data. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] form a matched pair: the Perspective piece draws historical analogy to tobacco and fossil-fuel science denial to catalog mechanisms of tech-industry influence (suppression of internal research, design bias, selective funding, performative collaboration), while the empirical companion quantifies this in top journals, finding roughly half of high-profile social media papers have disclosable industry ties—most undisclosed—concentrated among a small elite of repeatedly-engaged scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the field. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a scathing internal critique of the Meta2020 partnership specifically, arguing its null findings reflect low "temporal validity" and underpowered design as much as any real absence of effect, and concluding that social science is structurally incapable of keeping pace with rapidly mutating platforms—hence the need for proactive, mandated self-disclosure by platforms rather than reactive academic study. [[Allen2025-ot]] responds to this same crisis of temporal validity by celebrating "platform-independent" experimental methods (browser extensions plus LLM reranking) as a necessary adaptation given closing APIs and rare collaborations. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines an adjacent dependency: how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend their funding relationships with the very platforms whose content they evaluate, deploying apologia strategies of differentiation and transcendence that mirror journalism's traditional "wall" metaphors.

## Platforms as discursive and design actors

Several papers turn from data infrastructure to the platforms' own communicative and design strategies for managing accountability pressure. [[De2026-ld]] coins "changecraft" to describe how Meta, YouTube, X, and TikTok discursively stage policy change as neutral, responsive innovation while consolidating power—explicitly linking this to regulatory pressures like Germany's NetzDG. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects Meta's Newsroom discourse specifically, showing how floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" externalize blame while eliding the platform's own affordances (notably Facebook Groups), with these very terms diffusing into regulatory language. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] operationalizes DSA Article 25's prohibition on manipulative design by mapping dark-pattern taxonomies onto legal categories of autonomy violation, applying the framework live to the EU's case against X's paid verification. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] demonstrates empirically that X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a strongly linear ideological dimension in its embedding space, blurring GDPR-relevant distinctions between active and passive profiling, while its companion [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows Community Notes structurally undermoderates polarizing content by design, since such content rarely attracts the cross-partisan agreement the bridging algorithm requires.

## Downstream consequences: what gets missed when data and moderation fail

A final set of papers illustrates the substantive stakes of these access and governance failures through concrete cases of moderation breakdown, migration, and algorithmic effects. [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] both document how content survives and adapts around moderation—jihadist propaganda via TikTok vernacular evasion, Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology" persisting across a decentralized YouTube ecosystem faster than removal can act. [[Vincent_undated-re]] tracks rising misinformation prevalence on TikTok specifically through repeated platform measurement. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Gauthier2026-iq]] both audit algorithmic political effects on X/Twitter, the former showing algorithmic visibility gains for right-leaning accounts are better explained by proximity to Musk and agitating content than ideology per se, the latter running a field experiment showing algorithmic feeds shift users toward conservative positions in a durable, asymmetric way that helps reconcile earlier Meta null results. [[Wang2026-ub]] documents the failed migration of academic Twitter users to Mastodon despite motivated coordination, while Bastos2025-ol mines a large troll-farm dataset for visual signatures of state-linked influence operations. [[Moran2025-qn]] and [[Donovan2025-ws]] both diagnose the retreat of institutional trust-and-safety capacity—Donovan historicizing "misinformation-at-scale" as structurally built into engagement-driven business models, Moran documenting the professional upheaval of the trust and safety field itself. [[Votta2025-xz]], [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]], [[Mosleh2024-op]], and [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] extend the empirical reach into political advertising delivery algorithms, independent ad-auditing, cross-platform news engagement divergence, and the broader methodological transformations big data has wrought on political communication scholarship, respectively. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] and [[Swartz2026-zb]] widen the frame further still, treating scam ecosystems and deepfake fraud as emergent governance challenges that implicate the same platforms, the same liability debates, and the same gatekeeper-versus-victim framing found throughout this literature. Finally, [[Richter2026-bt]] and [[Mahl2026-hc]] bookend the topic at a more diagnostic level—tracing how stakeholder composition shaped early AI discourse, and synthesizing expert consensus that governance of and by platforms remains the single most important intervention point for the future of the information ecosystem, with [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] sounding the sharpest alarm that platforms bear direct responsibility for democratic backsliding.

Taken together, these papers trace a coherent arc: an era of accidental openness gave social science an unprecedented empirical purchase on platforms, that openness was progressively enclosed by commercial and political decisions, and the DSA now stands as the first serious regulatory attempt to reopen it by fiat—an attempt whose real-world implementation is uneven, contested, and shadowed by continuing industry capture of both data and the scholarship built upon it.
