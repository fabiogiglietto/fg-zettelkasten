# Platform Governance, APIs & Data Access

## The arc of the post-API age

The baseline narrative threading through this collection is a historical one: [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the "post-API age" at the moment Facebook's Pages API closed in 2018, and [[Bruns2019-nr]] immediately reframes that closure as an "APIcalypse" — not a privacy fix but a strategic move to frustrate critical, public-interest scrutiny. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] extend this into fuller periodizations (laissez-faire → academic cooperation → pay-to-play/walled garden), showing that every platform's access regime is downstream of scandal, ownership change, or commercial calculation rather than principled design. Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantify what was lost when Twitter's APIs shut down and pivoted to $42,000/month pricing: a bibliometric "eulogy" documenting double-digit annual growth followed by a 13% publication decline in 2024. [[Yang2026-tq]] generalizes this trajectory across social science as a whole — a tripling of social-media-based scholarship through the early 2020s, then a plateau and decline concentrated in Twitter/X and Facebook research, exactly where API restriction hit hardest. [[Bruns2026-yv]] closes this historical loop with an essay on Twitter's own "death" as a public-debate space, arguing that governance failure and data-access retrenchment were two faces of the same platform decay.

## DSA Article 40 as the new settlement — and its frictions

A second cluster treats the EU Digital Services Act, especially Article 40, as the field's emergent (if contested) remedy. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frame Article 40 as a genuine shift from discretionary "data philanthropy" to a binding legal right, while cautioning that current turbulence — platform stalling, legal smokescreens — should be read as evidence the regulation is finally biting, not that it has failed. [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] add the practitioner's view from inside the vetting and application process: circular resource problems, power asymmetries between platforms and academe, and the "price, proficiency, or permission" triad that determines who can actually use any access regime. [[Annabell2026-ev]] and [[Peters2026-mo]] dig into the regulatory text itself, showing that core terms — "data," "data quality" — are left undefined or politically contested, letting platforms (Meta, Booking.com, X) narrow their obligations through self-serving interpretation during the Delegated Act consultation. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and [[Bechmann2026-dr]] gesture toward the broader research-infrastructure and conceptual reframing this moment demands — moving past narrow causal-effects designs toward platform behavior as democratic infrastructure. [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the whole DSA apparatus within a comparative legal-governance frame, arguing that the DSA's "flow optimisation" model, NetzDG's "patrolling," and US "free circulation" statutes converge on a shared logic of security bureaucratisation via transparency, auditing, and reporting mandates.

## Auditing the access mechanisms themselves

A substantial empirical strand interrogates whether the tools DSA access actually produces are fit for purpose. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's Research API still riddled with unexplained inconsistencies despite its 2023 EU expansion; [[Rieder2025-ju]] delivers a devastating audit of YouTube's search API, showing a three-phase temporal decay that makes historical retrieval unreliable — "forgetful by design." [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this to YouTube's moderation transparency around the 2024 EU elections, finding inconsistently applied publisher labels and vague removal statements. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses the DSA's own transparency data to expose stark cross-lingual disparities in human moderation staffing, with Global South languages receiving a fraction of English's coverage. [[Zheng2026-bi]] offers a constructive counterpoint, building independent random-sampling tools (TubeStats, TokStats) precisely because neither platform's sanctioned API can produce representative samples. [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] turn the critical lens onto Meta's own transition from CrowdTangle to the Meta Content Library, developing a "seeing like an API" framework and documenting how "clean room" environments — while addressing real privacy concerns — structurally favor well-resourced, code-literate, quantitative researchers over qualitative, cross-platform, or Global South scholarship. [[Schulte2026-df]] arrives at a similar infrastructural diagnosis from an empirical angle, treating the 2025 German election as a case study in how platform-specific scraping constraints shape what can even be asked about multi-platform campaigns.

## What the Meta data actually reveals — and its artifacts

A tightly linked set of papers uses Meta's successive data products (URL Shares Dataset, CrowdTangle, Meta Content Library) both substantively and as objects of methodological critique. [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] show how Meta's privacy-preserving 100-public-shares threshold systematically distorts prevalence estimates and manufactures spurious cross-country trend similarity — artifacts researchers must disentangle from genuine social signal. Building on Meta Content Library access, [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] demonstrate that Facebook actively curates reach via partisan-alignment penalties and journalistic-quality rewards that fluctuate with election cycles and undisclosed policy changes — directly contradicting any "neutral conduit" framing, and revealing, in the Italian case, that Meta's political-content-reduction policy began roughly ten months before its announced global rollout. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces the discursive shrinking of "fake news" into "borderline content" across 2016–2024 and finds that well-performing borderline content remains almost exclusively right-wing, a pattern partly obscured by Meta's pivot from engagement to reach metrics. [[Holt2026-zq]] examines what Facebook users themselves flag as "false news" post-fact-checking, finding user reports track political disagreement more than falsity — a troubling omen for Meta's 2025 shift toward Community Notes, which [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] examine from the fact-checkers' side, documenting professional skepticism about crowdsourced replacement and the rhetorical work fact-checkers do to defend platform funding ties.

## The Meta2020 reckoning and the limits of platform-cooperative science

The 2020 Meta/academic election studies form their own evidentiary center of gravity. [[Guess2023-ai]] and [[Guess2023-ur]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] report the original null/near-null findings on reshares, chronological feeds, and ideological segregation; [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the sharpest meta-scientific critique, arguing the collaboration's "temporal validity" and "poetic validity" are structurally compromised — platforms change faster than social science can characterize them, and null findings conveniently serve platform interests. [[Allen2025-ot]] and [[Gauthier2026-iq]] push past this impasse methodologically: Gauthier's field experiment on X finds algorithmic feeds do shift political attitudes conservative-ward through a persistence mechanism (induced following) that helps reconcile the earlier Meta nulls, while Allen and Tucker celebrate platform-independent browser-extension experimentation as a necessary adaptation to closed APIs. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] bring complementary algorithmic audits to X/Twitter, respectively showing that visibility gains for right-leaning accounts are mediated by proximity to Musk and agitation rather than ideology per se, and that the recommender's embedding space inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear ideology signal. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] and [[Miller2026-cp]] turn this scrutiny onto Community Notes/X's bridging-based moderation itself, finding structural undermoderation of polarizing content even as the AI Note Writer ecosystem scales human-AI collaboration.

## Industry capture, discourse, and the political economy of the research relationship

A reflexive cluster asks what platform control over data does to the research enterprise itself. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document — with striking empirical specificity — how industry ties saturate high-profile social media research (≈80% once authors, editors, and reviewers are combined), biasing topic selection toward user-level misinformation research and away from platform-dynamics critique, echoing tobacco- and pharma-industry playbooks. [[Donovan2025-ws]], [[Gillespie2010-as]], [[Gillespie2022-jx]], [[van-Dijck2018-up]], [[De2026-ld]], [[Hurcombe2025-cs]], and [[Petre2019-zh]] together build the discursive and conceptual vocabulary for understanding platform self-presentation: "platform" as a strategically neutral metaphor, "reduction" as an invisible form of moderation evading transparency regimes, "changecraft" and "platform paternalism" as the rhetorical machinery through which platforms narrate their own power as benevolent and inevitable. [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the parallel institutional collapse of trust-and-safety as a profession, while [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Swartz2026-zb]] situate platform accountability within wider democratic backsliding and the normalization of scam-driven platform economies. [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] shows this same "governance-washing" dynamic operating specifically around climate misinformation, where public commitments rarely translate into enforceable rules.

## Harms at the edges: extremism, deepfakes, elections, and legal risk

Several papers push the topic outward to specific harm domains shaped by (and shaping debates about) data access. [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] document how jihadist and manosphere content exploit TikTok's and YouTube's recommendation architectures faster than moderation and deplatforming can contain them. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] map AI-enabled scam and "deceptive information operations" ecosystems that existing CIB-style enforcement categories cannot capture. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]], [[Votta2025-xz]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], and [[Richter2026-bt]] examine election-specific data access and advertising transparency gaps across Brazil, Germany, and the US. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]], [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]], [[Goldberg2026-eb]], and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extend the governance conversation into generative-AI-mediated search, chatbot persuasion, and public-square design, suggesting the data-access crisis is poised to recur in AI form. Underpinning much of this empirical work, [[Park2026-tr]] documents the chilling legal risks researchers themselves absorb when platform-sanctioned access fails and scraping or adversarial methods become necessary — a reminder that behind every API audit and data-donation workaround in this literature lies a researcher weighing real professional and legal jeopardy, a condition [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Allcott2019-gn]] remind us long predates, but is now acutely compounded by, the platforms' current architecture of control.
