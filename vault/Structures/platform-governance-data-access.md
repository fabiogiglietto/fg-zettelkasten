# Platform Governance & Researcher Data Access

## The arc: from open data to regulated access

This body of work traces a shift from an era of relatively open, if precarious, platform data access to one defined by formal regulatory intervention — chiefly the EU's Digital Services Act — and its uneven, contested implementation. The historical throughline is laid out by [[Freelon2024-sc]], Bastos2025-ya, and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], who each periodize the collapse of the "Wild West" API era into today's "walled garden": Cambridge Analytica's fallout, Musk's dismantling of Twitter's Academic API, CrowdTangle's 2024 shutdown, and TikTok's capped Research API. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the cost in scholarly terms — a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications by 2024 after two decades of growth — while [[Yang2026-tq]] shows the broader pattern across social science disciplines: empirical social-media research tripled from 2010 to the early 2020s, then plateaued and declined precisely as API restrictions bit. [[Bruns2026-yv]] and [[Wang2026-ub]] extend this into a story of platform and community decline itself — Twitter's "enshittification" and academia's failed migration to Mastodon — suggesting that data infrastructure and research community are entangled fates.

## Article 40 and the promise (and friction) of regulated access

A cluster of papers directly assesses the DSA's Article 40 as the mechanism meant to reverse this closure. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] — several written from the vantage of researchers embedded in EU policy processes — describe Article 40 as a genuine paradigm shift from "independence by permission" toward a binding legal right, while cataloguing the friction of implementation: misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, circular funding problems, and platform stalling tactics. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] sharpen this into critique of the "clean room" model (epitomized by the Meta Content Library), arguing that lawful access is not the same as *useful* access — clean rooms privilege quantitative, code-fluent, well-resourced Global North researchers and structurally exclude qualitative and cross-platform work. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows this contestation playing out textually: data *quality* was nearly absent from official DSA documents until academic and NGO stakeholders forced its partial inclusion, while Meta strategically inverted the argument to claim access was futile. [[Cullen2026-cb]] extends the critique backward to the CrowdTangle era, proposing a "seeing like an API" framework for how platform-mediated infrastructure shapes what can even be asked. Together these pieces resist reading current turbulence as regulatory failure; as [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] argues, platform resistance is itself evidence the DSA is finally constraining real power asymmetries.

## Auditing the auditors: what regulated APIs actually deliver

A second cluster empirically tests whether DSA-mandated tools work as promised. [[Rieder2025-ju]] finds YouTube's search API "forgetful by design," with retrievable content decaying sharply within weeks — a direct threat to systemic-risk research. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] document similar shortfalls in TikTok's Research API and YouTube's moderation transparency respectively, the latter also proposing "observability" as a better transparency ideal than raw access. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA-mandated transparency data itself to reveal stark disparities in moderator allocation by language, showing that even where access exists, it can expose (rather than resolve) inequity. [[Zheng2026-bi]] offers a constructive counterpoint: independently engineered random-sampling tools (TubeStats, TokStats) that supply denominators platforms won't provide. [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] generalize the problem to election research, framing multi-platform observability as a research-infrastructure question rather than a series of one-off studies, and cataloguing the "price, proficiency, permission" barriers — plus the WEIRD-centric skew of what access exists — across 72 tools.

## Industry influence, evidentiary integrity, and the Meta2020 reckoning

A parallel thread interrogates what happens to knowledge production when platforms remain gatekeepers even amid nominal openness. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] draw explicit analogies to tobacco, pharma, and food-industry science, documenting undisclosed industry ties among a concentrated elite of authors, editors, and reviewers, and showing industry-tied research skews toward user-focused topics that deflect from platform-level dynamics. [[Munger2025-cz]] and [[Allen2025-ot]] use the Meta2020 Facebook/Instagram election-study partnership as the paradigmatic case: methodologically ambitious yet undermined by low "temporal" and "poetic" validity, underpowered designs, and conceptual incoherence, exposing structural limits of platform-mediated science and pointing toward proactive regulation as the only durable fix. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] anticipate these tensions from the earlier "big data" moment, while [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] provides a cautionary methodological case study — showing how an ostensibly neutral anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset actually encodes hidden algorithmic shifts, a warning echoed methodologically in [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], both of which use platform data to show Facebook actively curating (not neutrally transmitting) news visibility in ways that track governance interventions like "break the glass" measures.

## Policy change as object of study: Meta's political content reduction and beyond

Several papers turn DSA-enabled data access itself toward auditing specific platform policy shifts. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] is the centerpiece: using the Meta Content Library to show Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach fell 72% under Meta's political content reduction policy — implemented roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout and undisclosed in DSA transparency reports — while extremist accounts compensated through sheer posting volume, a finding that indicts Meta's transparency practices even as it validates the research value of the new infrastructure. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] examines the parallel end of Meta's Third-Party Fact-Checking Program, finding Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing empirically unfounded and fact-checkers skeptical of Community Notes as a replacement — a skepticism borne out technically by [[Bouchaud2026-np]], which shows Community Notes' bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates precisely the polarizing content most relevant to elections. [[Holt2026-zq]] complements this by showing user "False News" reports track political disagreement more than falsity, undercutting the premise that crowdsourced reporting can substitute for professional verification. [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] examine the discursive and institutional politics underlying these shifts — how fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform dependency, and how Meta's own Newsroom frames "problematic content" to externalize blame — while [[De2026-ld]] theorizes platforms' broader communicative strategy ("changecraft") for narrating policy change as continuity rather than rupture.

## Downstream consequences: bans, deplatforming, and algorithmic governance

Where data access permits it, researchers trace concrete effects of platform governance decisions on democratic discourse. [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents Brazil's X ban producing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet"; [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-fraud accounts collapsing an entire community's authority structure; [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] audit recommender systems directly, finding algorithmic visibility gains tied to proximity to Musk and inadvertent ideological profiling embedded in embedding space. [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Karo2026-dn]] extend this to content moderation's limits against ambient extremism (Andrew Tate, jihadist TikTok vernaculars), arguing moderation is structurally outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation. [[Brady2026-ln]] offers a rare experimental intervention — a Bluesky field study showing engagement-based feeds amplify intergroup, moralized content and distort social-norm perceptions, while a "diversified extremity" algorithm partially mitigates this — modeling what independent, non-platform-dependent causal research on governance questions can look like when platforms permit it (or are circumvented, as in [[Allen2025-ot]]'s browser-extension approach).

## Regulatory theory and the wider stakes

Zooming out, [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the DSA within a comparative taxonomy of content-moderation regimes (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation), arguing all converge on "security bureaucratisation" — transparency and auditing as modes of governing platforms as quasi-public infrastructure. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes for reframing platforms as "democratic infrastructure" beyond narrow causal-effects paradigms, a call [[Vertesi2026-lv]] radicalizes by warning that accountability research itself can be captured by "decoys" that obscure the underlying political economy of Big Tech. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] shows how legal categories (DSA Article 25's autonomy violations) can be operationalized into HCI design audits, offering a template for translating regulation into empirical method. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]], [[Mahl2026-hc]], and [[Donovan2025-ws]] situate all of this within the larger diagnosis of democratic backsliding and platform-native misinformation, arguing that no single intervention — moderation, literacy, fact-checking — suffices without coordinated, structural reform. [[Park2026-tr]] closes the loop on researcher precarity itself, documenting the chilling legal risks (CFAA-style prosecution threats, institutional abandonment) faced by those who conduct exactly this kind of independent platform research, underscoring that data access is only one of several fronts on which the position of the independent researcher — and the DSA/ECAT expert role this topic implicitly centers — remains institutionally fragile.
