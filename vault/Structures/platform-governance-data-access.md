# Platform Governance & Researcher Data Access

## The Governance Turn: From Platform Discretion to Regulatory Mandate

The corpus under this heading traces a long arc from an era of platform discretion over data and discourse toward one of contested, partially enforced regulatory obligation. [[Gillespie2010-as]] supplies the conceptual starting point: "platform" itself is shown to be strategic discursive work, letting intermediaries claim neutrality while making substantive curatorial interventions. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] scales this up into the "platform society," where private infrastructures absorb public functions and public values must be fought for rather than assumed. The intervening decade is a story of platforms consolidating this discretionary power — and of researchers losing the means to study it. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the rupture point: Facebook's 2018 Pages API shutdown inaugurates the "post-API age," and [[Bruns2019-nr]] immediately reframes the Cambridge Analytica fallout not as privacy protection but as an "APIcalypse" — a pretext platforms used to frustrate independent accountability research while channeling academics into asymmetric, industry-controlled alternatives like Social Science One. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] captures the same period's methodological fallout for political communication more broadly: blurred organizational boundaries, opaque micro-targeting, and attention dynamics that existing tools could not measure.

The EU's Digital Services Act (DSA), and specifically Article 40, emerges across many papers as the regulatory answer to this discretionary regime — but one whose implementation is itself contested terrain. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] converge on a cautiously optimistic reading: Article 40 is a genuine paradigm shift from data philanthropy to legal right, even as vetting delays, resource asymmetries, and platform foot-dragging threaten to hollow it out. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodizes this longer history explicitly — "Wild West," "Golden Age," commercial paywall, DSA mandate — arguing that each phase is reversible by ownership change or corporate strategy, a fragility [[Bechmann2026-dr]] echoes by calling for research paradigms that treat platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure rather than something only causal experiments can certify. [[Crosset2026-mq]] zooms out comparatively, showing that DSA-style "flow optimisation," Germany/Austria's NetzDG-style "patrolling," and US "free circulation" laws are not converging on one model but are three distinct security rationalities that nonetheless share a bureaucratic logic of transparency, auditing, and reporting — platforms rendered quasi-public infrastructures through administrative rather than direct control.

## What Article 40 Actually Delivers (and Withholds)

A dense cluster of papers empirically tests the DSA's data-access machinery against its promises. [[Peters2026-mo]] and [[Annabell2026-ev]] both show that foundational concepts — "data quality" and "data" itself — are left undefined in the regulation, becoming contested, politically negotiated terms that platforms (especially Meta, Amazon, Booking.com, X) exploit to narrow scope toward aggregated, quantified datasets, sidelining qualitative and humanities-style research. [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes the resulting exclusions as a "price, proficiency, or permission" problem, finding only 6 of 72 cataloged access tools address consumption-side data and most access regimes remain WEIRD-centric. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]], [[Rieder2025-ju]], and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] provide platform-specific audits of what DSA-mandated transparency actually yields: TikTok's Research API remains inconsistent, YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design" with severe temporal decay undermining retrospective election research, and DSA-mandated moderator transparency data reveals stark linguistic inequities (e.g., Twitter/X moderators for Spanish/Portuguese/Arabic content at 7–9% of English allocation). [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this into content moderation audits proper, finding inconsistent application of YouTube's "News Funding Notice" labels and vague removal communications, and calls for reconceiving transparency as "observability." [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] offers a broader survey of the API/tools landscape in this new era, while [[Philipp2026-tl]] focuses specifically on how regulated access reshapes election research, finding uneven eligibility and persistent gaps around advertising data.

The "clean room" has become the DSA era's signature access architecture, and two papers scrutinize it critically. [[Bruns2026-pn]] argues that Meta Content Library's secure-environment design, while addressing legitimate privacy concerns, structurally privileges well-resourced, code-fluent, quantitative researchers in the Global North, excluding qualitative and cross-platform work. [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "Seeing like an API" framework shows the same dynamic operated even before DSA mandates: CrowdTangle's technical and governance design shaped what research questions were askable, and researchers' adaptive workarounds (optimizing, extending, verifying) both resisted and entrenched platform epistemic control — a cautionary precedent for whatever comes after clean rooms. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]], Bastos2025-ya, and [[Yang2026-tq]] quantify the damage in bibliometric terms: Twitter-based scholarship grew 25% annually through 2022 before declining 13% in 2024 after the $42,000/month API paywall, and social-science use of social media data generally has plateaued and begun receding since 2022 — a shift [[Allen2025-ot]] frames as motivating a new wave of "platform-independent" experimental methods (e.g., browser-extension LLM rerankers) that bypass platform cooperation altogether, and [[Zheng2026-bi]] answers with open infrastructure (TubeStats/TokStats) built on unpermissioned random sampling.

## Meta's Retreat: Political Content Reduction, Fact-Checking, and the CrowdTangle Shutdown

A second thematic cluster centers on Meta's specific policy retrenchments and their empirical traceability despite — or because of — data restriction. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] is the anchor study here: using the Meta Content Library itself, it detects that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' reach by 72% roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout, with extremist accounts compensating via posting volume — a finding that simultaneously validates DSA-enabled infrastructure and exposes a transparency deficit in Meta's own disclosures. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] extend this skeptical-but-constructive use of Meta's URL Shares and Privacy-Protected datasets, showing respectively that Facebook's gatekeeping intensifies around political crises (the 2020 "break the glass" period) and that a simple 100-shares anonymization threshold produces spurious cross-country uniformity that could be mistaken for substantive findings — both papers modeling how to use imperfect platform data rigorously while flagging its artifacts.

The collapse of third-party fact-checking receives sustained attention. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] directly empirically tests Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors, finding content removal occurs in only ~30% of verified-false cases and that fact-checkers themselves favor transparency and counter-speech over deletion — while expressing skepticism toward Community Notes as a replacement. [[Holt2026-zq]] complements this from the demand side, showing user-reported "False News" links mostly reflect political disagreement about polarized topics rather than detection of falsity, undercutting the premise that crowd-reporting can substitute for professional verification. [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents the precarious rhetorical position fact-checkers occupy even before this collapse, defending platform funding via differentiation and transcendence strategies against charges of co-optation. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces Meta's definitional drift from "fake news" to "borderline content" across three election cycles, arguing this terminological softening — paired with the shift from engagement to reach metrics — obscures that high-performing borderline content remains overwhelmingly right-wing. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] provides the discursive genealogy underlying all this: Meta's Newsroom systematically frames problematic content as originating from "inauthentic" outside actors, deploying floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behavior" that later diffuse into regulatory language itself — a feedback loop also visible in [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft," where platforms narrate their own policy pivots (including Meta's fact-checking reversal) as continuity rather than rupture.

## Industry Capture of the Evidence Base

A distinct but tightly related thread interrogates not platform policy but platform influence over the science used to evaluate it. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the central metascientific critique of the Meta2020 partnership, arguing that even its most rigorous experiments suffer from low "temporal" and "poetic" validity, and that empirical social science is structurally incapable of keeping pace with platforms that can change overnight — hence his call for mandated self-disclosure by platforms rather than reactive academic study. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] generalize this into a systematic indictment: roughly half of high-profile social media research carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small recurring set of scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, with industry-tied work skewing toward user-blame topics (misinformation sharing) and away from platform-dynamics research — a pattern explicitly likened to tobacco and pharma science. [[Heiss2026-qv]] draws out the policy implication: because platforms uniquely monopolize the data needed to study them, no amount of open-science hygiene (preregistration, disclosure norms) can substitute for independent data access, making DSA-style mandates necessary rather than optional. [[Park2026-tr]] pushes the argument into researchers' lived experience, documenting legal chilling effects from overbroad computer-crime statutes that deter the very independent, adversarial research the DSA is meant to enable.

## Algorithmic Governance as Object of Study

Running alongside the access-and-influence debates is a body of work that uses whatever data remains available to audit platform algorithms directly, implicitly making the case for why access matters. [[Gauthier2026-iq]], [[Efstratiou2025-gs]], and Bouchaud2026-yc each demonstrate concrete political consequences of recommender design on X: algorithmic feeds shift users rightward via persistent following patterns rather than reversible exposure, Musk's own account became newly central to visibility dynamics, and the "Who to Follow" embedding space linearly encodes ideology (ρ=0.887) independent of demographics — with the authors proposing a projection method to debias it. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] and [[Miller2026-cp]] turn to Community Notes specifically, the former showing its bridging-based design structurally undermoderates polarizing content by construction, the latter documenting how Community Notes' new AI-writer API tries to preserve human rating authority while scaling note production — together framing crowdsourced moderation as simultaneously promising and structurally limited, a tension [[Goldberg2026-eb]]'s broader survey of LLM-enabled "digital public square" technologies (bridging systems, collective dialogue, AI-assisted moderation) treats as the next governance frontier. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] supplies the conceptual frame uniting all of these: "reduction" (demotion without removal) is itself an under-scrutinized, largely invisible mode of content governance that resists existing transparency and accountability tools — a diagnosis [[McNally2025-dn]] empirically operationalizes by showing Facebook's News Feed changes produce detectable, lagged effects on hard-news engagement, challenging the "black box" framing and making the case for systemic algorithmic auditing under DSA Article 40(4). [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] remains the benchmark large-N study this literature talks back to, finding stark asymmetric ideological segregation concentrated in a homogeneously conservative corner of Facebook's news ecosystem — a finding [[Rogers2026-zz]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] both extend and complicate using successor datasets.

## Platform-Specific Governance Failures and the Limits of Self-Regulation

Several papers document how self-regulation fails on specific harms, reinforcing the case for external oversight. [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] both show deplatforming and keyword moderation are structurally outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation — jihadist content evades TikTok filters via audio/meme/emoji camouflage, while Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology" persists on YouTube through a decentralized ecosystem of reposts that recommendation algorithms amplify faster than moderators can remove. [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] finds climate misinformation is rarely codified in enforceable platform rules despite public claims of concern, terming this gap "governance-washing." [[Ahuja2025-ku]] and [[Petre2019-zh]] approach platform power from the design and discourse side respectively: the former builds a law-to-design framework mapping dark patterns onto DSA Article 25 autonomy violations (applied directly to the EU's case against X's blue checkmarks), while the latter shows how platforms moralize "gaming the system" accusations to naturalize their own discretionary, paternalistic authority over visibility. [[Moran2025-qn]] and [[Donovan2025-ws]] document the human and organizational retreat underlying all this: trust-and-safety as a profession is being dismantled by layoffs and political pressure, even as Donovan argues misinformation-at-scale is a structural feature of engagement-driven business models that platforms will not voluntarily fix. [[Ventura2026-yc]] and [[Bruns2026-yv]] close out this thread by examining the political consequences of governance ruptures themselves — Brazil's X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet," while Twitter's broader trajectory under Musk is read as the terminal stage of enshittification, with successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) failing to resolve the moderation and governance failures that doomed it.

## Synthesis: Scale, Scams, and the Stakes of Oversight

Finally, several essays step back to ask what all this amounts to. [[Helmond2026-ll]] and [[Boyd2026-op]], writing as twin anniversary reflections, argue that the platforms these governance debates were built to regulate are already mutating past recognition — toward AI-centric infrastructure and "parasocial" consumption respectively — suggesting regulatory frameworks keyed to feed algorithms and API access may be chasing a moving target. [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extend the stakes beyond misinformation to the "scam age," arguing that fraud, like political content moderation, is best addressed by shifting liability to platform gatekeepers rather than individual users — a governance logic structurally identical to the DSA's systemic-risk approach. [[Mahl2026-hc]] and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] provide the expert-consensus and political-stakes bookends: Delphi-method experts rate platform governance reform as the single most important intervention against misinformation, while Lewandowsky frames platform accountability as inseparable from the broader retreat of democracy itself. Read together, this topic's papers trace a single throughline: as platforms' discretionary power over discourse, data, and evidence has grown, so too has an increasingly sophisticated — but still structurally outmatched — infrastructure of regulatory mandate, independent audit, and metascientific scrutiny attempting to hold that power accountable.
