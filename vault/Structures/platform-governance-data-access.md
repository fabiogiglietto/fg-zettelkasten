# Platform Governance and Researcher Data Access

## From Open APIs to the Post-API Age

The documentary backbone of this topic is a historical arc: platforms once treated data access as an act of corporate largesse, and that largesse has been progressively withdrawn, contested, and partially re-imposed by regulation. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the rupture point — Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure — and argues that API-dependent training is structurally fragile. [[Bruns2019-nr]] reframes the same moment as the "APIcalypse," arguing that platforms used Cambridge Analytica as cover to suppress *critical* scholarship specifically, with Social Science One functioning as a gatekept, PR-friendly substitute for genuine access. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodize what follows — laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation, pay-to-play — across Twitter/X, Meta, TikTok, Reddit, and YouTube, showing that CrowdTangle's shutdown, X's $42,000/month Enterprise API, and TikTok's throttled Research API are variations on one structural pattern rather than isolated corporate decisions. Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantify the human cost of this pattern specifically for Twitter studies, with the latter documenting a measurable decline in Twitter-based scholarship after 2023 pricing changes — a decline [[Yang2026-tq]] confirms at the level of the entire social-science literature, showing that empirical social-media research tripled and then plateaued exactly as API regimes tightened, with scholars only beginning to improvise around alternative platforms and donation-based methods.

## The DSA as a Regulatory Answer — and Its Frictions

Article 40 of the EU Digital Services Act is this literature's central regulatory hope, and a cluster of papers interrogates how much that hope survives contact with implementation. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] describe what the law changes in practice for election and platform researchers; [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] argues that the turbulence now visible — platform stalling, legal smokescreens, narrow interpretations — should be read not as regulatory failure but as proof the law is finally biting entrenched power, a "marathon" framing echoed by [[Pierri2026-ib]], who nonetheless catalogs severe practical bottlenecks: slow vetting, circular resource problems, and platforms' capacity to outreach academic correctives with their own PR. [[Annabell2026-ev]] shows that even the category of "data" itself is a contested floating signifier, with Meta, Amazon, and X working to narrow Article 40's scope to quantified, pre-aggregated datasets against broader academic conceptions. [[Peters2026-mo]] documents a parallel struggle over "data quality," largely ignored by EU institutions until academic and NGO stakeholders forced partial recognition into the Delegated Act. Empirically, the promise looks thin: [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's DSA-compliant Research API riddled with unexplained inconsistencies; [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API to be "forgetful by design," systematically losing findable videos within weeks in ways that undermine any DSA-grounded systemic-risk audit; [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this critique to YouTube's content-moderation transparency during EU elections; and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA-mandated transparency data itself to expose stark linguistic inequities in human moderator allocation, with Spanish, Portuguese, and Arabic receiving a fraction of English's coverage. [[Lukito2026-nb]] synthesizes this landscape as a "price, proficiency, or permission" problem that structurally privileges well-resourced, Western researchers, a diagnosis [[Bruns2026-pn]] deepens through an ethnography of Meta Content Library "clean rooms," which trade privacy compliance for the exclusion of qualitative, cross-platform, and non-coding researchers. [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "seeing like an API" framework and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]]'s survey of research tooling both underline that infrastructure — not just legal entitlement — determines what can be known.

## Industry Influence and the Epistemics of Platform-Dependent Research

A second thread interrogates the integrity of knowledge produced under conditions of platform dependency. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document, empirically and historically, how tech firms shape research through suppressed internal findings, selective funding, undisclosed editorial ties, and "performative" collaborations — concluding that roughly 80% of high-profile social-media science carries some undisclosed industry entanglement. [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates this within a longer lineage of tobacco- and pharma-style industry capture, arguing that open-science norms like preregistration cannot compensate for platforms' exclusive control of data and question-framing. [[Munger2025-cz]] turns this critique onto the paradigm case, the Meta2020 election partnership: methodologically ambitious yet underpowered, conceptually incoherent across papers, and structurally incapable of producing durable knowledge about platforms that change faster than peer review — a verdict [[Allen2025-ot]] revisits more sympathetically in assessing platform-independent, LLM-based experimental alternatives as a necessary adaptation to closed APIs. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] offers a concrete illustration of how platform-engineering choices (a 100-share anonymization threshold) can silently distort cross-national research findings, while [[Park2026-tr]] documents the chilling legal risks — CFAA-style prosecution threats, stockpiled undisclosed findings, career damage — that independent researchers face simply for using unsanctioned methods to study platforms that will not grant sanctioned ones.

## Case Studies in Platform Policy Change: Meta's Retreat from Moderation

Several papers converge on Meta's 2021–2025 pivot away from active moderation as a paradigmatic test of what regulated transparency can and cannot reveal. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses the Meta Content Library itself to detect the undisclosed rollout of Meta's political-content-reduction policy in Italy — months before Meta's official timeline — evidencing both a transparency deficit and the research value of DSA-enabled infrastructure. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends this into a broader argument that Facebook actively curates (rather than neutrally transmits) visibility, with partisan and journalistic-quality effects fluctuating in step with known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces Meta's shrinking vocabulary for the same phenomenon — from "fake news" to "false news" to "misinformation" to "borderline" — alongside its pivot from engagement to reach metrics, showing both narrative depoliticization and the persistence of right-wing dominance among top-performing borderline content. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] interrogate the endgame of this trajectory: the end of Meta's Third-Party Fact-Checking Program and the shift to Community Notes and user reports, with the former showing content removal was always rare and the latter showing user "false news" flags mostly register political disagreement rather than falsity. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] supplies the discursive prehistory, showing how Meta's Newsroom long externalized blame onto "inauthentic" outside actors while erasing the role of its own Groups and ranking architecture — a strategic self-narration generalized by [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" across Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X. [[Donovan2025-ws]] frames the whole arc as evidence that "misinformation-at-scale" is a structural feature, not a bug, of engagement-driven business models that platforms will only ever partially and reversibly correct, while [[Gillespie2022-jx]] supplies the conceptual key that much of this unfolds as invisible "reduction" rather than visible removal — a form of governance nearly impossible for outside researchers to measure precisely because it leaves no absent content behind.

## Conceptualizing the Object: Platforms, Infrastructure, and Discourse

Underpinning these empirical interventions is a smaller set of foundational and reflexive texts. [[Gillespie2010-as]] remains the theoretical anchor for seeing "platform" itself as strategic rhetoric that elides curatorial power, a claim [[van-Dijck2018-up]] scales into a full societal thesis about the contest between private platform logics and public values. [[Boyd2026-op]] revisits this vocabulary two decades on, arguing that "social media" has become a misnomer for "parasocial media" dominated by scrolling and influencer content — a diagnosis with direct governance stakes, since what counts as "harm" or "discourse" to regulate depends on what the platform actually is. [[Tornberg2026-im]] pushes the periodization forward again, arguing that the "platform society" is giving way to an "AI society" of cognitive lock-in and alignment politics, for which the DSA's hard-won data-access architecture has, as yet, no equivalent for large language models — a regulatory blind spot [[Goldberg2026-eb]] approaches more optimistically, surveying LLM-enabled bridging, dialogue, and moderation tools as a potential "second paradigm shift" for digital public squares, conditional on augmenting rather than replacing human deliberation.

## Algorithmic Visibility, Audits, and Moderation at the Edges

A dense empirical cluster uses audit methods to make opaque ranking and moderation systems legible. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] both probe X's recommender internals post-Musk, the former showing algorithmic amplification of agitating content and Musk-proximity rather than pure partisanship, the latter revealing that X's embeddings inadvertently encode a near-perfectly linear ideological dimension with implications for GDPR-style profiling law. [[McNally2025-dn]] demonstrates that Facebook's News Feed, far from an unknowable "black box," leaves statistically detectable traces of strategic ranking interventions — direct evidence for the feasibility of DSA Article 40(4) systemic audits. [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Karo2026-dn]] extend audit methods to extremist and manosphere content on YouTube and TikTok, showing that deplatforming and keyword moderation are consistently outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation and adversarial vernacular tactics shared across ideological movements. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] bring this scrutiny to generative search, finding Google's AI Overviews inconsistently guarded against emergent conspiracy framing and discursively positioning Google as both commercial platform and epistemic authority. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds methodologically, building random-sampling tools (TubeStats, TokStats) to supply the "denominators" that keyword-based and API-constrained research otherwise lacks, while [[Votta2025-xz]] shows that even paid political advertising — ostensibly the most transparent platform product — is shaped by opaque delivery algorithms beyond advertiser control.

## Crowdsourced Alternatives and Their Limits

Community Notes emerges as the signature replacement for professional and algorithmic moderation, and receives sustained scrutiny. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows its bridging-based design systematically undermoderates polarizing content exactly where electoral stakes are highest, while [[Miller2026-cp]] offers the platform's own account of scaling the system via an open AI-writer API, framing human rating authority as the system's democratic safeguard. Both studies, read against [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]]'s finding that professional fact-checkers view Community Notes as vulnerable to coordinated political manipulation, suggest crowdsourced moderation substitutes one set of governance opacities for another.

## Shutdowns, Bans, and the Politics of Platform Exit

A further cluster examines what happens when access to a platform itself — not just to its data — is foreclosed. [[Ventura2026-yc]] shows Brazil's 2024 ban on X produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as compliance diverged by partisanship, while [[Wang2026-ub]] documents the largely failed migration of academic Twitter users to Mastodon, and [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter/X's broader institutional decline as a case of platform "enshittification" that successor platforms have so far failed to remedy through moderation design. [[Moran2025-qn]] reports on the related collapse of the professional Trust & Safety field itself, suggesting that governance capacity, not just access policy, is being hollowed out industry-wide.

## Harms the Access Regime Is Meant to Illuminate

Finally, several papers supply the substantive stakes that make data-access politics consequential rather than merely procedural. [[Mahl2026-hc]] synthesizes expert consensus that platform governance reform ranks among the most important levers against conspiracy and misinformation, ahead of individual-level literacy interventions. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] ties platform dynamics directly to global democratic backsliding. [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extend the governance lens to the "scam economy" and AI-driven financial fraud, arguing that anti-fraud infrastructures double as surveillance and that liability should shift from victims to platform gatekeepers. [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] show how propaganda and "deceptive information operations" exploit the very infrastructures — hashtags, groups, engagement ranking — that platforms claim support authentic discourse, a structural reading that Meta's own enforcement categories, per Giada et al., cannot capture. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] operationalizes DSA Article 25's autonomy protections against dark-pattern design, directly informing enforcement against cases like X's verification redesign. [[Balluff2026-bv]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] use computational methods to detect covert media capture and alternative-media network structures that bear on how "problematic" influence is defined and regulated. [[Richter2026-bt]] traces how divergent US and German stakeholder ecologies in early AI discourse prefigure today's regulatory asymmetries, while [[Crosset2026-mq]] and [[Bechmann2026-dr]] offer the most synthetic theoretical moves — reading DSA-style transparency mandates as a form of "security bureaucratisation" that turns platforms into quasi-public infrastructures, and arguing that researchers should study platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure rather than isolate causal effects. [[Schulte2026-df]], [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]], [[Farkas2026-lr]], [[Oswald2025-km]], and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] round out the picture from the demand side — documenting the infrastructural bottlenecks of multi-platform election research, the feasibility of independent ad-auditing before DSA existed, fact-checkers' precarious rhetorical dependence on the very platforms they monitor, the production-consumption skew that makes visible discourse unrepresentative of public opinion, and the fragmented state of "algorithm literacy" as a precondition for any of this governance actually reaching citizens. Together, this corpus traces a single, recursive argument: every advance in researcher access (CrowdTangle, Social Science One, Academic APIs, Article 40) has been followed by a retrenchment, and each retrenchment has, in turn, generated the next wave of methodological improvisation, audit, and regulatory demand — a cycle this topic's papers show no sign of having reached equilibrium.
