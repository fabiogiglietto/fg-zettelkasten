# Platform Governance & Research Data Access

## The arc of the field: from open data to walled gardens, and the politics in between

This corpus tells a story with a clear narrative spine: a golden age of relatively open platform data, a traumatic rupture (the "APIcalypse"), and a contested, still-unfolding attempt to rebuild researcher access through regulation. [[Gillespie2010-as]] and [[van-Dijck2018-up]] supply the conceptual ground floor — platforms as discursively self-legitimating, value-laden infrastructures rather than neutral conduits — on which almost everything else builds. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the inflection point (Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure) and [[Bruns2019-nr]] diagnoses it as a deliberate, not merely privacy-motivated, move to frustrate critical scholarship. [[Freelon2024-sc]], [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], and Bastos2025-ya periodize this longer history across platforms, converging on the same moral: access has always been contingent, "independence by permission," reversible at the whim of ownership or scandal. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and [[Yang2026-tq]] quantify the resulting scholarly damage bibliometrically — a 25%-per-year research boom cut short by API paywalls and plateauing/declining output precisely when Twitter/X and Facebook data dried up.

## Regulation as the proposed fix — and its friction

The EU Digital Services Act, especially Article 40, is the corpus's dominant regulatory hope. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[Lukito2026-nb]] all treat Article 40 as a genuine paradigm shift — from platform philanthropy to legal entitlement — while cataloguing the friction of implementation: narrow eligibility interpretations, slow vetting, inconsistent response times, and platforms treating "data" itself as a contestable category, as [[Annabell2026-ev]] shows through its discourse analysis of the term's deliberate ambiguity in legal text. [[Peters2026-mo]] extends this by showing that "data quality" was similarly absent from official DSA language until academic/NGO stakeholders fought it into the Delegated Regulation's recitals — evidence that even the terms of transparency are themselves negotiated terrain. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] push past the optimistic reading: the EU's formal proceedings against X, TikTok, and Meta for Article 40 breaches, and the restrictive "clean room" architecture of the Meta Content Library, show that mandated access does not guarantee *useful* access — reinforcing a two-tier, WEIRD-centric research ecology that [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] both flag as a structural equity problem. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]] offer concrete audits (TikTok's Research API, YouTube's search endpoint) demonstrating that even "compliant" APIs remain unreliable instruments — YouTube's is "forgetful by design," decaying within weeks. [[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] extend the audit logic to moderation itself, finding inconsistent publisher labeling and stark linguistic disparities in human moderator allocation that DSA transparency reports don't adequately surface. [[Crosset2026-mq]] zooms out theoretically, reading DSA-style regulation alongside US state laws as competing "circulation regimes" united by a shared logic of security bureaucratisation — transparency as a governance technology in its own right.

## Industry capture of the research enterprise

A second cluster interrogates not platform data access per se but the scientific field's dependence on platforms as institutional actors. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] form a tight sequence: the latter empirically demonstrates that roughly half of high-profile platform research has undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small recurring cohort who also staff editorial and review positions — "industrial saturation" approaching 80%. [[Munger2025-cz]] and [[Allen2025-ot]] offer complementary metascientific critiques of the Meta2020 collaboration specifically, arguing that even well-resourced, consensus-driven platform partnerships produce narrow, temporally fragile findings, while [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "seeing like an API" framework shows how CrowdTangle's technical and governance design shaped not just what could be studied but who could study it — researchers' adaptive workarounds simultaneously resisting and entrenching platform epistemic control. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]] ground this in hard methodological detail, showing how Meta's URL Shares Dataset threshold artifacts (the 100-public-shares cutoff) distort cross-country and misinformation-prevalence estimates in ways orthogonal to the "real" phenomena researchers think they're measuring.

## What gets lost: moderation, algorithms, and the political consequences of platform retreat

A third strand uses newly available (if imperfect) data to show what's actually happening inside platforms' black boxes — and what happens when moderation infrastructure is withdrawn. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] theorizes "reduction" (algorithmic demotion short of removal) as an under-scrutinized governance mode, a frame empirically cashed out by [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], which uses the Meta Content Library to show Meta's political content reduction policy cut Italian MPs' reach by 72% while extremist accounts compensated through volume — and that Meta failed to disclose this in DSA filings, a direct transparency-deficit indictment. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces Meta's shifting "fake news → misinformation → borderline" vocabulary across three elections, finding problematic content's performance declining by 2024 even as what remains successful skews sharply right-wing — echoed by [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]]'s finding that partisan content faces an algorithmically-dampened "reach penalty" that intensifies around election periods, evidence against purely structural (homophily-based) explanations. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] provide experimental and observational evidence that X's recommender system causally and asymmetrically amplifies conservative/agitating content, complicating the FIES's earlier null findings ([[Munger2025-cz]]; [[Allen2025-ot]]), while [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] finds X's recommender inadvertently encodes a robust linear ideology signal with GDPR-relevant implications. [[McNally2025-dn]] challenges the "black box" framing directly, showing Facebook's News Feed algorithm leaves detectable, laggedtraces in engagement data — an argument for systemic algorithmic auditing under DSA Article 40(4). [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] analyze the platforms' own self-narration of these changes, showing how Meta's Newsroom and cross-platform "changecraft" discourse displace responsibility onto "inauthentic" outside actors while obscuring platform-design and business-model culpability.

## The post-fact-checking moment and its consequences

Meta's 2025 abandonment of third-party fact-checking for Community Notes anchors a cluster of papers assessing what moderation retreat means in practice. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically dismantles Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing, showing fact-checkers never had removal power and that only ~30% of flagged content was ever taken down; [[Holt2026-zq]] complements this by showing user "False News" reports track political disagreement rather than falsity, raising doubts about crowd-based moderation as a substitute. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines fact-checkers' own rhetorical self-defense of platform funding dependency, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] and [[Miller2026-cp]] offer dueling technical assessments of Community Notes/bridging-based systems — one finding structural undermoderation of polarizing content by design, the other (from inside X) documenting AI-assisted scaling that preserves human rating authority. [[Moran2025-qn]] and [[Donovan2025-ws]] zoom out to the "trust and safety" profession's broader destabilization and the historical construction of "misinformation-at-scale" as inseparable from engagement-driven business models.

## Harms at the edges: extremism, scams, and deepfakes exploiting governance gaps

A final set of papers shows what proliferates in the gaps left by retreating moderation and contested regulation. [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] document jihadist and manosphere content respectively exploiting TikTok's and YouTube's recommendation architectures through vernacular camouflage that outpaces removal; [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] generalizes this into "Deceptive Information Operations," arguing existing CIB/false-news taxonomies miss manipulation that doesn't require false content at all. [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extend the governance-gap argument to the "scam economy," arguing anti-fraud infrastructure itself becomes a surveillance and governance mechanism, with deepfake-enabled fraud exposing jurisdictional fragmentation and Section 230 liability shields. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers a theoretical corrective for attributing coordinated manipulation under data scarcity. [[Ventura2026-yc]] and [[Bruns2026-yv]] close the loop at the level of whole-platform governance, showing that state bans (Brazil's X ban) and platform mismanagement (Twitter's "death") each reshape the partisan architecture of public debate in path-dependent, not easily reversible ways.

## Synthesis and infrastructure-building responses

Several papers propose constructive paths forward rather than diagnosis alone: [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats random-sampling tools address the "denominator problem" left by opportunistic APIs; [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Bechmann2026-dr]] call for treating multi-platform observability as reusable democratic infrastructure rather than one-off studies; [[Goldberg2026-eb]] speculates on LLM-enabled bridging and deliberation systems as a "second paradigm shift" for digital public squares; and [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study crystallizes expert consensus that platform governance reform, not individual-level literacy fixes, is the top research priority. [[Park2026-tr]] reminds us that all this work carries real legal risk for researchers themselves, while [[Vertesi2026-lv]] and [[Petre2019-zh]] supply critical-theoretical bookends — warning that accountability discourse itself can be captured as a "decoy" that legitimizes underlying platform power, and that platforms' moralizing language about "gaming the system" masks their own economic interests. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] serve as connective tissue, tracking the field's own evolving self-understanding of data access as a condition of political communication scholarship itself.
