# Platform Governance & Data Access

# Platform Governance & Data Access

## The closing of the open-API era and its scholarly costs

The papers filed here trace a shared origin story: a "Golden Age" of relatively open platform data followed by systematic closure. Bastos2025-ya and [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrate Twitter's arc from an unusually open research infrastructure to a commercially paywalled and politically degraded platform under Musk, while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the consequence bibliometrically: a 25% annual growth rate in Twitter-based scholarship through 2022 collapsing into decline once Enterprise API pricing hit $42,000/month. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes this history across six major platforms, arguing that data access is now permanently contingent on platform whim rather than settled infrastructure, and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] extends the framing to Twitter/X specifically, tracing "Wild West" to "Walled Garden" phases and showing the pattern (Meta's CrowdTangle closure, Reddit's pricing, TikTok's restrictions) is structural, not idiosyncratic. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review supplies the macro-empirical confirmation: reliance on social-media data across social science tripled through the early 2020s before plateauing and declining in 2022–2024, precisely as APIs closed. Together these pieces establish the volume's baseline diagnosis — that platform-controlled data access is inherently fragile, and that this fragility has already measurably shrunk what can be known about digital public life.

## The DSA as a contested regulatory fix

Against this backdrop, the EU's Digital Services Act — especially Article 40 — emerges as the central regulatory intervention under scrutiny. Several papers assess it optimistically-but-critically: [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] describe how Article 40 reopens research possibilities for platform and election studies while flagging uneven implementation; [[Pierri2025-hm]] and its near-duplicate [[Pierri2026-ib]], drawn from direct EU policy engagement, catalog implementation bottlenecks — misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, a "circular problem" disadvantaging under-funded researchers, and the looming blind spot of LLMs as unregulated systemic-risk actors. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] offers the volume's most explicitly strategic reading: current turbulence around Article 40 (platform resistance, procedural friction) should be read not as regulatory failure but as evidence the law is finally biting on real power asymmetries, and the correct posture is to "stand firm" rather than retreat. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] complicate the picture by documenting a bifurcated global research infrastructure — EU-only legal access alongside expensive commercial channels — that reproduces WEIRD-centric exclusion, a concern also central to [[Bruns2026-pn]]'s critique of "clean room" access environments (notably the Meta Content Library), which argues that mandating access is not the same as making it usable, since clean rooms privilege code-fluent, well-resourced, quantitative researchers over qualitative, cross-platform, or Global South scholars. [[Crosset2026-mq]] steps back to theorize the DSA alongside US state legislation as one of three competing "circulation regimes" (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimisation) converging on a shared logic of security bureaucratisation — transparency and auditing as modes of governing platforms at a distance rather than directly regulating speech.

## Auditing the auditors: does mandated access actually deliver?

A cluster of empirical audits tests whether DSA-mandated transparency mechanisms function as intended, generally finding they fall short. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] documents persistent limitations in TikTok's expanded Research API despite DSA compliance pressure; [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," systematically undercounting older videos in ways that undermine studies of past events and DSA-relevant systemic-risk research; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data itself to expose major cross-lingual disparities in human moderation staffing, with Global South languages receiving a fraction of English's coverage; and [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that "data quality," central to whether Article 40 access is meaningful, was largely absent from official EU deliberation and had to be forced onto the agenda by academic and NGO stakeholders against platform resistance (Meta notably inverted the argument to claim access was futile). [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds pragmatically by building independent infrastructure (TubeStats/TokStats) to supply representative denominators that neither platform APIs nor DSA portals currently provide. [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends the audit logic to YouTube's content-moderation claims around the 2024 EU elections, finding inconsistent application of "authoritative source" labeling and opaque removal communications, and explicitly calls transparency "observability" — a property of infrastructure, not a checkbox. Cumulatively, these audits argue that legal mandates for access are necessary but insufficient without scrutiny of what the access actually enables.

## Industry capture of the research enterprise

A second major thread interrogates not access mechanics but epistemic independence. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] provide the most systematic indictment: roughly half of high-profile social media research in top journals has disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, concentrated among a small repeatedly-engaged elite who also dominate editorial gatekeeping — yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the field. [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates this within a comparative frame (tobacco, pharma, food industries), arguing platforms' exclusive control of data creates unprecedented dependency. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers a related but distinct critique of the landmark Meta2020 partnership, arguing that even well-resourced, methodologically ambitious collaborations produce estimates with poor temporal and "poetic" validity, because platforms change faster than science can characterize them — concluding that proactive regulatory disclosure obligations, not reactive research, are the only viable path. [[Allen2025-ot]] frames platform-independent experimental methods (browser extensions, LLM-based content reranking) as a necessary adaptation to this closing, uncooperative environment. [[Park2026-tr]] adds a chilling, complementary dimension: researchers conducting the very independent audits this literature calls for face serious legal risk under overbroad computer-crime and IP statutes, producing self-censorship, abandoned projects, and even prosecutions — showing that data-access politics extend into researchers' personal legal exposure.

## Meta as case study: policy opacity and discursive management

Several papers use Meta specifically as a site for exposing the gap between platform communication and platform practice. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows how Meta's Newsroom discursively externalizes blame for problematic content onto "inauthentic" outside actors while eliding its own algorithmic and Groups-driven contributions, with key terms like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" migrating into regulatory language. [[De2026-ld]] generalizes this into "changecraft," a cross-platform repertoire of discursive strategies (visible aesthetics, continuity-framing, patchworking) that manages perception of change while consolidating platform power — directly relevant to Meta's fact-checking reversal and X's post-acquisition messaging. Empirically, [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses the Meta Content Library to show Meta's political-content-reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' reach by up to 72%, beginning roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout and going undisclosed in DSA compliance reports — a direct empirical demonstration of the transparency deficits others theorize. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] similarly use longitudinal engagement data (CrowdTangle, the URL Shares Dataset) to detect hidden algorithmic interventions, arguing the News Feed is not an unknowable "black box" but an auditable sociotechnical system if researchers have sustained access — while also warning that dataset engineering choices (like the 100-share threshold) can themselves distort what researchers infer. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] pushes this further, showing partisan-alignment penalties and journalistic-quality rewards for reach fluctuate in lockstep with known governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures), evidencing active curation rather than neutral conduits. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] then examine the aftermath of Meta's January 2025 fact-checking rollback: the former shows removal was always rare (~30%) and fact-checkers favor transparency over deletion, rejecting Zuckerberg's censorship framing; the latter shows user-flagged "False News" mostly reflects political disagreement about polarized topics rather than detection of falsity, raising doubts about Community-Notes-style crowdsourced replacement. [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "Seeing like an API" study of CrowdTangle users' lived experience closes the loop, showing how the tool's technical design and governance rules actively shaped what researchers could see and conclude — a microcosm of platform-mediated epistemic control.

## Crowdsourced moderation, bans, and downstream democratic effects

A final cluster examines governance mechanisms once removed from direct platform control. [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] scrutinize X's Community Notes, finding respectively that Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under a bridging algorithm (undermining "biased fact-checker" narratives) and that the same bridging design structurally undermoderates polarizing content by requiring cross-partisan agreement — a design flaw with electoral stakes. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] extends audit methodology to recommender internals, showing X's "Who to Follow" embeddings inadvertently encode users' political ideology with striking fidelity, raising GDPR/DSA profiling concerns and demonstrating a removal method that preserves relevance while stripping political signal. [[Ventura2026-yc]] examines platform bans as a governance tool, finding Brazil's 2024 X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as compliance itself became partisan. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] operationalizes DSA Article 25's dark-pattern provisions into a design-factor framework applicable to live enforcement (the EU's case against X's paid verification). [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] show moderation's limits against adversarial and diffuse content ecosystems (jihadist TikTok vernaculars; Andrew Tate's post-deplatforming "ambient" persistence on YouTube), arguing recommendation-driven circulation structurally outpaces removal. [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the institutional retrenchment behind these failures — the trust-and-safety profession itself destabilizing under political and economic pressure — while [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes the whole arc, arguing misinformation-at-scale is not a bug but a feature of engagement-driven business models that platforms only ever address behaviorally, never structurally, and increasingly abandon under political backlash.

## Synthesis and forward-looking framings

Several pieces attempt to step back and reframe the field's normative stakes. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues platform research should move beyond narrow causal-effects designs toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] anticipates many of these concerns pre-DSA, identifying blurred political-organizational boundaries, opaque micro-targeting, and strategic amplification as core methodological challenges. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] looks forward rather than backward, proposing LLM-enabled deliberation, bridging, and moderation tools as a potential "second paradigm shift" for digital public squares, tempered by concerns about synthetic participation and legitimacy. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study and [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s "synthetic reality" framework both diagnose a darkening horizon — expert consensus that things will not improve without coordinated multi-level governance, and a warning that generative AI's erosion of verifiability may impose a permanent "epistemic tax." [[Waight2026-ts]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extend platform-governance concerns into adjacent AI-driven harms (state-influenced LLM outputs; deepfake financial fraud), arguing that the same gatekeeper-accountability logic developed for social media data access must extend to AI training pipelines and scam infrastructures. [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] round out the picture by showing how governance failures manifest economically — scams as a structuring logic of platform economies, and algorithmic ad-delivery systems shaping political campaign costs in ways invisible to advertisers and regulators alike. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] serve as bookending case studies — a survey of the current API/tooling landscape, and an early (2018) demonstration that independent, volunteer-based auditing was already filling gaps platforms refused to close, prefiguring the entire DSA-era apparatus this note has traced.
