# Platform Governance & Researcher Data Access

## The arc of a field losing, then reclaiming, its object of study

The papers filed here trace a single unfolding story: a research field built on ad hoc, platform-granted access to data suddenly had that access withdrawn, and has spent the last several years trying to build something more durable — legally, infrastructurally, and epistemically — in its place. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and Bastos2025-ya provide the historical spine, periodizing the shift from an open "Wild West" API era through "academic cooperation" to today's fragmented regime of pay-to-play APIs, walled gardens, and unofficial workarounds. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] retells this history specifically for Twitter/X, showing that what looks like platform-specific drama (Musk's shutdown of the Academic API) is actually an instance of a structural pattern repeated across Meta's CrowdTangle closure, Reddit's paywall, and TikTok's restrictions. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] supplies the bibliometric proof of concept: Twitter-based scholarship grew 25% annually for over a decade, then stagnated and declined once Enterprise pricing hit $42,000/month — a "eulogy" corroborated at broader scale by [[Yang2026-tq]]'s finding that social-media-based social science research plateaued and fell in 2022–2024 across disciplines. [[Bruns2026-yv]] and [[Swartz2026-zb]] frame this as part of a larger platform trajectory — Twitter's "death" as public sphere, and platforms' migration toward extractive, engagement-driven business logics — while [[Helmond2026-ll]] situates today's access retrenchment within a broader "AI-centered" phase of platformization in which behavioral inference increasingly substitutes for the open-web signals researchers once observed.

## CrowdTangle's shutdown as a case study in platform-mediated knowledge

Several papers zoom in on CrowdTangle as the emblematic casualty of this transition. [[Cullen2026-cb]] develops a "seeing like an API" framework from interviews with CrowdTangle users, showing how the tool's technical design and governance rules shaped not just what data researchers could get but what questions they could even ask. [[Bruns2026-pn]] follows the story forward into CrowdTangle's replacement, the Meta Content Library, arguing that its "clean room" architecture — mandatory data deletion, restrictive coding environments, exclusion of qualitative tools — represents a new mode of platform control that satisfies legal mandates for access while structurally disadvantaging qualitative, cross-platform, and Global South research. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] offers a cautionary methodological coda from the earlier Social Science One URL Shares Dataset: even privacy-preserving engineering choices (the 100-share anonymization threshold) can introduce platform-wide artifacts that masquerade as social signal, a warning about trusting platform-mediated data at face value. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds to this whole landscape by building independent infrastructure (TubeStats, TokStats) that manufactures defensible random samples of YouTube and TikTok precisely because neither platform's "research API" offers anything of the kind — a diagnosis echoed forcefully by [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s audit of YouTube's search API (finding it "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay and irreproducibility) and by [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]]'s critique of TikTok's Research API as failing to deliver on DSA promises.

## The DSA as the field's great regulatory experiment

Against this backdrop of platform retrenchment, the EU's Digital Services Act — and Article 40 in particular — emerges as the central regulatory intervention the corpus is watching, testing, and arguing about. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] together form something like a running commentary on Article 40's promise and friction: it is a "landmark" shift from discretionary "data philanthropy" to a binding legal right, yet implementation is bedeviled by misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, circular funding problems, slow vetting, and platform obstruction. De Vreese and Tromble's contribution is notably contrarian in tone — arguing that visible turbulence is evidence the regulation is *working*, biting on real platform power, not proof of failure. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] extend this into concrete monitoring of compliance, including EU proceedings against X, TikTok, and Meta for Article 40.12 breaches. [[Peters2026-mo]] adds an underappreciated angle: the fight over what counts as "data quality" in the Delegated Regulation was itself a site of stakeholder contestation, with Meta strategically inverting researchers' own arguments to claim access would be futile. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] show DSA obligations (Article 25's autonomy protections, profiling concerns) generating new HCI and audit methodologies aimed squarely at enforcement. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] use DSA-mandated transparency data itself as a research resource — exposing, respectively, glaring linguistic disparities in moderation staffing and inconsistent application of authoritative-source labeling on YouTube — demonstrating that even imperfect transparency regimes generate auditable traces. [[Crosset2026-mq]] pulls back to compare the DSA against US state and German legislation, arguing all these regimes converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" that reconstitutes platforms as quasi-public infrastructures via reporting and auditing rather than direct content control.

## Political content policy as object and as case: the researcher's own vantage point

Several papers examine specific platform policy shifts using exactly the kind of DSA-enabled infrastructure under debate, effectively modeling what "meaningful" data access can produce. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] is the pivotal case: using the Meta Content Library to trace Meta's political content reduction policy in Italy, it finds the policy began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout, suppressed mainstream parliamentarians' reach by up to 72%, and left a transparency gap that Meta never disclosed in its own DSA compliance reporting — while extremist accounts adapted by posting more. This is paired with [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], which uses Meta's Privacy-Protected URLs Dataset to show that partisan and quality effects on visibility fluctuate in lockstep with known governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures), evidence that Facebook is an active curator, not a neutral conduit — directly complicating the influential 2020 Meta-academic election studies. [[McNally2025-dn]] complements this with a longitudinal audit showing Facebook's News Feed algorithm treats "hard news" distinctly from other content with detectable ~19-24 day lags, arguing this undermines the "black box" framing and legitimates DSA-based systemic audits as a matter of principle, not just aspiration. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] performs an analogous natural experiment on Musk-era Twitter, showing algorithmic visibility gains for "agitating" content and proximity to Musk himself rather than partisanship per se. Together these studies model a research program explicitly premised on treating platform policy change as an observable, auditable object — precisely the mandate DSA researcher access is meant to serve, and precisely the kind of study threatened when such access disappears (as in [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s study of Brazil's X ban producing durable partisan "sorting ratchets," conducted despite, not because of, platform cooperation).

## Industry entanglement, epistemic dependency, and the limits of collaboration

A distinct thread interrogates the *terms* of platform-researcher collaboration itself, independent of formal access mechanisms. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a sobering meta-scientific verdict on the Meta2020 partnership: even well-resourced, methodologically ambitious collaboration produces findings with poor temporal and external validity, because platforms change faster than science can track them — an argument for proactive regulatory mandates rather than reactive study. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] quantify a structural version of this problem: roughly half of high-profile social media research has undisclosed industry ties, editorial and peer-review roles are similarly compromised, and industry-tied work skews toward user-focused topics (misinformation sharing) at the expense of platform-dynamics research — a bias with clear resonance to debates elsewhere in this collection about what DSA-enabled research can and cannot see. [[Heiss2026-qv]] draws the explicit parallel to tobacco and pharma science, arguing that DSA-style mandated access plus stronger disclosure norms are the two-track solution. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines an adjacent dependency: European fact-checkers' rhetorical defense of their own platform funding relationships, showing how an ostensibly independent accountability institution manages its own entanglement with the very companies it monitors. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] analyze platforms' own discursive self-management of these controversies — Meta's Newsroom externalizing blame onto "inauthentic" actors, and a broader "changecraft" vocabulary describing how platforms narrate policy change as continuity or benevolence to defuse scrutiny — while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] documents the endpoint of this dynamic empirically, showing Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors is largely unsupported by what actually happens to debunked content post-verification.

## Beyond APIs: legal risk, alternative methods, and the shape of a post-API research agenda

The remaining papers sketch what researchers do, and what they risk, once conventional access fails. [[Park2026-tr]] documents the chilling effects and legal jeopardy (CFAA, CMA) facing social computing researchers who scrape or otherwise circumvent platform terms of service — a sobering counterpoint to calls for independent "unpermissioned" research such as [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s. [[Lukito2026-nb]] surveys 72 data-access tools and diagnoses a "price, proficiency, or permission" triad of barriers that structurally excludes under-resourced and non-Western researchers, echoing [[Bruns2026-pn]]'s and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]]'s warnings about a WEIRD-skewed "data abyss." [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] apply these constraints to concrete election-research cases (the German 2025 federal election; DSA-era API portals), arguing platform observability itself must be treated as reusable research infrastructure rather than a one-off project cost. [[Allen2025-ot]] highlights platform-independent experimentation (browser extensions plus LLM reranking) as a promising methodological workaround when platform cooperation is unavailable, while [[Bechmann2026-dr]] proposes reconceiving platforms' collective behavior itself as "democratic infrastructure," moving past narrow causal-effects paradigms. Finally, [[Mahl2026-hc]] and [[Goldberg2026-eb]] gesture toward the field's normative horizon — expert consensus that platform governance reform (not just individual-level literacy fixes) is the top-rated intervention against misinformation, and a speculative agenda for AI-mediated "digital public squares" (bridging systems, community notes, collective dialogue) that could reshape governance questions yet again. Threaded through cases like [[Bouchaud2026-np]] (Community Notes' structural undermoderation of polarizing content), [[Karo2026-dn]] (jihadist exploitation of TikTok's moderation architecture), and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]]'s "Zombie Internet" of coordinated deceptive operations, the collection converges on a shared diagnosis: platform self-governance and voluntary transparency have consistently proven insufficient, and the DSA's Article 40 — however halting in practice — represents the field's most serious attempt yet to convert researcher access from platform favor into public right.
