# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The Shape of the Field

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as a kind of meta-commentary that frames the whole set: it argues that misinformation research is a younger sibling of climate psychology, and should borrow the older field's hard-won lessons — attend to motivational and social drivers rather than pure information deficits, measure real behaviour rather than cognitive proxies, and be wary of both under-generalising from US/online samples and of corporate entanglement in intervention research. Read against the other eight papers, this piece supplies the diagnostic vocabulary for judging them: which interventions target beliefs versus behaviour, which generalise across contexts, and which risk overclaiming from lab-bound or short-term effects. It also foreshadows a recurring finding across the set — that belief change and behaviour change are frequently decoupled, and that "effective" is a much lower bar than "consequential."

## Content and Framing: How Far Can a Message Alone Go?

[[Voelkel2026-lc]] provides the most sobering empirical test of message-based intervention at scale: a megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies finds real but small (1–4 percentage point) attitudinal effects, near-total absence of partisan heterogeneity (undercutting theories that demand audience-tailored framing), and — crucially — no effect whatsoever on costly behaviour (donations), with some messages even backfiring. This directly operationalises the concern Spampatti et al. raise about behavioural proxies: attitude shifts and donation shifts were uncorrelated or negatively related, exposing a stark attitude–behaviour gap for short-form persuasion. [[Lieu2025-nl]] complements this from the misinformation side, showing that it is *what* a false claim targets, not *how* it is logically malformed, that drives perceived veracity and political polarization; fallacy type (FLICC) had no effect, while content category (CARDS) — especially claims attacking climate solutions or scientists — did, and did so asymmetrically by ideology. Together these two papers suggest that both pro-climate and anti-climate short-form messaging operate more through content-specific, identity-resonant levers than through generic rhetorical structure, and that neither domain shows much promise for shifting hard behavioural outcomes with a single message exposure.

## Inoculation: From the Lab to the Feed and the Chat Window

A second cluster tests whether *pre-emptive* exposure — inoculation — can do better than one-shot persuasion. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] moves inoculation out of the lab entirely, embedding a 19-second prebunking ad directly in Instagram feeds at scale (n=375,597) and showing a durable, 21-point improvement in detecting emotional manipulation that persists five months later and drives more information-seeking — a rare demonstration of ecological validity and durability at once, and a direct answer to Spampatti et al.'s call for real-world, high-impact outcome measures. [[Szabo2026-rd]] pushes inoculation into interactive territory, introducing "Conversational Inoculation" via an LLM chatbot and finding that, once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled, dialogic inoculation outperforms both traditional reading (supportive) and writing (refutational) inoculation. Qualitative analysis suggests the advantage lies not in argument content per se but in interaction design — adaptability, trust-building, and minimizing friction — which reframes inoculation efficacy as partly an HCI problem, not purely a message-content problem. Read together, these two papers trace an arc from passive, broadcast-style prebunking (effective, cheap, scalable, but generic) toward adaptive, personalized dialogue (more effective per individual, but harder to scale and evaluate).

## Conversational AI as a Persuasive and Trust-Building Agent

The chatbot thread deepens further with [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]], both of which test whether sustained, evidence-based AI dialogue — rather than brief inoculation — can shift entrenched beliefs. Costello, Pennycook and Rand's finding that a three-round GPT-4 conversation durably cuts conspiracy belief by ~20%, generalising even to unrelated conspiracies and surviving two months, directly challenges motivational accounts of belief entrenchment and instead supports an analytic-reasoning view: people update when evidence is sufficiently detailed and personalized, not categorically resistant to facts. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic beyond conspiracy theories to structurally different contested domains (climate, vaccination, inequality), finding that brief GPT-5 dialogues shift both beliefs and policy support, with the largest gains precisely among initial skeptics and those higher in trust in science — the audiences classic message-based campaigns (per [[Voelkel2026-lc]]) struggle to move. Notably, Kotz et al. show the model adapting strategy to the interlocutor (more debunking/steelmanning with skeptics, more bridging-to-action with sympathizers), which resonates with Szabo et al.'s claim that chatbot efficacy hinges on adaptive interaction rather than static script.

Trust, however, is not simply a moderator of chatbot success but a variable interacting with prior belief in complicated ways. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the emerging "AI dialogue works" narrative by testing a *balanced* (not corrective) news chatbot and finding, counterintuitively, that higher-conspiracy-belief users trust and rate it *more* positively than low-conspiracy users — inverting the assumption that conspiracy believers are reflexively distrustful of institutional or algorithmic sources. This suggests the mechanism in Costello and Kotz's success (personalized, evidence-dense dialogue) may not simply be "more trust" but something about perceived respect for the user's reasoning process, since even a chatbot merely presenting balanced (not confrontational) content can win over skeptical audiences.

## Emotion as an Underexplored Lever

Finally, [[Xue2025-bp]] turns the emotion question back onto the corrective side of the equation, showing that fact-checking content itself — nominally the genre of dispassionate correction — is often heavily emotionally coded, and that this emotionality shapes engagement and sentiment toward fact-checked targets. This sits provocatively alongside [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]'s finding that inoculation against *emotional manipulation* specifically (fearmongering) is highly effective, and alongside Szabo et al.'s linguistic analysis showing successful chatbot inoculation dialogues were higher in emotional and "authentic" language. Together these findings hint that the field's traditional information-deficit framing (fix beliefs by supplying facts) is giving way to a more affect-aware model of both misinformation and its correction — precisely the shift Spampatti et al. call for when they argue that cognitive corrections alone are insufficient and that motivational, emotional, and social drivers must be engaged directly.
