# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The Arc: From Static Messages to Adaptive Dialogue

The papers filed here trace an unmistakable arc in climate and misinformation communication research: a movement away from static, one-size-fits-all messages toward interactive, adaptive, and personalized interventions — alongside a parallel maturation of methodological rigor (megastudies, field trials, registered reports) used to test whether any of this actually works.

At one end of this arc sits the sobering empirical baseline established by [[Voelkel2026-lc]]. Testing the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies in a large registered-report megastudy, the authors find that even the most celebrated framings in the literature produce only small shifts in attitudes (1–4 percentage points), minimal partisan heterogeneity, and — critically — no movement on costly behavior like donations. This result functions as a corrective to decades of underpowered, single-message studies and raises a foundational question that haunts the rest of the topic: if short, static messages have such limited reach, what kind of intervention could do better? [[Lieu2025-nl]] complicates the picture further by showing that it is not the logical structure of misinformation (FLICC fallacies) but its *content* (CARDS categories, especially attacks on climate solutions) that drives perceived veracity and political polarization — suggesting that message-design efforts focused on debunking logical form may be targeting the wrong lever entirely.

## Scaling Inoculation Beyond the Lab

A second cluster addresses the gap between lab-based inoculation theory and real-world deployment. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] provides a rare large-scale field test, showing that a 19-second prebunking video delivered as an Instagram ad to over 375,000 users measurably improves recognition of emotional manipulation, with effects persisting five months later and increasing information-seeking behavior — demonstrating that inoculation can be cheaply scaled within existing ad infrastructure rather than confined to survey experiments. [[Szabo2026-rd]] pushes inoculation further toward interactivity, introducing "Conversational Inoculation" via an LLM chatbot (MindFort/"Forty") that outperforms traditional reading- and writing-based inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is accounted for. The qualitative findings here are important: effectiveness hinges not just on argument content but on interactional design — adaptability, trust-building, and minimizing friction — anticipating the dialogue-based interventions that follow.

## Dialogue and Personalization as the New Frontier

The clearest throughline across this topic is the rise of conversational AI as a persuasive technology that can do what static messages cannot: adapt. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the pivotal paper here, demonstrating that personalized, evidence-based GPT-4 dialogues durably reduce conspiracy beliefs by ~20%, with effects persisting two months and generalizing beyond the focal belief — directly challenging motivational accounts that treat conspiracy belief as impervious to counterevidence. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic across domains (climate, vaccination, inequality), showing that AI dialogues shift both beliefs and contested policy support, with effects concentrated precisely among initially skeptical participants and amplified by trust in science — the audience static messaging has historically failed to move. Together, these two papers suggest that the "attitude-behavior gap" and partisan intransigence documented by [[Voelkel2026-lc]] may be less a fixed feature of persuasion and more an artifact of message brevity and impersonality.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] offers a complementary but more ambivalent perspective on chatbot-based interventions, testing "balanced news chatbots" that present mixed mainstream/alternative perspectives. Counterintuitively, higher-conspiracy-belief users trust and accept such chatbots *more* than skeptics of conspiracy theories — a finding that sits in tension with assumptions (echoed in [[Kotz2026-lk]] and [[Costello2024-bg]]) that skeptical or conspiracy-prone audiences are the hardest to reach. This paper is a useful counterpoint: it suggests perceived usefulness and trust, not ideological congruence, may govern uptake of conversational tools, and that selective engagement persists even among nominally "open" users.

## Reflexive and Methodological Commentary

Two papers step back from empirical intervention-testing to interrogate the field itself. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] argues that misinformation research should borrow from climate psychology's more mature engagement with organized opposition, collective-action framing, and real-world behavioral outcomes — directly diagnosing the weakness that [[Voelkel2026-lc]] empirically demonstrates (overreliance on attitudinal proxies, neglect of behavior) and anticipating the corporate-collaboration cautions relevant to chatbot/AI-vendor partnerships seen in [[Dubey2026-bl]] and the AI dialogue studies. [[Bravo2026-ue]], meanwhile, maps the adjacent but underintegrated terrain of visual climate communication, showing that message-based research (text, framing, dialogue) has far outpaced study of imagery, especially on social media and in non-Western contexts — a gap partially addressed methodologically by [[Arminio2025-tw]], whose VLLM-based clustering pipeline offers a computational tool for analyzing the connotative meaning of climate-related social media images at scale. These two visually-oriented papers sit slightly apart from the dialogue/inoculation core but gesture toward where message-based intervention research will need to expand next: beyond text-based framing and chatbot dialogue into the visual and multimodal terrain where much climate (mis)information actually circulates.

## Synthesis

Read together, these ten papers chart a field in transition: away from testing which static slogan moves the needle, and toward understanding *why* and *for whom* adaptive, personalized, dialogic engagement succeeds where brief messaging fails. The strongest effects across this set emerge not from clever framing but from sustained, responsive interaction — whether human-authored debate, chatbot conversation, or LLM dialogue — while the weakest and most fragile effects belong to single-shot messages tested at scale. The unresolved tensions worth tracking forward are whether these conversational gains hold outside controlled platforms, whether they survive exposure to organized disinformation campaigns (per [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s warnings about vested interests), and whether the asymmetric trust effects seen in [[Dubey2026-bl]] will undermine or merely complicate the optimistic scalability claims of [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]].
