# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## From Cognitive Theory to Applied Intervention

The cluster opens, conceptually if not chronologically, with two papers that frame why message-based interventions matter and what they should be measured against. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] establishes the now-dominant "classical reasoning" account of misinformation susceptibility: belief in false news is driven less by partisan motivated reasoning than by a failure to deliberate, reliance on heuristics like familiarity, and an under-examined gap between what people believe and what they share. This reframing matters for the whole topic because it licenses interventions that work by *redirecting attention* or *supplying reasoning scaffolds* rather than by dismantling identity-protective motives — a premise that several chatbot and inoculation studies in this set implicitly adopt. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] pushes the field in a complementary but corrective direction, importing lessons from climate psychology's longer fight against organized denial. Its central methodological critique — that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world behavioral outcomes, and that effects found in US partisan contexts rarely generalize — functions as an implicit evaluative yardstick for the rest of the papers filed here: do they test attitudes or behavior? Do they generalize beyond WEIRD, English-speaking samples? Read together, these two papers set the terms of debate that the empirical studies below play out.

## Scaling Inoculation: From Lab Demonstrations to Live Feeds

A second thread tests the classic "prebunking" paradigm for real-world and conversational delivery. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] moves inoculation out of the lab entirely, embedding a 19-second prebunking ad directly into Instagram's Story feed for over 375,000 users and showing a 21-point gain in fearmongering detection that persists five months later — direct evidence against Spampatti's concern about ecological validity, since this is a genuinely scaled, behaviorally-measured field deployment. [[Szabo2026-rd]] pursues a different axis of scaling: not reach, but *interactivity*. Its "Conversational Inoculation" method pits a chatbot-delivered dialogue against traditional reading- and writing-based inoculation, finding that the conversational format outperforms static refutation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled for. Its qualitative findings — that effectiveness hinges on adaptability, trust-building, and minimizing interactional friction rather than argument content alone — reframe inoculation less as information transfer and more as a relational process, anticipating the AI-dialogue papers that follow.

## Conversational AI as a Persuasive Technology

The most striking methodological pivot in this collection is the shift from static or scripted messages to adaptive, LLM-powered dialogue. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the pivotal demonstration: brief, personalized GPT-4 conversations reduce conspiracy belief by roughly 20%, durably and across unrelated conspiracies, directly challenging motivational accounts of belief entrenchment and aligning with Pennycook's reasoning-based framework — if the evidence is sufficiently tailored and detailed, even "identity-protected" beliefs move. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic beyond conspiracy theories into contested policy domains — climate, vaccination, inequality — and finds that GPT-5 dialogues shift beliefs and policy support most among initial skeptics, with trust in science as the key moderator; critically, it shows that belief change does not automatically cascade into policy support, which must be targeted directly. This is one of the few studies here to combine the "real behavioral outcome" aspiration of Spampatti with a genuinely cross-domain test of generalizability. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the optimistic AI-dialogue story with a counterintuitive finding: a "balanced" chatbot presenting both mainstream and conspiratorial climate framings is trusted *more*, not less, by people with higher conspiracy beliefs — suggesting that perceived neutrality or utility, rather than ideological congruence, may be what earns engagement from otherwise resistant audiences. Taken together, these three papers suggest conversational AI's persuasive power lies less in superior facts than in adaptive framing and relational trust, echoing Szabo's qualitative mechanisms.

## What Content Moves People — and What It Doesn't

A final thread interrogates message content and tone rather than delivery mechanism. [[Voelkel2026-lc]] is the sobering centerpiece: a preregistered megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies finds only small, largely non-partisan attitudinal effects and *no* movement on costly behavior (actual donations), directly validating Spampatti's warning that attitudinal proxies decouple from real-world outcomes. [[Lieu2025-nl]] dissects the *content* side of misinformation itself, crossing the CARDS taxonomy of contrarian claims with the FLICC taxonomy of rhetorical fallacies; it finds that logical fallacy type barely matters, but that content attacking climate solutions is uniquely credible and polarizing — a finding with direct implications for where inoculation and debunking resources should be targeted. [[Xue2025-bp]] examines the opposite side of the exchange, showing that fact-checking content itself is often emotionally charged despite its objectivity norms, and that this emotionality shapes engagement and sentiment — raising the question of whether corrective messaging should lean into affect (as inoculation and chatbot dialogues implicitly do) or resist it in the name of credibility.

## Synthesis

Across the set, a coherent arc emerges: early cognitive-reasoning accounts ([[Pennycook2021-jq]]) and a call for ecological and behavioral rigor ([[Spampatti2026-kx]]) motivate a shift from static warnings toward scaled, adaptive, and conversational interventions ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], [[Szabo2026-rd]], [[Costello2024-bg]], [[Kotz2026-lk]], [[Dubey2026-bl]]), while message-content studies ([[Voelkel2026-lc]], [[Lieu2025-nl]], [[Xue2025-bp]]) temper enthusiasm by showing how fragile, content-dependent, and behaviorally limited many framing effects remain. The throughline is a growing consensus that *interaction design* — personalization, trust, adaptability — may matter as much as *argument content*, but also a persistent, unresolved tension between attitude change (relatively easy to produce) and durable behavioral or policy consequence (still rare and hard-won).
