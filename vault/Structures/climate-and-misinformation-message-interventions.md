# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The Shape of the Field: From Static Messages to Interactive Interventions

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as something like a meta-commentary on the entire cluster: it argues that misinformation research is a young field that can borrow hard-won lessons from climate psychology's decades of confronting organised denial. Its central provocations — that cognitive correction alone is not enough, that studies should measure real behaviour rather than proxy beliefs, that effects must be tested across countries and domains rather than assumed to generalise from the US — read almost as a checklist against which the other nine papers can be evaluated. Several of them, intentionally or not, already answer parts of that checklist; others illustrate exactly the limitations Spampatti et al. warn about.

## Testing the Ceiling of Static Messages

The most direct empirical test of "does information/framing alone work" comes from [[Voelkel2026-lc]], a large registered-report megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies. Its findings largely vindicate Spampatti et al.'s scepticism about purely cognitive, one-shot interventions: several widely cited strategies failed to replicate, the messages that did work produced only small attitudinal shifts (1–4 points), effects did not differentiate by party, and — crucially — none moved costly behaviour (donations), with some even backfiring. This establishes an empirical ceiling for single-exposure message framing and motivates the turn toward more interactive or repeated-contact interventions found elsewhere in this cluster. [[Lieu2025-nl]] complements this by dissecting the *content* rather than the *framing* side of the ledger: testing the CARDS taxonomy of contrarian claims against the FLICC taxonomy of logical fallacies, it finds that the specific fallacy used to deliver misinformation barely matters, but the substantive target does — attacks on climate solutions are perceived as most credible and most polarising. Together, Voelkel and Lieu suggest that the content and target of a message matter far more than its superficial framing or logical structure, a finding with direct implications for how prebunking and chatbot interventions (discussed below) should be designed.

## Inoculation Taken to Scale and Made Conversational

Two papers extend inoculation theory in complementary directions. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that a single 19-second prebunking video, deployed as an Instagram ad to over 375,000 users, produces a durable (5-month) and substantial gain in the ability to spot emotional manipulation — directly answering Spampatti et al.'s call for interventions tested at ecologically valid scale and for real behavioural outcomes (click-through rates, not just self-report). [[Szabo2026-rd]] pushes inoculation from broadcast to dialogue, introducing "Conversational Inoculation" via an LLM chatbot (MindFort/"Forty") that adapts its refutations to the individual user. It finds that chatbot-delivered inoculation outperforms both traditional reading- and writing-based inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled, and identifies interactional factors — trust-building, adaptability, minimising friction — as mechanisms distinct from argument content itself. Read together, these two papers trace an arc from cheap, scalable, one-way prebunking to costlier but more responsive, dialogic inoculation, raising the open question of where the trade-off between scale and personalisation is best struck.

## Conversational AI as a Persuasive (and Sometimes Trusted) Interlocutor

The dialogic thread continues, and intensifies, in three papers using conversational AI not merely to inoculate against future misinformation but to directly revise existing false beliefs. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the landmark demonstration that a few rounds of personalised, evidence-based conversation with GPT-4 durably (≥2 months) reduces belief in specific conspiracy theories by roughly 20%, generalising to unrelated conspiracies and behavioural intentions — a finding that challenges motivational accounts of conspiracy belief as impervious to evidence, echoing Spampatti et al.'s call to look beyond purely cognitive framings while showing that sufficiently tailored cognitive engagement can still work. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic across three structurally different contested domains (climate, vaccination, inequality) with a much larger sample, finding that AI dialogue shifts both beliefs and concrete policy support (carbon and estate taxes), that effects are strongest precisely among the skeptics who are hardest to reach by conventional messaging, and that trust in science — not trust in AI itself — is the key amplifying moderator. This is a direct rebuttal, in miniature, to the concern in Voelkel2026-lc that framing effects are small and behaviourally inert: interactive, adaptive dialogue seems to reach further than static messages, especially for policy-relevant, costlier outcomes.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates this optimistic picture. Testing "balanced" chatbots that present both mainstream and conspiratorial perspectives on climate change, it finds — against the authors' own hypothesis — that people with *higher* conspiracy beliefs trust and rate such chatbots more favourably than low-conspiracy users, with perceived usefulness and trust (not ease of use) driving acceptance. This suggests that the design choice of "balance" itself, rather than tailored refutation as in Costello2024-bg, may function differently depending on the audience's prior epistemic stance — a caution against assuming that any chatbot format will produce a Costello-like correction effect, and a reminder of Spampatti et al.'s point about heterogeneity across audiences and domains.

## Vulnerability Effects and the Limits of Correction

[[Choi2026-bz]] shifts the lens from message design to audience psychology, showing that the mere experience of struggling to authenticate one (deep)fake item carries over to shape confidence and susceptibility toward *later*, modality-matched fake content. This "modality-congruent carryover effect" is a useful counterweight to the intervention-focused papers above: it suggests that repeated exposure to synthetic and disinformation content has cumulative psychological effects independent of any corrective intervention, and that inoculation or chatbot interventions may need to account for users' pre-existing, exposure-driven confidence states rather than treating each encounter as an independent trial.

## Beyond the Single Claim: Narratives and Arguments

Finally, [[Ramos2026-qo]] moves the unit of analysis up a level, from individual claims or single conversations to the evolving *narrative* structures that disinformation exploits during real crises (the Valencia flood conspiracy). Its argument — that fact-checking alone cannot keep pace with mutating, emotionally resonant narratives, and that argument-checking (targeting premises and fallacies) and narrative monitoring are needed alongside claim correction — resonates with the logic-based approach of Lieu2025-nl and with Szabo2026-rd's emphasis on scaffolding independent reasoning rather than merely refuting facts. It also operationalises, in an applied newsroom setting, the systems-level and real-world-behaviour orientation that Spampatti2026-kx calls for at the level of research design.

## Synthesis

Across these ten papers a clear arc emerges: static, one-shot messages — even the most heavily cited ones — produce only modest, often behaviourally inert effects ([[Voelkel2026-lc]], [[Lieu2025-nl]]); scaling brief inoculation to real feeds helps but leaves personalisation on the table ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]); making inoculation and correction conversational and adaptive — via chatbots and LLMs — appears to unlock stronger, more durable belief change, especially among the hardest-to-reach skeptics ([[Szabo2026-rd]], [[Costello2024-bg]], [[Kotz2026-lk]]), though audience trust dynamics can cut in unexpected directions ([[Dubey2026-bl]]); underlying vulnerability is shaped by cumulative, modality-specific experience rather than single exposures ([[Choi2026-bz]]); and durable resistance may ultimately require moving past individual claims to contest entire narratives and argumentative structures ([[Ramos2026-qo]]). [[Spampatti2026-kx]] frames all of this as a call to keep pushing toward real behavioural outcomes, cross-context generalisability, and systems-level thinking — a standard the more recent conversational-AI studies in this set are beginning, unevenly, to meet.
