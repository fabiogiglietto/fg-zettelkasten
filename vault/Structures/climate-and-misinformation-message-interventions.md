# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The Arc of Inquiry

This cluster traces a shift in message-based climate and misinformation research: from testing static, one-shot messages against a fixed audience, toward interactive, adaptive interventions that can be personalised and scaled. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as the meta-commentary that frames this whole trajectory, arguing that misinformation research is a younger field that can borrow from climate psychology's harder-won lessons — particularly its distrust of purely cognitive, information-deficit models, its insistence on measuring real-world behavioural outcomes rather than lab proxies, and its wariness of both vested-interest capture and premature generalisation across contexts. The other seven papers can be read as empirical instantiations of exactly the tensions this piece identifies: the gap between attitude change and behaviour change, the need for contextual and topic-level heterogeneity, and the promise (and risk) of new technological delivery mechanisms such as chatbots.

## The Limits of Short-Form Messaging

[[Voelkel2026-lc]] delivers the most sobering empirical test of the field's foundational assumption — that carefully framed messages can move the needle on climate attitudes and behaviour. Its megastudy design, explicitly built as a corrective to underpowered, non-replicating prior work, finds that even the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies produce only small, largely partisan-neutral shifts in attitudes and intentions, and *no* effect on costly behaviour (donations), sometimes even backfiring. This directly substantiates the behavioural-measurement critique in [[Spampatti2026-kx]]: attitudinal effects and behavioural effects are decoupled, and the field's reliance on the former overstates persuasive power. [[Lieu2025-nl]] complements this by dissecting the anatomy of *misinformation* messages rather than pro-climate ones, showing that the surface logical form of an attack (the FLICC fallacy) matters far less than its rhetorical target (the CARDS content category): attacks on climate solutions are perceived as most credible and most polarising, especially for conservatives. Together these two papers suggest that message *content and framing* operate on relatively shallow, easily saturated levers — persuasive gains are real but modest, and audience-targeted tailoring (partisan-specific framing, specific fallacy-debunking) may matter less than assumed.

## Inoculation and Prebunking at Scale

A second strand asks whether resistance can be built *before* exposure, rather than corrected after. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] provides some of the strongest applied evidence yet that brief psychological inoculation — a 19-second prebunking video embedded as an ordinary Instagram ad — can scale to hundreds of thousands of users, produce a 21-point accuracy gain in spotting emotional manipulation, and persist five months later, all at low cost. This is the clearest answer in the set to [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s call for ecologically valid, real-world behavioural measurement (click-throughs, not just self-report) and for demonstrating generalisability beyond the lab. [[Szabo2026-rd]] pushes inoculation into interactive territory, introducing "Conversational Inoculation" via an LLM chatbot (MindFort/"Forty") that outperforms traditional reading- and writing-based inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled for. Its qualitative analysis — highlighting adaptability, trust-building, and interactional friction as mechanisms — begins to answer a question the static-message papers cannot: *why* does format matter, not just content. This paper serves as the bridge into the chatbot-focused cluster below.

## Conversational AI as a Persuasive and Trust-Building Agent

The most striking innovation across these papers is the move from message to *dialogue*. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the pivotal result: three-round, personalised GPT-4 conversations reduce conspiracy belief by roughly 20%, durably, and generalise beyond the targeted belief — directly challenging motivational accounts that treat conspiracy belief as impervious to evidence, and instead supporting an analytic-reasoning account in which depth and personalisation, not audience psychology, were the missing ingredient in prior debunking failures. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic beyond conspiracy belief into contested policy domains (climate, vaccination, inequality), showing that GPT-5 dialogues shift both beliefs and policy support, with effects concentrated among *skeptics* and amplified by trust in science — a finding that speaks to [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s point about heterogeneity: the same intervention lands very differently depending on prior audience disposition, and belief change does not automatically transfer to policy support without directly targeting it.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the optimism of these AI-dialogue results by probing *acceptance* rather than persuasive efficacy: using a Technology Acceptance Model on a "balanced news chatbot" (Infobot) that mixes mainstream and conspiratorial climate content, it finds — counterintuitively — that higher-conspiracy-belief users trust and accept the chatbot *more* than low-belief users, driven by perceived usefulness and trust rather than ease of use. This result sits in productive tension with [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]]: where those papers show AI dialogue can durably change belief, Dubey's finding suggests the *uptake* side of the pipeline (why would a skeptic engage with the chatbot at all) may be less fraught than assumed, though it also warns that trust in these tools is not synonymous with belief change, and that selective engagement persists even among accepting users.

## Synthesis

Read together, the eight papers sketch a coherent developmental story that mirrors [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s prescriptions almost point for point: static, generic messages ([[Voelkel2026-lc]], [[Lieu2025-nl]]) show real but limited and behaviourally shallow effects; brief, scalable inoculation ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]) shows that timing and delivery format can extend real-world impact cheaply; and personalised, conversational AI ([[Szabo2026-rd]], [[Costello2024-bg]], [[Kotz2026-lk]], [[Dubey2026-bl]]) emerges as the most promising frontier precisely because it can adapt to individual reasoning, build trust, and reach skeptics that one-size-fits-all messages cannot. The unresolved thread running through all of them, flagged explicitly by [[Spampatti2026-kx]], is whether these gains — in attitudes, in stated intentions, in short-term behavioural proxies — will translate into the durable, high-stakes, real-world behaviour change that remains the field's most elusive and most consequential outcome.
