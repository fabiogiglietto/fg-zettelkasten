# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The deficit model under strain

A recurring throughline across this literature is the slow abandonment of the assumption that correcting facts or crafting the "right" message will move attitudes and behaviour in any straightforward way. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] makes this explicit as a meta-level argument, importing lessons from climate psychology's decades-long struggle with organised denial into the younger field of misinformation research: information alone is not enough, real-world behavioural outcomes are rarely measured, and effects vary enormously across countries, domains, and online/offline contexts. This scepticism about single-shot factual correction is borne out empirically by [[Voelkel2026-lc]], whose registered-report megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies finds only small (1–4 percentage point) attitudinal shifts, little partisan heterogeneity (undercutting theories that audience-tailored framing is necessary), and—crucially—no effect whatsoever on costly behaviour such as actual donations, with some messages even backfiring. Together these two papers frame the central problem this whole topic wrestles with: attitude change is comparatively easy to produce with short-form messaging, but behaviour change is not, and the psychological mechanisms that would bridge the gap remain underspecified.

## What makes misinformation persuasive in the first place

Before asking what corrective messages work, several papers interrogate what makes misinformation itself effective. [[Lieu2025-nl]] crosses the content-based CARDS taxonomy of contrarian claims with the logic-based FLICC taxonomy of rhetorical fallacies and finds that content category, not fallacy type, drives perceived veracity: claims attacking climate *solutions* are rated most credible and, among conservatives specifically, most damaging to climate belief. This has direct implications for the message-design work of Voelkel and colleagues—if solution-attacking misinformation is where the real ideological leverage lies, debunking and prebunking resources might be better targeted there than spread evenly across fallacy types. [[Xue2025-bp]] complicates the normative picture further by showing that fact-checking itself, ostensibly a genre of dispassionate correction, is often emotionally charged, and that this emotionality shapes engagement and sentiment toward fact-checked targets—suggesting that the "facts vs. feelings" dichotomy underlying much of this literature is not as clean as intervention designers might wish. Methodologically, [[Gardam2025-er]] and [[Arminio2025-tw]] push the analytic frontier beyond text: the former argues that climate denial discourse on Instagram is fundamentally multimodal and demands visual-first analysis, while the latter demonstrates that vision-language models can recover the *connotative* meaning of climate-related images (e.g., clustering wind turbines and solar panels by their cultural meaning rather than surface appearance) far better than CNN pipelines. These two papers matter for the topic less as intervention studies than as infrastructure: they gesture toward the kind of image-aware, multimodal understanding of climate misinformation that message-based interventions will eventually need to target, since so much denial content circulates as memes and visuals rather than argumentative text.

## Inoculation: from lab demonstration to scaled deployment

A second cluster tests whether psychological inoculation—exposing people to a weakened form of a manipulation technique so they can resist the real thing—can move from controlled lab settings into the wild. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] is the most ecologically ambitious: a 19-second prebunking video deployed as an Instagram ad to over 375,000 users produced a 21-point improvement in participants' ability to spot fearmongering, an effect that held up five months later and roughly tripled click-through to further information, all at low cost. This paper matters because it answers, at least for one narrow technique (emotional manipulation), the generalisability concern that [[Spampatti2026-kx]] raises about lab-to-field gaps. [[Szabo2026-rd]] pushes inoculation in a different direction—toward *interactivity*—by building an LLM-chatbot ("Forty") that conducts a structured inoculating dialogue and comparing it against traditional reading- and writing-based inoculation across four topics. The chatbot condition outperforms both traditional formats once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled, and qualitative analysis attributes this to the chatbot's capacity to adapt to the user's stated attitudes, build trust through partnership rather than authority, and scaffold independent reasoning rather than simply supplying refutational content. This is an important bridge point in the topic: it suggests that the *format* of inoculation (static content vs. responsive dialogue) may matter as much as its *content*, setting up the pivot to AI dialogue interventions more broadly.

## Conversational AI as a persuasive and inoculating agent

The most striking empirical development running through this set is the emergence of LLM-powered dialogue as an intervention modality in its own right, moving well beyond inoculation into direct belief revision. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the foundational demonstration: personalised, evidence-based three-round conversations with GPT-4 Turbo reduced belief in conspiracy theories by roughly 20%, durably (≥2 months) and with spillover to unrelated conspiracies, directly challenging motivational accounts that treat conspiracy belief as inherently resistant to counterevidence. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic across domains—climate change, vaccination, and economic inequality—showing that brief AI dialogues shift beliefs and, with appropriately targeted prompts, policy support (e.g., carbon and estate taxes), with effects concentrated among initially skeptical participants and amplified by trust in science. Read alongside [[Szabo2026-rd]], these papers converge on a shared mechanism: adaptability. The chatbot's capacity to tailor its argument, tone, and rhetorical strategy (prebunking/steelmanning for skeptics, bridging-to-action for the already-convinced, per Kotz et al.'s text analysis) appears to be what static messages and one-size-fits-all inoculation content cannot achieve. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the optimism, however: testing a "balanced news chatbot" that deliberately presents mainstream and alternative climate perspectives together, the authors find—counter to their own hypothesis—that people with higher conspiracy beliefs *trust and prefer* the balanced chatbot more than low-conspiracy users do, and that selective reading-time engagement persists regardless of belief group. This suggests that trust in a conversational agent and actual belief revision are not the same thing, and that the encouraging durability results from Costello and Kotz may depend heavily on how directly evidence is personalised and confronted, not merely on the presence of a conversational interface.

## Synthesis: convergences and open tensions

Across the eleven papers, a rough arc emerges: message-content studies ([[Voelkel2026-lc]], [[Lieu2025-nl]], [[Xue2025-bp]]) establish that static, generic framings have real but modest and behaviourally shallow effects; inoculation studies ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], [[Szabo2026-rd]]) show that prebunking can scale and persist, especially when interactive; and AI-dialogue studies ([[Costello2024-bg]], [[Kotz2026-lk]], [[Dubey2026-bl]]) suggest that personalised, adaptive conversation may be the most powerful lever yet identified—capable of moving even entrenched believers—while also revealing new puzzles about trust, selective engagement, and the difference between attitude change and durable behaviour change. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] provides the connective tissue for the whole set, arguing that the field needs to measure real behavioural stakes (something only Voelkel's donation outcome and Costello's behavioural-intention items really attempt), attend to cross-context heterogeneity, and integrate individual-level mechanisms with systemic ones. The multimodal methods papers ([[Gardam2025-er]], [[Arminio2025-tw]]) sit slightly apart as infrastructure rather than intervention tests, but they point to an underexplored frontier: nearly all the intervention studies here operate on text, while the denial and conspiracy content circulating on platforms like Instagram is substantially visual—suggesting that the next generation of message-based interventions may need to be as visually and multimodally literate as the misinformation they are designed to counter.
