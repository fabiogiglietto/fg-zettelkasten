# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## Setting the Agenda

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as the conceptual frame for this whole cluster of studies: it argues that climate psychology, having weathered decades of organised denial, has hard-won lessons for the younger field of misinformation research — chiefly that cognitive corrections alone are insufficient, that behavioural (not merely attitudinal) outcomes are undermeasured, and that context (country, domain, online vs. offline) radically conditions what "works." Read against this backdrop, the other eight papers in this collection can be seen as a series of empirical tests of exactly these claims — some vindicating the call for real-world behavioural measurement, some illustrating the risk of overgeneralising from small samples or single platforms, and some pushing methodology itself toward the systems-level, multimodal analysis that Spampatti et al. say the field still lacks.

## Testing What Actually Moves Attitudes: Messages, Framings, Fallacies

Two large-scale studies interrogate the core assumption that carefully worded messages can shift climate attitudes and behaviour. [[Voelkel2026-lc]] subjects the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies to megastudy scrutiny (N=13,544, preceded by null-heavy pilot replications) and finds a sobering pattern that directly echoes Spampatti's critique: several messages reliably nudge attitudes and intentions by a few percentage points, effects are strikingly consistent across partisan lines (undercutting theories that demand audience-tailored framing), yet none moves actual costly behaviour — donations were unaffected or even backfired. This is the attitude–behaviour gap Spampatti et al. warn about, demonstrated at scale. [[Lieu2025-nl]] complements this by disaggregating misinformation itself rather than corrective messaging, crossing the CARDS taxonomy of contrarian content with the FLICC taxonomy of rhetorical fallacies. Its central finding — that the *logical form* of a fallacy barely matters, but the *content category* does, with attacks on climate solutions and scientists proving most credible and most polarising — suggests that debunking and prebunking efforts might be more efficiently targeted at specific claim-types than at fallacy-types, a nuance that pure inoculation-theory accounts (which often treat "logical flaws" as interchangeable) can miss. Together these two papers push against overly optimistic assumptions in the messaging literature: content-level specificity and behavioural (not just attitudinal) endpoints turn out to matter more than headline claims about "the most persuasive frame."

## From Static Messages to Conversational and AI-Mediated Interventions

A second thread moves from one-shot framed messages toward dynamic, personalised, and conversational interventions — precisely the kind of engagement-based approach Spampatti et al. argue is needed to supplement pure information provision. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the pivotal demonstration: multi-round, evidence-tailored dialogues with GPT-4 Turbo durably reduced conspiracy belief by roughly 20%, with effects persisting two months and generalising to unrelated conspiracies — directly challenging motivational accounts of conspiracy belief as impervious to counter-evidence, and showing that depth and personalisation, not mere correction, drive belief change. [[Szabo2026-rd]] operationalises a similar logic for inoculation theory itself, introducing "Conversational Inoculation" via the MindFort chatbot; when controlling for baseline susceptibility, the chatbot condition outperforms both traditional reading- and writing-based inoculation, with qualitative analysis pointing to adaptability, trust-building, and reduced interactional friction as the mechanisms that static refutational texts cannot provide. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] shows that even brief, non-conversational inoculation can scale dramatically in the wild: a 19-second prebunking ad delivered to over 375,000 Instagram users raised recognition of emotional manipulation by 21 points, with the effect holding at five months — evidence that ecological validity and scalability, another Spampatti concern, are achievable outside the lab. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the conversational-agent story with a counterintuitive result: "balanced news" chatbots presenting mainstream and alternative climate perspectives were trusted *more*, not less, by higher-conspiracy-belief users, defying assumptions from the reactance/motivated-reasoning literature and suggesting that perceived usefulness and trust—not ideological congruence—drive chatbot acceptance. Read together, these four studies suggest that interactivity, personalisation, and trust are emerging as the operative ingredients across very different intervention formats (LLM dialogue, chatbot inoculation, ad-based prebunking, balanced-news bots), even as they leave open exactly which mechanism—corrective depth, adaptive dialogue, or platform trust—is doing the work in each case.

## Extending the Evidence Base: Visual and Multimodal Misinformation

The final pair of papers steps back from testing interventions to ask whether the field is even studying the right *evidence*. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues that text-centric methods misrepresent how climate denial and conspiracy narratives actually circulate on image-driven platforms like Instagram, proposing a visual-first methodology for multimodal discourse analysis. [[Arminio2025-tw]] offers a complementary computational answer to the same problem: a VLLM-based pipeline that clusters climate-related Instagram images by *connotative* (culturally embedded) rather than merely denotative meaning, substantially outperforming CNN-based clustering while remaining interpretable via keyword summaries. Neither paper directly tests a message intervention, but both address a gap the rest of this cluster leaves exposed — nearly all the framing, inoculation, and chatbot studies here operate on text, whereas the actual misinformation ecosystem (memes, symbols, visual rhetoric) is substantially non-textual. In Spampatti et al.'s terms, these two papers extend the "contextual generalisability" and "systems thinking" agenda from message-content to message-*modality*, hinting that future inoculation and framing interventions may need visual and multimodal analogues to keep pace with how climate misinformation is actually consumed and shared.
