# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The Arc: From Static Messages to Adaptive Dialogue

The papers in this collection trace a methodological and theoretical arc from one-shot, static message interventions toward increasingly adaptive, conversational, and personalized approaches to building resistance against climate misinformation and conspiracy beliefs. Read together, they chart both the promise and the limits of message-based interventions, and converge on a key insight: the format and interactivity of a message may matter as much as its content.

## Setting the Stakes: Why Climate and Misinformation Research Need Each Other

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as a conceptual anchor for the whole topic, arguing that climate psychology—having matured under decades of organised denial—offers transferable lessons for the younger field of misinformation research. Its core critique, that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world behavioural outcomes rather than cognitive proxies, serves as an implicit yardstick against which the other papers in this set can be judged. Several papers here directly answer this call: [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] and [[Costello2024-bg]] measure durable, real-world behavioural and belief outcomes at scale, while [[Voelkel2026-lc]] explicitly tests whether attitudinal shifts translate into costly behaviour (donations)—and finds they largely do not, vindicating Spampatti et al.'s warning about the attitude-behaviour gap.

## Static Messages: Reliable but Modest Effects

[[Voelkel2026-lc]] provides the most rigorous test of classic, non-interactive climate messaging. Its registered-report megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messages finds that several strategies reliably shift attitudes, but effects are small (1-4 percentage points), largely immune to partisan targeting, and crucially fail to move actual donation behaviour—with some messages even backfiring. This null-to-small-effects result on behaviour stands in productive tension with [[Lieu2025-nl]], which shows that the *content* of misinformation (not its logical form) drives differential impact: contrarian claims attacking climate solutions are rated most credible and most polarizing, especially among conservatives, suggesting that intervention designers should prioritize content-matched rather than one-size-fits-all rebuttals. Together these two papers suggest diminishing returns from further refining static, generic messages and point toward the need for more targeted or interactive approaches—a transition the remaining papers take up directly.

## The Turn to Interactivity: Chatbots and Conversational Inoculation

A cluster of papers tests whether making interventions conversational and adaptive improves on static messaging. [[Szabo2026-rd]] directly pits a "Conversational Inoculation" chatbot against traditional reading- and writing-based inoculation, finding that the chatbot format outperforms classic approaches once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled—crediting adaptability, trust-building, and minimized friction as key mechanisms. This echoes the mechanism-level question raised by [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], whose large-scale Instagram field study demonstrates that even a brief, non-conversational prebunking video can scale cheaply and produce durable (five-month) resistance to emotional manipulation, suggesting that interactivity, while helpful, is not strictly necessary for real-world efficacy at scale—cost and reach can matter as much as sophistication.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the simple "more engagement is better" story by probing *trust* in chatbot-delivered balanced information. Counterintuitively, users with higher conspiracy beliefs trust and prefer "balanced news chatbots" more than low-conspiracy users, challenging assumptions (shared implicitly by [[Lieu2025-nl]] and others) that conspiracy believers are reflexively defensive toward corrective or balanced content. This finding reframes the design question: the barrier to influencing skeptical audiences may be less about believers' intransigence and more about whether the delivery mechanism itself feels trustworthy and non-paternalistic.

## LLM Dialogues: Durable, Generalizable, and Targeted at Skeptics

The two most ambitious papers, [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]], extend the conversational turn to large language models explicitly designed for personalized counter-argumentation. [[Costello2024-bg]] shows that tailored, evidence-based GPT-4 dialogues reduce conspiracy belief by ~20%, durably and across unrelated conspiracies, directly contradicting motivational accounts that treat conspiracy belief as impervious to facts—dovetailing with Dubey et al.'s surprising trust finding by suggesting high-quality, responsive engagement can overcome assumed defensiveness. [[Kotz2026-lk]] generalizes this logic beyond conspiracy theories to climate change, vaccination, and economic inequality, finding that GPT-5 dialogues shift both beliefs and policy support, with effects concentrated precisely among the skeptics that static messaging (per [[Voelkel2026-lc]]) struggles to move, and amplified by trust in science. Text analysis in [[Kotz2026-lk]] reveals the AI adaptively deploying prebunking/debunking strategies with skeptics and bridging-to-action strategies with supporters—an empirical demonstration of the very adaptability that [[Szabo2026-rd]] identifies qualitatively as the chatbot's key mechanism of success.

## The Emotional Register Across Formats

Running beneath these format comparisons is a question about emotional framing. [[Xue2025-bp]] shows that fact-checking content—nominally committed to objectivity—is often emotionally charged, and that this emotionality shapes engagement and audience sentiment. This complicates the picture painted by [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], where inoculating against *emotional manipulation* is the explicit goal, and by [[Voelkel2026-lc]], where some emotionally resonant frames (e.g., Purity Framing) outperform others. Together, these papers suggest emotionality is neither simply the enemy of accurate communication nor a straightforward lever for persuasion, but a cross-cutting design variable that researchers must reckon with regardless of whether the intervention is a single fact-check, an inoculation ad, or an AI dialogue.

## Synthesis

Across this set, the trajectory is clear: simple, static, broadcast-style messages ([[Voelkel2026-lc]], [[Lieu2025-nl]]) produce real but modest and behaviourally shallow effects; brief, scalable inoculation interventions ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]) can achieve durable cognitive shifts cheaply; and increasingly personalized, conversational, AI-mediated formats ([[Szabo2026-rd]], [[Dubey2026-bl]], [[Costello2024-bg]], [[Kotz2026-lk]]) achieve the strongest, most durable, and most generalizable effects—especially among the skeptical audiences hardest to reach through one-size-fits-all messaging. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] frames the stakes and methodological gaps that this empirical arc partially fills (durable, behavioural, cross-domain outcomes) and partially still leaves open (real-world behaviour beyond stated intentions, cross-national generalizability, and the risks of relying on corporate platforms and AI systems as communication intermediaries).
