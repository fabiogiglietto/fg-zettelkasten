# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## Setting the Stage: Cross-Pollinating Climate Psychology and Misinformation Science

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as the connective tissue for this whole collection, arguing explicitly that climate communication and misinformation research are twin fields facing organised, motivated opposition and sharing the same collective-action structure (individual vs. collective interest, short- vs. long-term incentives). Its six lessons—move beyond cognitive proxies toward real-world behavioural outcomes, attend to cross-national and cross-domain heterogeneity, integrate individual- and system-level analysis, and treat corporate collaborations and researcher networks as double-edged tools—read almost as a diagnostic checklist against which the other ten papers can be measured. Notably, several of the empirical papers here already anticipate its critique (e.g., [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] and [[Voelkel2026-lc]] both push toward real-world, at-scale behavioural measurement rather than lab proxies), while others (e.g., [[Dubey2026-bl]], [[Lieu2025-nl]]) remain squarely in the attitudinal/perceptual register that Spampatti et al. flag as insufficient.

## Scaling Inoculation: From the Lab to the Feed and the Newsroom

A strong throughline is the maturation of inoculation theory from static "prebunking" messages toward interactive and infrastructural forms. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] provides the cleanest evidence that classic single-technique inoculation (against emotional manipulation/fearmongering) generalises to real Instagram scroll feeds at massive scale, with effects persisting five months and even increasing information-seeking behaviour—a rare case of an intervention satisfying Spampatti et al.'s call for ecologically valid, behaviourally measured effects. [[Szabo2026-rd]] pushes inoculation into dialogic territory, showing that an LLM-powered "conversational inoculation" chatbot (MindFort/"Forty") outperforms both traditional reading- and writing-based refutational inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is accounted for, and identifies interaction-design features—adaptability, trust-building, minimised friction—as decisive, not just message content. [[Ramos2026-qo]] extends the logic outward from individual belief change to newsroom and crisis-response practice: drawing on the Valencia DANA floods disinformation episode, it argues that fact-checking alone cannot neutralise self-mutating conspiracy narratives, and proposes "argument-checking" (targeting fallacious premises and warrants, in the spirit of inoculation/prebunking theory) combined with narrative monitoring as an institutional-scale complement to claim-level correction. Together these three papers trace inoculation's evolution from a message format to an interactive system to an organisational workflow.

## Conversational AI as a Persuasion Engine

A cluster of papers converges on large language models as dialogic interlocutors capable of shifting entrenched beliefs, complicating older motivational accounts of belief resistance. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the foundational result: personalised, evidence-tailored GPT-4 conversations durably reduced conspiracy belief by roughly 20%, generalising to unrelated conspiracies and surviving even among the most committed believers—directly challenging identity-protective/motivated-reasoning accounts in favour of analytic-reasoning models of belief revision. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this paradigm across three ideologically loaded domains (climate, vaccination, inequality) with GPT-5, showing that skeptics—not already-sympathetic audiences—are where AI dialogue moves the needle most, and that trust in science is the dominant moderator; it also shows the model adaptively deploys different rhetorical strategies (steelmanning and prebunking for skeptics vs. bridging-to-action for supporters), suggesting the mechanism is not brute repetition but tailored argumentation, echoing Szabo et al.'s emphasis on adaptive dialogue design. [[Dubey2026-bl]] offers a complementary, more cautionary angle: rather than a persuasive agent, its "balanced news chatbot" is designed as a neutral broker exposing users to both mainstream and alternative perspectives, and counterintuitively finds that higher-conspiracy-belief users trust and adopt it more than low-belief users—raising open questions about whether "balance" framings function differently from the direct evidentiary confrontation used in Costello and Kotz's designs, and whether trust in AI systems interacts with prior belief in ways current TAM-style models only partially capture.

## The Limits of Framing: What Megastudies and Content Studies Reveal

Not every message-based approach fares as well under scrutiny. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s registered-report megastudy delivers a sobering counterpoint to the optimism above: of the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies, several failed to replicate, and even the six that "worked" shifted attitudes by only 1–4 percentage points with essentially no partisan heterogeneity and zero effect on costly behaviour (donations)—in one case even backfiring. This result substantiates Spampatti et al.'s warning that lab-derived cognitive effects often do not travel to behaviour, and stands in productive tension with the AI-dialogue papers, which achieve much larger and more durable shifts by using multi-turn, personalised argumentation rather than single-shot framed messages—suggesting interactivity and tailoring, not just message content, may be the active ingredient.

On the misinformation-content side, [[Lieu2025-nl]] complements this framing-focused literature by dissecting what makes climate misinformation itself persuasive: crossing the CARDS taxonomy of contrarian claims with the FLICC taxonomy of logical fallacies, it finds that content (attacks on climate solutions) drives perceived veracity and political polarisation far more than the specific fallacy used—implying that debunking efforts should prioritise argument content over fallacy labelling, an insight with direct relevance to how "argument-checking" in [[Ramos2026-qo]] might be targeted. [[Gardam2025-er]] pushes the analysis of misinformation content into the visual register, arguing that Instagram-style climate denial is fundamentally multimodal and that text-centric coding schemes (like CARDS/FLICC) risk missing the primary rhetorical work done by images—an implicit methodological challenge to studies such as Lieu et al. that treat text as the unit of analysis. Finally, [[Xue2025-bp]] turns the lens back onto the correctors themselves, showing that fact-checking posts are often saturated with emotional language despite fact-checking's normative claim to objectivity, and that this emotionality shapes engagement and audience sentiment—raising the uncomfortable possibility that effective correction (as in the emotion-focused inoculation of [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]) may require the same affective tools that misinformation itself exploits.

## The Arc

Read together, these papers trace a shift from testing which static messages persuade (Voelkel's discouraging megastudy verdict) toward building adaptive, dialogic, and infrastructural interventions—chatbots, AI dialogues, narrative-monitoring workflows—that respond in real time to an audience's specific doubts, evidence, and emotional register. Running beneath this arc is Spampatti et al.'s meta-level challenge: the field's growing sophistication in intervention design must be matched by equal attention to real-world behavioural outcomes, generalisability across contexts, and institutional resilience against the same organised opposition that climate science has weathered for decades.
