# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster traces a shift in climate-communication and misinformation research away from static, one-size-fits-all messages toward interactive, adaptive, and mechanistically grounded interventions — while simultaneously questioning how much any message-based approach can actually move real-world behaviour. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as the connective tissue for the whole set: it explicitly argues that climate psychology and misinformation research are sibling fields facing organised opposition and shared collective-action problems, and it diagnoses the field's core weaknesses — overreliance on cognitive proxies, thin behavioural measurement, and insufficient attention to cross-national and cross-domain heterogeneity. Nearly every other paper in this set can be read as either exemplifying or answering one of Spampatti et al.'s six prescriptions.

## Testing the limits of static messaging

At the most traditional end, [[Voelkel2026-lc]] delivers a sobering empirical check on decades of "most-cited" climate messaging strategies: in a massive registered-report megastudy, six of ten canonical messages nudge attitudes by only a few percentage points, show little partisan heterogeneity (undercutting audience-tailoring theories), and—critically—fail to move actual pro-environmental donations, sometimes backfiring. This paper operationalises exactly the concern Spampatti et al. raise about high-impact behavioural outcomes being rare and effects being domain-specific; it also empirically confirms the attitude–behaviour gap that motivates the field's turn toward richer, more adaptive interventions. [[Lieu2025-nl]] complements this by dissecting the *misinformation* side of the same coin: crossing CARDS content categories with FLICC fallacy types, it finds that logical structure barely matters but message content does — attacks on climate solutions are uniquely credible and polarizing. Together, Voelkel and Lieu suggest that what is said (content/topic) matters far more than surface rhetorical form (framing strategy or fallacy type), a finding with direct implications for where debunking and prebunking resources should be targeted. [[Xue2025-bp]] extends this content-sensitivity question to the corrective side of the ecosystem, showing that fact-checking itself is saturated with emotional language that shapes engagement and sentiment — undermining the assumption that corrections are neutral, dispassionate information transfers, and reinforcing Spampatti et al.'s call to attend to emotional and motivational drivers alongside cognitive ones.

## Scaling inoculation into the wild

A second strand tests whether inoculation-style "prebunking," long validated in the lab, survives contact with real platforms and modalities. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] is the standout demonstration that a 19-second prebunking ad, deployed at scale on Instagram to hundreds of thousands of users, produces a robust and durable (5-month) improvement in recognising emotional manipulation, alongside behavioural evidence (click-throughs) that Spampatti et al. would count as a genuine high-impact outcome measure. [[Choi2026-bz]] complicates this picture from the audience side: it shows that the *experience* of struggling to authenticate one deepfake carries over to shape confidence and vulnerability toward subsequent fake content, but only within the same modality (text-to-text, video-to-video). This modality-congruent carryover effect suggests inoculation and authentication difficulty are not uniform, format-independent phenomena — a caveat relevant to designing prebunking campaigns across text, image, and video-based platforms alike.

## Conversation as the new unit of intervention

The most concentrated cluster of papers reframes the entire intervention paradigm around dialogue rather than one-shot messages, with LLM-powered chatbots as the technology of choice. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the foundational demonstration: personalised, evidence-tailored GPT-4 conversations durably cut conspiracy belief by roughly 20%, generalising to unrelated conspiracies and behavioural intentions, and directly challenging motivational accounts that treat conspiracy belief as impervious to counterevidence. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic across domains — climate, vaccination, inequality — showing that AI dialogues shift both beliefs and concrete policy support, are most effective precisely among the skeptics traditional messaging struggles to reach, and that GPT-5 adaptively varies its rhetorical strategy (prebunking versus bridging) depending on the interlocutor's baseline stance. [[Szabo2026-rd]] operationalises "conversational inoculation" as a formal method (MindFort/"Forty"), finding that chatbot dialogue outperforms both reading- and writing-based inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled, and identifies interaction-design factors — adaptability, trust-building, minimizing friction — as necessary complements to argument content itself. [[Dubey2026-bl]] adds a crucial audience-acceptance dimension often missing from efficacy-focused work: testing a "balanced news" climate chatbot, it finds — counterintuitively — that higher-conspiracy-belief users trust and accept the chatbot *more* than low-conspiracy users, challenging assumptions that distrustful audiences will reject algorithmic mediators outright.

## Synthesis

Read together, these ten papers chart a field moving from "what should the message say" toward "who is the message from, how is it delivered, and can it adapt in real time." The conversational-AI papers ([[Costello2024-bg]], [[Kotz2026-lk]], [[Szabo2026-rd]], [[Dubey2026-bl]]) suggest that personalisation and responsiveness — not just content accuracy — may be what static campaigns ([[Voelkel2026-lc]]) and formal-fallacy-based corrections ([[Lieu2025-nl]]) have been missing, while the field-scale prebunking success of [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] shows brief, non-conversational interventions can still work when precisely targeted at a specific manipulation technique. Yet [[Choi2026-bz]] and [[Xue2025-bp]] warn that audience psychology (carryover confidence effects, emotional reactivity to corrections) mediates how any of these interventions land, and [[Spampatti2026-kx]] frames the entire enterprise as urgently needing better behavioural measurement, cross-context generalisability, and collective scientific organisation to match the coordination of the vested interests these interventions are designed to counter.
