# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster traces an evolving answer to a deceptively simple question: what kind of message actually changes minds on climate change and adjacent conspiracy beliefs, and does it change behaviour or merely attitude? The papers move from cataloguing what content and delivery formats matter, through testing brief inoculation and framing interventions at scale, to the newest and most disruptive development — using conversational AI as a personalized, dialogic intervention. Running beneath all of this is a methodological throughline about ecological validity: lab findings that fail to travel to real feeds, real chatbots, and real behavioural outcomes.

## What kind of misinformation content matters

[[Lieu2025-nl]] disaggregates "misinformation" into content (CARDS) and rhetorical form (FLICC), finding that logical fallacy type is largely inert while content category is not: attacks on climate *solutions* are judged most credible and most polarizing, especially among conservatives. This result reframes the debunking agenda — it's not enough to teach people to spot fallacious reasoning patterns; interventions need to target the specific claims (solution-aversion narratives, attacks on scientists) that do the real persuasive work. [[Gardam2025-er]] pushes the content question into a different modality, arguing that on image-first platforms like Instagram, climate denial and conspiracy narratives are constructed multimodally, and text-centric coding schemes (like CARDS/FLICC) risk missing where the persuasive work actually happens. Together these two papers suggest the "content" side of message-based intervention design is more heterogeneous — across claim-type, platform, and modality — than intervention studies typically assume.

## Brief interventions at scale: inoculation and framing megastudies

Two large-N studies test whether short, scalable messages can move the needle in the real world. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] shows that a 19-second prebunking video, deployed as an actual Instagram ad to over 375,000 users, produces a robust and durable (5-month) improvement in recognizing emotional manipulation, alongside increased information-seeking — a rare demonstration of inoculation theory surviving contact with a commercial ad platform rather than a lab or YouTube pre-roll. [[Voelkel2026-lc]] tests breadth rather than depth: the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies, in a registered-report megastudy of over 13,000 Americans. The results temper enthusiasm found elsewhere — most messages nudge attitudes by only 1-4 percentage points, show little partisan heterogeneity (undercutting audience-tailoring theories), and critically, none moves actual donation behaviour, with some even backfiring. Read together, these two papers triangulate an emerging consensus: brief message interventions can shift narrow, well-matched attitudinal targets (recognizing a fallacy, endorsing a belief) but the attitude-behaviour gap remains stubborn, and effect sizes at internet scale are real but modest.

## From static messages to conversational agents

The most striking development in this set is the pivot from one-shot messages to interactive, personalized dialogue. [[Costello2024-bg]] establishes proof of concept: three-round GPT-4 conversations tailored to an individual's specific conspiracy and evidence reduce belief by ~20%, durably, and even generalize to unrelated conspiracies — directly challenging motivational/identity-protective accounts of conspiracy belief in favor of an analytic-reasoning model in which resistance reflects insufficiently tailored counterevidence rather than imperviousness to facts. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic across domains (climate, vaccination, inequality) with GPT-5, showing the approach is not conspiracy-specific but a general-purpose persuasive technology — critically, effects concentrate among *skeptics* and are amplified by trust in science, and policy support requires directly targeting policy attitudes rather than hoping belief change spills over. [[Szabo2026-rd]] approaches the same dialogic turn from an HCI angle, building a chatbot ("Forty") that operationalizes "conversational inoculation," finding it outperforms static reading/writing inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled — and, importantly, identifying *why*: adaptability, trust-building, and minimizing interactional friction, not just argument content, drive efficacy. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the optimistic chatbot narrative from the reception side: testing "balanced" climate chatbots across conspiracy-belief levels, it finds — counterintuitively — that higher-conspiracy users trust and rate chatbots *more* positively than skeptical-of-conspiracy users, suggesting acceptance of AI messengers doesn't map neatly onto priors about defensiveness, and that perceived usefulness/trust (not risk or ease-of-use) drive adoption intentions.

## Synthesis and meta-level lessons

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] sits above the empirical papers as a field-level diagnosis, arguing that misinformation research should borrow from climate psychology's longer fight against organized denial: move beyond cognitive proxies toward real-world behavioural outcomes (a critique the Voelkel donation-null result vindicates), integrate individual- and system-level analysis, attend to cross-national heterogeneity (echoing the content/context sensitivity in Lieu and Gardam), and treat motivational/emotional/social drivers as co-equal to informational ones — a frame that anticipates why static fact-corrections underperform relative to tailored AI dialogue. Read as a whole, this hub note traces a field maturing from "which message wins" toward "which relationship, and which delivery architecture, wins" — with static, one-size-fits-all messaging (however well-cited) showing diminishing returns, and personalized, adaptive, dialogic formats (chatbot or AI) emerging as the more promising, if still nascent and unevenly received, frontier.
