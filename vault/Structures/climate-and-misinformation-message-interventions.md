# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The arc: from diagnosing stickiness to engineering resistance

The papers in this collection trace a historical and conceptual arc from understanding *why* misinformation persists to testing *what* can be done about it, with climate change serving throughout as both a paradigm case and a uniquely stubborn testbed. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] anchors the earliest stage of this arc, documenting the "continued influence effect" and establishing that simple retraction is rarely sufficient — corrections can backfire, worldview shapes resistance, and only specific techniques (pre-exposure warning, repetition, alternative causal narratives) reliably help. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] complicates the worldview-centric story by arguing that poor truth discernment is driven less by motivated partisan reasoning than by inattention and lack of deliberation, reframing the intervention target from "defeating bias" to "cultivating attention to accuracy." Together these two reviews set up the field's foundational tension — is resistance to correction mainly motivational/ideological or cognitive/attentional? — a tension that reverberates through nearly every experimental paper that follows.

## Inoculation and prebunking: from theory to scale

A cluster of papers operationalizes "prebunking" as the dominant applied strategy. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that brief inoculation videos deployed as Instagram ads can durably (five months) improve real users' ability to detect emotional manipulation, answering longstanding ecological-validity concerns about lab-based inoculation work. [[Szabo2026-rd]] pushes inoculation into conversational territory, introducing "Conversational Inoculation" via an LLM chatbot and showing it outperforms static reading/writing-based inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled — importantly, the mechanism is not better arguments but interaction design (adaptability, trust, reduced friction). [[Lieu2025-nl]] interrogates inoculation's logical-fallacy-based cousin, the FLICC framework, against the content-based CARDS taxonomy, finding that *what* misinformation attacks (solutions, scientists) matters far more than *how* it is fallacious — a finding with direct implications for which content prebunking efforts should prioritize.

## Conversational AI as a persuasive and corrective technology

A second cluster investigates large language models not as inoculators but as direct persuaders/correctors. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the pivotal paper here, showing that personalized, evidence-dense AI dialogue can durably cut conspiracy belief by ~20%, challenging motivational accounts of conspiracy resistance and reviving a "give people good enough evidence" account of belief change. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic across climate, vaccination, and inequality, showing AI dialogue shifts beliefs and policy support most among skeptics and is moderated heavily by trust in science — intriguingly, policy attitudes require direct targeting rather than relying on belief change to spill over. [[Lin2025-xp]] generalizes further into electoral persuasion, finding AI dialogue persuasion exceeds traditional ad effects and operates mainly through facts/evidence rather than manipulation, while also surfacing a troubling asymmetry: right-leaning AI advocacy is less accurate. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] closes this cluster with a governance-relevant twist — disclosing AI identity does little to blunt persuasion, but disclosing *persuasive intent* roughly halves it, reframing transparency policy debates around intent rather than machine identity. [[Dubey2026-bl]] offers a complementary HCI angle on chatbot acceptance rather than belief change per se, finding the counterintuitive result that higher-conspiracy users trust "balanced" chatbots more than low-conspiracy users, questioning assumptions that conspiracy believers are reflexively distrustful of corrective technology.

## The limits of message framing and the attitude–behaviour gap

Running parallel to the AI-dialogue thread is a more skeptical strand focused on traditional message framing. [[Voelkel2026-lc]] delivers the field's most sobering finding: the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies, tested in a megastudy, produce only small attitude shifts, show no partisan heterogeneity (undermining targeted-framing theories), and fail entirely to move costly behaviour like donations. This resonates with [[Xue2025-bp]]'s finding that even fact-checking content — nominally the opposite of persuasive framing — is saturated with emotional language that shapes engagement and sentiment, suggesting emotionality is not an incidental feature of corrective communication but a strategic lever across the board. Read together, these two papers suggest that message content/framing may matter less than structural or interactional factors (dialogue, personalization, algorithmic context) emphasized elsewhere in the collection.

## System-level framing and self-reflective synthesis

[[Brady2026-ln]] shifts the unit of intervention from the message to the algorithm, showing that engagement-based ranking — independent of any specific message — amplifies moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social norms about political dialogue, while a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can partially correct this without sacrificing engagement. This complements the message-level interventions above by showing that *exposure architecture* itself is a lever, not just message content. Finally, [[Spampatti2026-kx]] steps back to synthesize the whole terrain, arguing that misinformation research should borrow climate psychology's hard-won lessons: move beyond cognitive proxies toward real-world behaviour, integrate individual- and system-level analysis (directly echoing [[Brady2026-ln]]'s algorithmic framing and [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s attitude-behaviour gap), attend to cross-national generalizability, and organize collectively against coordinated opposition. This closing paper effectively reframes the entire set — inoculation videos, AI dialogues, megastudies, algorithm redesigns — as fragments of a maturing science that still needs to close the gap between attitudinal effects and the collective-action behaviours that actually matter for climate and democratic resilience.
