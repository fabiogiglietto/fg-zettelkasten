# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The arc: from static messages to adaptive dialogue

The papers filed here trace a shift in how the field imagines intervention — from one-shot, broadcast-style messages toward interactive, personalized, and increasingly AI-mediated conversations. At one end sits [[Voelkel2026-lc]], a registered-report megastudy testing the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies on a huge US sample. Its sobering conclusion — small effects, little partisan heterogeneity, and no movement on costly behaviour like donations — sets the empirical floor against which every other intervention in this set should be judged. If the best-known static messages barely move behaviour, the case for more adaptive, dialogic, or emotionally resonant formats becomes more urgent, and much of the rest of the collection can be read as a response to this floor.

[[Lieu2025-nl]] complicates the picture from the misinformation side rather than the persuasion side: crossing CARDS content categories with FLICC fallacy types, it finds that *what* misinformation claims (attacking climate solutions or scientists) matters far more than *how* it argues (which logical fallacy it deploys). This is an important corrective for message-design research that has often assumed fallacy-focused "logic-checking" is the active ingredient — the content target, not the rhetorical form, seems to drive perceived veracity and political polarization.

## Prebunking and inoculation: from lab to feed to conversation

A second thread concerns inoculation theory and its evolution toward scale and interactivity. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that a static, 19-second prebunking video can be deployed as an ordinary Instagram ad and still produce durable (five-month), large effects on recognizing emotional manipulation — evidence that classic inoculation content, at least, survives the jump from lab to real-world feed. [[Szabo2026-rd]] pushes inoculation one step further into interactivity, comparing traditional reading/writing inoculation against a chatbot-delivered "Conversational Inoculation." The chatbot outperforms static formats once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled, and qualitative analysis locates the advantage not in argument content per se but in interaction design — adaptability, trust-building, and minimizing friction. Read together with [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], this suggests two viable but distinct paths to scalable resistance: cheap, broadcast prebunking for baseline effects, and costlier, adaptive dialogue for larger, individually tailored gains.

## Conversational AI as a persuasive technology

The most striking development in this set is the emergence of LLM dialogue as an intervention class in its own right. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the foundational demonstration: personalized, evidence-dense GPT-4 conversations durably cut conspiracy belief by ~20%, generalizing beyond the focal conspiracy and surviving two months — directly challenging motivational accounts that treat conspiracy belief as impervious to counterevidence. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic across domains (climate, vaccination, inequality) with GPT-5, showing that brief dialogues shift both beliefs and contested policy support, with the largest gains among initial skeptics and those high in trust in science — precisely the audiences message-based campaigns have struggled to reach. Together these two studies reframe the debate from "can messages work" to "can tailored, evidence-responsive dialogue succeed where generic messaging plateaus," directly answering the limitations [[Voelkel2026-lc]] documents for static content.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] adds a trust-and-acceptance layer to this AI-dialogue thread: testing "balanced" chatbots that mix mainstream and alternative climate perspectives, it finds — counterintuitively — that higher-conspiracy-belief users trust and accept such chatbots *more* than low-conspiracy users, challenging assumptions of blanket distrust among conspiracy-prone audiences and suggesting perceived usefulness/trust (not risk or ease of use) are what govern chatbot acceptance. This complements [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] by addressing not whether AI dialogue changes minds, but whether the very audiences who most need persuading will engage with it at all.

## Content, form, and the denial ecosystem

Two more technically oriented papers extend the topic into the visual and methodological terrain where much contemporary denial and conspiracy content actually circulates. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues that Instagram climate denial is fundamentally multimodal and that text-centric analysis misses how images carry primary rhetorical weight, proposing a visual-first discourse methodology. [[Arminio2025-tw]] operationalizes a version of this insight computationally, showing that vision-language models can cluster climate-related Instagram images by connotative (culturally embedded) meaning far better than CNN pipelines, with interpretable TF-IDF summaries. Neither paper tests an intervention directly, but both matter for this topic because they characterize the actual content ecosystem — increasingly visual, meme-driven, symbolically dense — that inoculation and debunking messages must eventually contend with; message-design and chatbot research risks irrelevance if it continues to target only textual claims.

## A reflexive synthesis

Finally, [[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as the collection's meta-commentary, explicitly arguing that misinformation research should borrow from climate psychology's more mature engagement with organised opposition, motivational (not just cognitive) drivers, and real-world behavioural outcomes. Its critique — that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure high-impact real-world behaviour — lands as a direct challenge to several papers here that rely on short-term attitudinal or accuracy-recognition proxies ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], [[Szabo2026-rd]], [[Lieu2025-nl]]), while validating the megastudy behavioural rigor of [[Voelkel2026-lc]] and the durability testing of [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]]. It also foregrounds a tension latent throughout the set: as interventions move toward corporate-platform deployment (Instagram ads, LLM chatbots), the question of company collaboration, data access, and vested-interest distortion — largely bracketed in the empirical papers — becomes unavoidable for the field's next phase.
