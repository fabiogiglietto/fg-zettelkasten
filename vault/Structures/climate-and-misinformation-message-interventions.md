# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## Framing the field: why climate and misinformation belong together

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as something close to a field-defining statement for this whole collection, arguing that climate psychology and misinformation research are converging crises that should borrow methods and humility from one another. Its call to move beyond cognitive proxies toward real-world behavioural outcomes, to attend to cross-national heterogeneity, and to treat corporate/platform entanglements as a methodological hazard rather than a footnote, reads almost as an annotated table of contents for the other fourteen papers filed here. Several of its diagnostic complaints — that interventions overweight belief change and underweight behaviour, that findings from WEIRD/US samples travel poorly, and that vested interests shape both the problem and the research ecosystem — are directly tested, confirmed, or complicated by the empirical work below.

## The baseline problem: why corrections and short messages underperform

Two older, foundational reviews set the ceiling on what message-based interventions can plausibly achieve. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] establishes the continued-influence effect: retractions rarely eliminate reliance on misinformation, can backfire through familiarity or worldview threat, and only reliably improve when paired with alternative causal narratives, forewarning, or repetition. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] complicates the standard partisan-motivated-reasoning story, showing that poor truth discernment is better explained by inattention and lack of reflection than by identity-protective cognition — a finding with direct consequences for intervention design, since it implies that nudging attention to accuracy (rather than correcting belief content per se) can be a lever. Together these two reviews supply the theoretical scaffolding — coherence gaps, backfire risk, attention vs. belief — that the newer message and chatbot studies either validate or push against.

That ceiling is empirically confirmed at scale by [[Voelkel2026-lc]], whose megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messages finds only small, largely belief-level effects (1–4 percentage points) that do not transfer to costly behaviour like donations, and that are strikingly similar across Democrats and Republicans — undercutting the audience-targeting assumption that drives much applied climate communication. This is the most sobering data point in the set: short-form, one-shot messages, however well-cited in the literature, do not reliably move the needle on anything beyond self-report attitudes, which is precisely the "overemphasis on cognitive proxies" that [[Spampatti2026-kx]] warns against.

## Where the content comes from: fallacies, framings, and emotion

If single "best" messages underperform, what differentiates misinformation that *does* stick? [[Lieu2025-nl]] decomposes climate misinformation along content (CARDS) versus logical-form (FLICC) lines and finds that fallacy type barely matters, but *content category* does — attacks on climate solutions are rated most credible and most polarizing, especially among conservatives. This pushes against purely logic-based "fallacy inoculation" approaches and toward content-specific debunking priorities. [[Xue2025-bp]] complements this from the correction side, showing that fact-checking posts themselves are saturated with emotional language despite their objectivity norms, and that this emotionality shapes engagement and sentiment toward fact-checked targets — a reminder that the affective dynamics documented in misinformation also operate in the corrective genre meant to counter it. [[Choi2026-bz]] adds a cognitive-process layer: difficulty authenticating one piece of (deep)fake content carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to shape confidence and susceptibility toward subsequent fake content — suggesting that single-exposure experimental designs common across this literature may understate real-world, sequential vulnerability.

## Prebunking and inoculation: from lab to feed

The inoculation-theory tradition gets its most ecologically valid test yet in [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], which scales a 19-second prebunking video to over 375,000 Instagram users and finds a durable (5-month), substantial improvement in recognizing emotional manipulation, at low cost — a rare instance in this set of a message-based intervention demonstrating both effect size and real-platform scalability, partially answering Spampatti et al.'s call for high-impact behavioural outcomes (here, click-through behaviour, not just self-report). [[Szabo2026-rd]] extends inoculation into interactive territory, showing that LLM-powered "conversational inoculation" (MindFort/"Forty") outperforms traditional reading- and writing-based inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is accounted for, with qualitative analysis pointing to adaptability, trust-building, and minimized friction as the active ingredients — effectively arguing that *how* an inoculation is delivered (dialogic vs. static) matters as much as its content.

## Conversational AI as a persuasion technology

This chatbot/dialogue thread is the collection's most rapidly developing arc. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the pivotal result: personalized, evidence-based GPT-4 dialogues durably (≥2 months) reduce belief in specific conspiracies by ~20%, generalizing to unrelated conspiracies, and challenge motivational accounts of conspiracy belief in favor of analytic-reasoning accounts — directly extending Pennycook et al.'s reasoning-deficit framework into an applied, scalable intervention. [[Kotz2026-lk]] generalizes this logic across climate, vaccination, and inequality, finding that AI dialogues are *most* effective precisely among initially skeptical participants (the audience traditional messaging struggles to reach), moderated strongly by trust in science — a finding that resonates with Voelkel et al.'s null partisan-heterogeneity result but flips it into optimism: skeptics are reachable, just not via static messages. [[Lin2025-xp]] broadens the frame further still, showing AI dialogue persuasion effects on voting behavior across four democratic contests, driven mainly by factual/evidentiary "central route" argumentation rather than manipulation — though it also surfaces an asymmetry troubling for the field's neutrality claims: AI advocating right-leaning candidates was systematically less accurate, echoing broader information-environment asymmetries that Spampatti et al. flag as a generalizability concern.

[[Dubey2026-bl]]'s "balanced news chatbot" work complicates the implicit assumption running through this cluster that conspiracy-minded users will resist technologically mediated correction: counter to expectation, higher-conspiracy-belief users trusted and rated the chatbot *more* positively than low-belief users, suggesting that perceived balance/usefulness can override anticipated reactance — a genuinely counter-intuitive result that nuances both the inoculation and the AI-dialogue findings above.

## Infrastructure, measurement, and governance context

Two papers sit slightly outside the direct message-testing arc but supply necessary scaffolding. [[Arminio2025-tw]] is methodological: it shows VLLM-based image-to-text pipelines capture *connotative* meaning in climate-related social media imagery far better than CNN approaches, offering a tool for future researchers studying how visual climate (mis)information circulates and clusters — relevant to any future structure-note successor that wants to extend message-testing beyond text. [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] closes the loop by examining the platform governance layer these interventions operate within, finding that climate misinformation is rarely treated as a formal, high-priority moderation category, with platforms favoring soft interventions and discursive "governance-washing" over enforceable rules — a sobering structural backdrop to the individual-level interventions reviewed above: however well a prebunking ad or chatbot performs experimentally, it operates inside platform ecosystems that largely decline to specify or enforce climate misinformation policy, and that can withdraw infrastructure (as Meta did with fact-checking and its Climate Science Center) at will.

## Synthesis

Read together, these papers trace an arc from diagnosis to intervention to scale-testing to structural critique. The reviews ([[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], [[Pennycook2021-jq]]) establish why corrections are hard and misattributed to motivated reasoning; the content/process studies ([[Lieu2025-nl]], [[Xue2025-bp]], [[Choi2026-bz]]) show which features of misinformation and correction actually move perception; the megastudy ([[Voelkel2026-lc]]) delivers a humbling verdict on static messaging at scale; and the inoculation and AI-dialogue studies ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], [[Szabo2026-rd]], [[Costello2024-bg]], [[Kotz2026-lk]], [[Lin2025-xp]], [[Dubey2026-bl]]) collectively suggest that interactivity, personalization, and dialogic adaptability are the most promising levers beyond one-shot messaging — exactly the kind of "motivational, emotional, and social" engagement that [[Spampatti2026-kx]] argues cognitive-only interventions lack. Yet [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] and the partisan-asymmetry findings in [[Lin2025-xp]] serve as a check on techno-optimism: even highly effective individual-level interventions are deployed within platform and information environments that are neither neutral nor reliably supportive of corrective infrastructure, meaning durable misinformation resistance likely requires pairing message-level innovation with the systemic and collective-action attention that Spampatti et al. call for.
