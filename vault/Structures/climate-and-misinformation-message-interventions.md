# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## Why Corrections Fail, and What Psychology Has Learned

The baseline problem this literature responds to is articulated most clearly by [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]]: misinformation is "sticky," retractions at best halve reliance on false beliefs, and corrections can even backfire through familiarity or worldview-protective reasoning. This classic diagnosis sets the agenda that the rest of the collection tries to answer — can we design messages, dialogues, or systems that do better than simple fact-correction? [[Spampatti2026-kx]] reframes this agenda at a field level, arguing that misinformation research should borrow from the more mature discipline of climate psychology: treat misinformation as a collective-action problem entangled with organised vested interests, move beyond cognitive proxies toward real-world behavioural outcomes, and attend to cross-country and cross-domain heterogeneity rather than assuming US-partisan findings generalise. Read together, these two papers bracket the rest of the topic: Lewandowsky explains the cognitive stickiness that interventions must overcome, while Spampatti insists that success be measured against motivational, social, and systemic factors, not just belief-change in the lab.

## Scaling Inoculation: From Lab Demonstrations to Live Feeds and Chatbots

A cluster of papers tests the classic inoculation-theory prescription — warn and pre-expose people to weakened forms of manipulation — for real-world deployability. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] pushes prebunking out of the lab entirely, showing that a 19-second Instagram ad exposing users to a single fearmongering technique produces a 21-point accuracy gain that persists five months later, at a cost low enough for platform-scale rollout. This answers one of Spampatti's calls directly: a real behavioural outcome (click-through, recognition under field conditions), not just a survey proxy. [[Szabo2026-rd]] complicates the "static inoculation" model by introducing *conversational* inoculation — an LLM chatbot ("Forty") that adapts its refutation dialogically rather than delivering fixed text — and finds that, once individual baseline susceptibility is accounted for, the chatbot condition outperforms both traditional reading- and writing-based inoculation. Its qualitative analysis of *why* (adaptability, trust-building, scaffolding independent thought, interactional friction) begins to specify mechanisms that static prebunking videos cannot probe, setting up the pivot from "inoculating messages" to "inoculating interactions" that dominates the next cluster.

## Conversational AI as Corrective and Persuasive Agent

The chatbot thread is taken furthest by three papers that treat dialogic AI not as a vehicle for brief inoculation but as a sustained persuasive interlocutor. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the pivotal demonstration: personalized, multi-round GPT-4 dialogues durably cut belief in specific conspiracy theories by ~20%, generalising to unrelated conspiracies and surviving two months — directly challenging motivational accounts of conspiracy belief as impervious to evidence, and suggesting instead that depth and personalization, not willingness, were the missing ingredients in prior debunking failures. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic beyond conspiracies to structurally different contested domains (climate, vaccination, inequality), showing that brief evidence-based AI dialogues shift both beliefs and policy support, with the largest gains among initial skeptics and those high in trust in science — a moderator profile that echoes Spampatti's call for attention to contextual heterogeneity. Where these two papers ask whether AI dialogue *can* move entrenched attitudes, [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] asks a governance question hanging over the whole enterprise: does disclosing that persuasion is happening undercut it? It finds that merely labeling content as AI-generated does little, but disclosing *persuasive intent* roughly halves the effect — implying that the chatbot interventions documented by Costello and Kotz derive real force from concealed-intent dynamics that transparency regulation could blunt. [[Dubey2026-bl]] adds a complementary, more HCI-flavoured finding: a "balanced" chatbot presenting both mainstream and alternative climate perspectives is, counter to expectations, trusted *more* by people with stronger conspiracy beliefs, with perceived usefulness and trust (not ease of use) driving acceptance — suggesting design-level trust-building, highlighted qualitatively in Szabo's chatbot study, may matter more than ideological matching for whether contested audiences even engage with a corrective tool.

## What Content and Framing Actually Move (and What They Don't)

A second cluster interrogates the persuasive message itself, independent of delivery agent. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s megastudy is the field's reality check on climate messaging: of the ten most-cited strategies, several fail to replicate, the rest shift attitudes only by a few percentage points with no partisan heterogeneity, and none move actual donation behaviour — a direct empirical instance of the attitude-behaviour gap that Spampatti warns the field to take seriously. [[Lieu2025-nl]] dissects the anatomy of climate *mis*information rather than corrective messaging, crossing CARDS content categories with FLICC rhetorical fallacies; it finds that logical-fallacy type barely matters but content does — misinformation attacking climate solutions is rated most credible and most polarizing — implying that debunking resources are better targeted at specific contrarian claims than at generic fallacy-spotting. [[Xue2025-bp]] turns the lens onto the correctors themselves, showing that fact-checking posts, despite their normative claim to objectivity, are often emotionally charged, and that this emotionality shapes engagement and sentiment toward the fact-checked target — a finding that sits uneasily alongside inoculation and AI-dialogue approaches, which largely assume corrective communication is (or should be) dispassionate, and alongside van der Linden's framing of emotional manipulation as the thing to be defended against rather than deployed.

## A Methodological Outlier with Future Relevance

[[Arminio2025-tw]] stands apart as a tooling contribution rather than an intervention study, but it bears on this topic's empirical infrastructure: by showing that vision-language models can cluster climate-related social media images by connotative, culturally embedded meaning (e.g., grouping wind turbines and solar panels as "renewable energy" imagery) rather than mere visual similarity, it offers a way to map the visual misinformation and framing ecosystem — memes, protest imagery, denialist iconography — that the text-focused studies above largely bracket. As message-based interventions move toward platforms like Instagram (as in van der Linden's study), tools of this kind may become necessary for identifying what visual content inoculation and chatbot interventions should actually target.

## The Arc

Taken together, these twelve papers trace a movement from diagnosing why corrections fail (Lewandowsky) toward increasingly interactive, personalized, and scaled remedies (prebunking ads, conversational inoculation, AI dialogue), while a parallel strand keeps testing whether the underlying content of messages — framings, fallacies, emotional tone — matters as much as delivery format. The throughline, made explicit in Spampatti's closing synthesis, is a growing insistence that effectiveness be judged by durable, real-world, behaviourally consequential outcomes and by generalisability across audiences and contexts, rather than by lab-bound shifts in stated belief — a standard that Voelkel's null behavioural effects and Rauchfleisch's transparency trade-off show is still far from reliably met.
