# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## Setting the terms: why climate and misinformation research need each other

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] serves as something like a methodological preamble for this whole cluster: it argues that misinformation research, still a comparatively young field, should borrow from the more battle-tested tradition of climate psychology, which has spent decades contending with organised denial and vested interests. Its central diagnostic point — that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world behavioural outcomes, and that effects on belief rarely translate into effects on sharing or costly action — reads almost as a preregistered critique of several other papers in this collection before they were even filed. That gap between attitude change and behaviour change becomes the organising tension for the rest of the topic.

## Framing effects on climate attitudes: diminishing returns from message content alone

[[Voelkel2026-lc]] puts Spampatti's warning to an empirical test at scale. Its megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies finds that several do move belief, concern, and policy support by small margins (1–4 percentage points), that these effects are strikingly non-partisan (contradicting the assumption that audience-tailored framing is required), but that none of them shift actual pro-environmental donations — and one, Scientific Consensus framing, even backfires on giving. This is the attitude–behaviour gap that Spampatti flags, demonstrated directly rather than asserted. [[Lieu2025-nl]] approaches the same terrain from the misinformation side rather than the persuasion side, crossing the CARDS taxonomy of contrarian claims with the FLICC taxonomy of logical fallacies. Its finding that logical-fallacy framing has essentially no effect on perceived veracity, while claim *content* (especially attacks on climate solutions) does — and does so in a strongly polarising way for conservatives — suggests that what matters in climate misinformation is less the rhetorical form of an argument than its target. Together, these two papers imply that both persuasive and manipulative climate messages operate primarily through content-specific, audience-conditioned pathways rather than generic framing or fallacy structure, a nuance that a purely inoculation-based or purely message-testing paradigm would miss on its own.

## Prebunking at scale: inoculation moves from the lab to the feed

A second arc concerns inoculation theory's migration from controlled experiments into live social platforms. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] (and its near-identical companion entry [[Allcott2019-gn]], reporting the same Instagram Story Feed campaign) demonstrates that a 19-second prebunking video, deployed as an ordinary ad to hundreds of thousands of UK users, can lift accuracy in spotting emotional manipulation by over 20 percentage points, with the effect persisting five months later and accompanied by increased information-seeking — all at a cost below typical platform ad rates. This is arguably the strongest existing proof that inoculation is not just theoretically sound but operationally scalable outside the lab, addressing exactly the ecological-validity concern Spampatti raises about the field's evidentiary base.

[[Szabo2026-rd]] extends inoculation from a one-way broadcast format into a two-way conversational one. Its MindFort/"Forty" chatbot system, tested against traditional Reading and Writing inoculation conditions, finds that conversational inoculation outperforms both once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled for — not because the underlying arguments differ, but because of interaction-design factors: adaptability to the user, scaffolding of independent reasoning, and trust built through dialogic partnership rather than one-directional refutation. This positions conversational inoculation as a bridge between the static prebunking of van der Linden/Allcott and the fully personalised, LLM-driven persuasion explored next.

## Conversational AI as a persuasive and corrective agent

The strongest thematic throughline in this topic is the emergence of large language models as dialogic interlocutors capable of shifting even entrenched beliefs. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the foundational demonstration: personalised, evidence-responsive GPT-4 dialogues durably reduced conspiracy belief by roughly 20%, with effects persisting two months, generalising to unrelated conspiracies, and holding even among the most committed believers — directly challenging motivational/identity-protective accounts of conspiracist cognition in favour of an analytic-reasoning model where the previous failure of debunking reflected insufficient personalisation rather than genuine imperviousness to evidence. [[Kotz2026-lk]] generalises this logic across domains, showing that brief GPT-5 dialogues shift beliefs and policy support not only on conspiracies but on climate change, vaccination, and economic inequality alike, with the largest gains concentrated among initially skeptical participants and amplified by trust in science — precisely the "hardest to reach" audience that framing-only interventions like Voelkel's megastudy struggle to move. Read together, Costello and Kotz suggest that depth and personalisation, not message content per se, may be the active ingredient that closes the gap between attitude change and durable, generalisable belief revision that Voelkel's short-form messages could not achieve.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the optimistic chatbot narrative by examining trust rather than efficacy. Its "balanced news" climate chatbot, presenting mainstream and conspiratorial perspectives side by side, is counterintuitively trusted *more* by high-conspiracy-belief users than low-belief users — suggesting that perceived neutrality itself, independent of content, can be what earns trust from otherwise skeptical audiences. This finding sits productively alongside Kotz's observation that skeptics are the most persuadable group when engaged directly: both papers point to a design lesson that acknowledging or incorporating audience skepticism, rather than confronting it head-on with corrective content, may be key to engagement.

## Complications: metacognitive costs and the limits of claim-level correction

Two papers push back against an implicit assumption running through the above — that identifying manipulation, once trained, generalises cleanly. [[Choi2026-bz]] shows a "modality-congruent carryover effect": difficulty authenticating one instance of (deep)fake news shapes confidence and susceptibility toward *subsequent* fake content, but only within the same modality (text-to-text, video-to-video). This suggests inoculation and prebunking effects documented elsewhere in this topic (e.g., van der Linden's video-based intervention) may not transfer as cleanly across formats as hoped, and that repeated exposure to synthetic media carries its own psychological costs to self-confidence in truth discernment that need to be measured alongside accuracy gains.

[[Ramos2026-qo]], drawing on the 2024 Valencia flood disinformation case, argues from practitioner experience that claim-level fact-checking — the implicit unit of correction in most of the experimental work here — is insufficient against disinformation that behaves as an evolving, emotionally resonant *narrative* rather than a discrete false statement. Its proposal to complement fact-checking with narrative monitoring and argument-checking (targeting fallacious premises and warrants rather than individual claims) echoes Lieu's finding that content and argumentative target matter more than fallacy type, and offers a real-world, crisis-driven counterpart to the more controlled, laboratory-style interventions dominating the rest of the topic.

## The arc

Taken together, this set traces a movement from message-testing (Voelkel, Lieu) to inoculation-as-broadcast (van der Linden/Allcott) to inoculation-as-dialogue (Szabo) to fully personalised AI persuasion (Costello, Kotz, Dubey), with Spampatti's programmatic critique bookending the collection as a reminder that behavioural, cross-contextual, and system-level validity remain unresolved, and Choi and Ramos supplying the necessary caution that both individual cognition and the structure of disinformation itself are more format- and narrative-dependent than a purely message-based intervention paradigm can fully capture.
