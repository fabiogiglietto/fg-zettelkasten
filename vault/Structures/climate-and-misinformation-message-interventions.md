# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## Foundations: why corrections fail and how discernment actually works

Two classic reviews anchor this literature by explaining the cognitive substrate that all later interventions must contend with. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] establishes the "continued influence effect": retractions rarely erase reliance on misinformation, can backfire through familiarity or worldview-driven entrenchment, and only reliably work when paired with pre-exposure warnings, repetition, or—crucially—an alternative causal narrative that fills the explanatory gap left by debunking. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] complicates the folk narrative that partisanship drives fake-news susceptibility, showing instead that poor truth discernment stems mainly from lazy, non-reflective reasoning and that the belief–sharing gap is largely an artifact of inattention rather than motivated deception. Together these papers frame the central tension that later studies in this set keep returning to: is misinformation resistance chiefly a *cognitive/attentional* problem (fixable by nudges, prompts, and better arguments) or a *motivational/structural* one (requiring narrative coherence, trust-building, or systemic reform)? Most of the applied work below can be read as testing one or both of these accounts.

## Framing and content at scale: what climate messages actually move

[[Voelkel2026-lc]] takes the discernment-versus-motivation debate into the domain of climate persuasion itself, running a megastudy on the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies. Its sobering finding—small, largely partisan-invariant attitude shifts but no effect on costly behavior like donations—echoes Lewandowsky's caution that correcting beliefs is not the same as changing conduct, and undercuts assumptions (shared by some inoculation and chatbot studies below) that audience-tailored framing is required to reach skeptics. [[Lieu2025-nl]] complements this by dissecting the *anatomy* of climate misinformation rather than pro-climate messaging: crossing the CARDS content taxonomy with the FLICC fallacy taxonomy, it finds that content category (especially attacks on climate solutions) drives perceived veracity and polarization far more than the logical fallacy used to deliver it. Read alongside Voelkel, this suggests message *content and target* matter more than surface rhetorical form—a claim with direct implications for the argument-based approaches discussed later.

## Inoculation and prebunking: scaling resistance before exposure

Two papers test "prebunking" as a proactive alternative to post-hoc correction. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that a 19-second inoculation video, deployed as an ordinary Instagram ad to hundreds of thousands of users, durably improves recognition of emotional manipulation for at least five months and increases information-seeking—strong evidence that lab-validated inoculation theory (the tradition Lewandowsky gestures toward with "pre-exposure warnings") generalizes to real platforms at low cost. [[Szabo2026-rd]] pushes inoculation further by making it interactive: its MindFort chatbot ("Forty") implements Conversational Inoculation and outperforms traditional reading- and writing-based refutation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled for. Its qualitative analysis—identifying adaptability, trust-building, and interactional friction as mechanisms—begins to answer a question the Instagram study leaves open: *why* do brief interventions work, and can dialogic, personalized delivery outperform static messaging? This sets up a natural bridge to the LLM-dialogue papers.

## Conversational AI as a corrective and persuasive agent

This subgroup is the most concentrated cluster in the topic and traces a clear arc from single-issue debunking to general-purpose, skeptic-targeted persuasion. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the pivotal demonstration: personalized, evidence-dense GPT-4 dialogues durably cut conspiracy belief by ~20%, generalizing across topics and persisting for months—directly challenging the motivated-reasoning account that treats conspiracy belief as impervious to evidence, and instead vindicating the "classical reasoning" position that [[Pennycook2021-jq]] argues for in the fake-news domain. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic beyond conspiracies to structurally different contested issues (climate, vaccines, inequality), showing GPT-5 dialogues shift beliefs and even policy support, with the largest gains among initial skeptics and those high in trust in science—a finding that qualifies Voelkel's pessimism about behavior change by suggesting interactive, adaptive argumentation (rather than static short-form messaging) may reach exactly the audiences campaigns most need to move. Text analysis showing the model adapts its rhetorical strategy (prebunking/steelmanning for skeptics, bridging-to-action for supporters) operationalizes the "adaptability" mechanism [[Szabo2026-rd]] identifies qualitatively.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] adds a necessary complication: when chatbots are designed to present *balanced* mainstream/conspiratorial content rather than corrective arguments, users higher in conspiracy belief actually trust and accept them *more* than low-conspiracy users, contradicting expectations from the distrust literature. This suggests that a chatbot's perceived neutrality or utility, not just the strength of its evidence, shapes uptake—an important boundary condition for the more directive, evidence-forward designs in Costello and Kotz, and a reminder that trust dynamics (echoed in Kotz's finding that trust in science moderates persuasion) are central to whether any conversational intervention gets a hearing at all.

## Beyond isolated claims: modality, narrative, and systemic framing

Three papers push the discussion outward from single-message effects toward the broader ecology in which corrections operate. [[Choi2026-bz]] shows that difficulty authenticating one piece of (deep)fake content carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to shape confidence and vulnerability toward subsequent content—implying that intervention effects (and failures) documented elsewhere may not be independent events but part of a chain of experience that platforms and researchers rarely track. [[Ramos2026-qo]], writing from inside a fact-checking organization responding to the 2024 Valencia floods, argues that claim-level correction (the unit of analysis in most studies above) is insufficient against evolving disinformation narratives, proposing "argument-checking" that targets the fallacious premises binding a narrative together—an applied, crisis-tested extension of Lewandowsky's alternative-causal-narrative principle and a real-world complement to Lieu's finding that content framing matters more than fallacy type in the lab.

Finally, [[Spampatti2026-kx]] steps back to argue that misinformation research as a whole should learn from climate psychology's longer struggle against organized denial: it should measure real-world behavior (fewer than 1% of studies currently do), integrate individual- and system-level analyses, attend to cross-national heterogeneity, and organize collectively against vested interests. This paper effectively names the throughline connecting the rest of the set—from megastudies (Voelkel) probing behavioral rather than attitudinal outcomes, to AI-dialogue studies (Costello, Kotz) demonstrating individual-level belief change without yet showing societal-scale impact, to Ramos's warning that narratives and organized actors, not isolated claims, are the real adversary. The topic's arc thus moves from cognitive mechanism (Lewandowsky, Pennycook) through message and format testing (Voelkel, Lieu, van der Linden, Szabo) to a new generation of adaptive, conversational tools (Costello, Kotz, Dubey) and closes on a call to reframe the whole enterprise in systemic, behaviorally grounded terms (Spampatti, Ramos, Choi).
