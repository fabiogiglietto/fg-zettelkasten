# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## Setting the Terms: What Should Message Interventions Achieve?

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] provides the conceptual scaffolding for this whole cluster of studies. Written as a comment piece rather than an empirical report, it argues that misinformation research is a younger field that can borrow directly from climate psychology's decades-long experience with organised denial and vested interests. Its central provocation—that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world behavioural outcomes, and that information-provision alone is insufficient because it neglects motivational, emotional, and social drivers—reads almost as a rubric against which the other ten papers in this collection can be evaluated. Several of them explicitly test the sufficiency of pure information provision; others attempt to move beyond attitudinal proxies toward durable, generalisable, or behaviourally consequential effects; and a subset directly confronts the corporate/platform entanglements the piece warns about (Meta-hosted field trials, GPT-powered chatbots).

## Testing Static Messages: Limited Returns from Content and Framing

The most direct empirical test of "does the message content matter" comes from [[Voelkel2026-lc]], a large registered-report megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies. Its headline finding—six of ten messages nudge multiple attitudinal outcomes, but only by 1–4 percentage points, with essentially no partisan heterogeneity and *no* effect on costly behaviour (donations)—is a sobering empirical anchor for the whole topic. It directly undercuts widespread assumptions in the messaging literature (audience-targeted "Republican-friendly" framings, moral reframing) and demonstrates the attitude–behaviour gap that Spampatti's call for high-impact outcome measures anticipates.

[[Lieu2025-nl]] complements this by disentangling *content* from *form*: crossing CARDS contrarian-claim categories with FLICC rhetorical fallacies, it finds that logical fallacy type has essentially no effect on perceived veracity or belief, while content category matters a great deal—misinformation attacking climate solutions is rated most credible and, along with attacks on scientists, is the most polarising for conservatives. Together with Voelkel's null partisan-heterogeneity result, this begins to sketch a picture in which *what* is claimed (solution-denial, consensus messaging) outperforms *how* it is rhetorically packaged, at least for short-form exposure.

[[Xue2025-bp]] pushes on the form side from the opposite direction, showing that fact-checking content itself—nominally the antidote to misinformation—is often saturated with emotional language, and that this emotionality shapes audience engagement and sentiment toward fact-checked targets. This complicates any simple "facts vs. feelings" dichotomy and dovetails with Spampatti's insistence that emotional and social drivers, not just cognitive corrections, need to be built into message design and evaluation.

## From One-Shot Messages to Inoculation and Dialogue

A second arc in this topic moves from static exposure to *interactive and pre-emptive* interventions. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that classic inoculation theory can be operationalised cheaply at scale: a 19-second prebunking ad shown to over 375,000 Instagram users produced a 21-point improvement in recognising emotional manipulation, an effect still detectable five months later. This is arguably the strongest behavioural/ecological-validity result in the set, directly answering Spampatti's call for real-world, scalable outcome measures.

[[Szabo2026-rd]] extends inoculation from broadcast video to dialogue, introducing "Conversational Inoculation" via an LLM chatbot (MindFort/"Forty"). Its finding that chatbot-based inoculation outperforms traditional reading/writing refutation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled for suggests that the *adaptivity* of conversational agents—not just their content—is doing causal work, echoing the interactional themes (trust-building, minimised friction) that recur across the chatbot papers.

That adaptivity is tested at much larger scale and across topics in [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]]. Costello, Pennycook, and Rand show that personalised, evidence-based AI dialogues durably (≥2 months) reduce belief in specific conspiracy theories by ~20%, generalising to unrelated conspiracies—directly challenging motivational accounts of conspiracy belief as impervious to counterevidence. Kotz2026-lk generalises this logic beyond conspiracy theories to structurally different contested issues (climate, vaccination, inequality), finding that brief GPT-5 dialogues shift both beliefs and policy support, with the largest gains among initial skeptics and those high in trust in science. Read together, these two papers make the strongest case in the collection that the failure of prior debunking is a failure of *depth and personalisation*, not an inherent resistance of belief to evidence—an argument that sits somewhat in tension with Voelkel's demonstration that even carefully revised static messages produce only small effects, suggesting dialogic, adaptive engagement may be doing something qualitatively different from message exposure alone.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] adds a necessary complication to this optimism about chatbots: acceptance, not just efficacy, is a gating condition. Testing "balanced" climate chatbots against users varying in conspiracy belief, it finds—counter to expectations from the conspiracy-belief literature—that higher-conspiracy users trust and rate the chatbot *more* positively, with perceived usefulness and trust (not ease of use) driving intention to use. This reframes chatbot deployment as much as a trust-engineering problem as an argument-quality problem, a theme implicit in Szabo's identification of "interactional friction" and "trust-building through partnership" as mechanisms.

## Audience-Side Vulnerability and Supporting Methodology

Two papers address how prior exposure and message *modality* shape susceptibility rather than testing an intervention per se. [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies a "modality-congruent carryover effect": difficulty authenticating an initial (deep)fake shapes confidence and susceptibility to *later* fake content, but only within the same modality (text-to-text, video-to-video). This is a cautionary finding for inoculation designers—prebunking effects, and vulnerabilities, may not transfer across format as readily as content-based theories assume, a nuance the Instagram and chatbot studies do not yet test directly.

Finally, [[Arminio2025-tw]] sits somewhat apart methodologically, but is directly relevant infrastructure for this topic: it demonstrates that vision-language models can cluster climate-related social media images by *connotative* meaning (e.g., grouping wind turbines and solar panels as "renewable energy" imagery) far better than CNN-based pipelines, with high interpretability via keyword summaries. For a research programme increasingly interested in visual and multimodal misinformation (fake images, memes, deepfakes as in Choi), this offers a scalable way to characterise the *content* of climate-related visual messaging at the scale Spampatti argues is necessary for systems-level, cross-contextual analysis.

## Synthesis

Across these eleven papers, a clear trajectory emerges: static, one-shot message framing produces reliable but small and behaviourally shallow effects ([[Voelkel2026-lc]], [[Lieu2025-nl]]); scaling and personalising the *delivery* of corrective content—via inoculation ads, conversational agents, or adaptive dialogue—yields larger and more durable shifts, especially among the skeptics traditional messaging fails to reach ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], [[Szabo2026-rd]], [[Costello2024-bg]], [[Kotz2026-lk]]). Trust, modality-specific cognitive carryover, and emotional framing of both misinformation and its correction ([[Dubey2026-bl]], [[Choi2026-bz]], [[Xue2025-bp]]) emerge as the underappreciated moderators that determine whether any given message or dialogue lands. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] frames why this matters at the field level, and [[Arminio2025-tw]] offers a methodological lever for studying the content side of this ecosystem at scale—together suggesting that the next generation of interventions must integrate adaptive, trust-sensitive delivery with systems-level measurement of real-world impact.
