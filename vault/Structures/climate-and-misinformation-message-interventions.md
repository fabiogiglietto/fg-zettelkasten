# Message-Based Interventions for Climate Communication and Misinformation Resistance

## The arc: from static messages to adaptive dialogue

The papers filed here trace a shift in how the field imagines intervention — from broadcasting a fixed, well-crafted message to an audience, toward calibrating a conversation to an individual. At one end sits the classical paradigm of message testing: pick a framing, present it, measure attitude change. [[Voelkel2026-lc]] is the most rigorous recent test of this paradigm at scale, and its results are sobering: across the ten most-cited climate messaging strategies, effects on attitudes are real but small (1–4 percentage points), essentially non-partisan, and crucially fail to move costly behaviour like donations. [[Lieu2025-nl]] complements this by showing that even the *content* of misinformation, not just of corrective messaging, matters more than its logical form — fallacy type barely affects perceived veracity, but which contrarian claim is used (attacks on solutions vs. attacks on the science) strongly shapes both credibility and partisan polarization. Together these two studies suggest that the "one clever sentence" model of persuasion — whether pro- or anti-climate — has hit a ceiling: framing effects are message- and content-specific, small, and hard to generalize across audiences or outcomes.

Against that backdrop, the collection pivots toward interventions that adapt to the recipient. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] shows that inoculation delivered as a short, non-adaptive video can nonetheless scale cheaply on real platforms (Instagram) and produce durable, meaningful gains in spotting emotional manipulation — evidence that even "static" prebunking can work in the wild if narrowly targeted at a specific manipulative technique rather than diffuse persuasion. But the papers on conversational AI push further, arguing that dynamic, personalized dialogue outperforms one-shot messaging precisely because it can respond to the specific evidence and reasoning a person offers. [[Costello2024-bg]] is the pivotal demonstration: GPT-4 dialogues tailored to an individual's stated conspiracy belief produce large, durable (2-month), and generalizing reductions in conspiracy endorsement, directly challenging motivational/identity-protection accounts of belief entrenchment in favor of an analytic-reasoning account — beliefs move when the counterevidence is good enough and personalized enough. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this logic across contested domains (climate, vaccines, inequality), showing AI dialogue's persuasive power is domain-general, hits skeptics hardest, and is moderated by trust in science rather than by ideology — a finding that echoes Voelkel's null on partisan heterogeneity but locates it in a much more powerful delivery mechanism. [[Lin2025-xp]] generalizes the AI-dialogue finding to real electoral persuasion across four contests, and importantly diagnoses *why* it works: not through manipulative psychological tactics but through the citation of relevant facts and evidence, i.e., a central-route persuasion process — though it also flags a normatively troubling asymmetry, with right-leaning-advocating AI models being systematically less accurate.

## Chatbots as a design space: trust, adaptivity, and friction

Two papers narrow in on chatbot design specifically. [[Szabo2026-rd]] operationalizes "conversational inoculation" as an HCI problem, showing that a chatbot condition beats traditional reading/writing inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled, and — importantly — attributes this advantage not simply to better content but to interactional features: adaptability, scaffolded independent thinking, trust-building, and minimized friction. This reframes inoculation efficacy as partly a matter of interface and relationship, not just argument quality, and it dovetails with [[Dubey2026-bl]]'s finding that trust and perceived usefulness (not ease of use) are the dominant predictors of acceptance for a "balanced news chatbot" — with the counterintuitive twist that higher-conspiracy-belief users trust and adopt the chatbot *more* than low-belief users. Read alongside Costello's and Kotz's findings that skeptics and conspiracy believers are the most, not least, movable by well-designed AI dialogue, Dubey's result suggests a coherent counter-narrative to the standard assumption that conspiracy-minded or distrustful audiences will reflexively reject counter-attitudinal digital tools; the harder problem may instead be mainstream users' selective engagement, and interface trust cues rather than ideological match.

## Cognitive vulnerability and the limits of the individual-level lens

[[Choi2026-bz]] complicates this generally optimistic arc by examining a vulnerability that any message-based or conversational intervention must contend with: repeated struggles authenticating fake content produce carryover effects on confidence and susceptibility, modality-specific (text-to-text, video-to-video), suggesting that platforms mixing deepfake and disinformation formats create durable, format-bound erosions in discernment that a single inoculation or dialogue session may not fully repair.

Finally, [[Spampatti2026-kx]] functions as the meta-commentary tying the set together, explicitly arguing that misinformation research (of exactly the kind represented by the other nine papers) should borrow from climate psychology's more mature engagement with organized opposition, systems-level thinking, and real-world behavioural outcomes rather than cognitive proxies. Its critique — that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world behaviour — lands as a direct challenge to several papers in this very cluster: Voelkel's megastudy answers it by explicitly testing behaviour (donations) and finding an attitude-behaviour gap; van der Linden's field study answers it with genuine platform deployment and click-through metrics; but the chatbot/dialogue studies (Costello, Kotz, Lin, Szabo, Dubey), for all their sophistication in personalization, largely still rely on self-reported belief and intention measures. The structure note's throughline, then, is a field moving convincingly from generic framing toward adaptive, trust-sensitive, evidence-based dialogue as the more powerful lever — while still confronting Spampatti's challenge that psychological effect sizes on attitudes, however impressive, have yet to be reliably shown to translate into the collective, costly, real-world action that climate and misinformation crises actually require.
