# Italian Election Media Mapping (MINE)

## The MINE Programme: Mapping Italian Elections Across Platforms and Cycles

The papers filed here constitute the empirical spine of the MINE research programme: a sustained, cumulative effort to map how Italian electoral politics unfolds across news media and social platforms, from the 2013 general election through 2018, 2019, and 2022, and now into the DSA-regulated present. Read together, they trace an arc from descriptive audience/engagement studies, through methodological innovation in detecting coordinated manipulation, to a reflexive confrontation with the platform infrastructures and regulatory regimes that make such research possible — or impossible — in the first place.

### Origins: hybrid media and partisan insularity

The earliest work, [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], predates the MINE label but establishes its foundational sensibility: Italian political communication must be studied as a *hybrid* system where television and social media co-constitute each other, and where apparent participatory abundance (millions of tweets) conceals narrow, TV-driven engagement. This hybrid-systems lens reappears, transformed, in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], which shifts the unit of analysis from talk-show second-screening to the 2018 election's news ecology proper. Here the programme's signature move crystallizes: combining Twitter-derived partisan attention scores with Facebook engagement metrics to show that populist-aligned news sources (M5S above all) are more "insular," and that insularity predicts a very particular attention economy — amplification via shares among the like-minded versus contestation via comments across partisan lines. This paper effectively sets the empirical template MINE will return to across cycles: partisan structure of news sharing, platform-specific interaction signatures, and the Five Star Movement as a recurring case of a digitally-native "cyber party."

### The coordination-detection turn

The programme's methodological center of gravity shifts decisively with [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], which reframes disinformation research away from content-veracity or actor-blacklisting toward *coordinated collective behavior* — networks of pages, groups, and profiles sharing URLs in suspicious lockstep. Applied across both 2018 and 2019 electoral cycles, this establishes "coordinated link sharing behavior" as a durable analytic object, one shown to correlate strongly with the propagation of problematic domains. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends this logic into the 2022 snap election, solving the static-list problem by building a continuously updating workflow that iteratively surfaces new coordinated actors — revealing not just ideologically motivated hyperpartisan networks (an M5S-aligned operation reaching over 1.5 million users) but also click-economy and religious-proselytism operations that piggyback on electoral attention. Together these two papers chart the programme's maturation from a one-off detection exercise into an infrastructural, near-real-time monitoring capacity.

### Data artifacts and the fragility of platform-provided evidence

A parallel and increasingly central strand interrogates the very data substrate on which this empirical mapping depends. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] is the pivotal cautionary paper: it demonstrates that Meta's 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in the URL Shares Dataset produces near-identical trajectories across 46 unrelated countries, exposing a platform-wide algorithmic artifact (likely Feed-ranking changes) that can masquerade as substantive cross-national or longitudinal findings. This finding reverberates through [[McNally2025-dn]], which — though centered on a UK outlet rather than Italian data — belongs to the same epistemic project of treating Facebook's News Feed not as an inscrutable black box but as a system whose interventions leave detectable, laggedstatistical traces. Both papers argue that engagement data must be read *through* the algorithm, not as a transparent window onto audience behavior, and both explicitly invoke the DSA as a potential remedy for the resulting evidentiary fragility. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] pushes this line furthest, using the Meta Content Library (a DSA-mandated resource) to show that Meta's political-content-reduction policy silently suppressed Italian parliamentarians' reach months before its announced rollout, while extremist accounts adapted by posting more — a finding that is simultaneously a substantive election-communication result and a case study in the transparency deficits that motivate the programme's newest methodological register.

### Computational scaling: from CrowdTangle to LLMs

A third strand tackles the practical challenge of processing the enormous corpora that cross-cycle comparison demands. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] jointly document the programme's turn toward LLM-based pipelines for clustering and labeling Italian political news shared on Facebook in 2018 and 2022, benchmarking embedding models and articulating validation protocols that acknowledge LLMs' novel epistemic status (neither classical classifiers nor human coders). These are explicitly infrastructural papers — they exist to make the substantive election-mapping work in the other strands feasible at scale, and their comparative 2018-vs-2022 framing keeps them tethered to the programme's core longitudinal design even as they read as methods contributions.

### Regulatory horizon and rhetorical framing

The two most recent additions gesture toward where the programme is heading. [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys the DSA's reshaping of data access itself, effectively theorizing the institutional condition of possibility for everything from [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] to future MINE work — a meta-commentary on the programme's own evidentiary infrastructure. [[Mosca2026-yh]], meanwhile, extends the substantive object of study from coordinated *sharing* to coordinated *accusation*, proposing that "fake news" charges themselves function as strategic rhetorical weapons subject to selective media amplification — a natural complement to the insularity/partisanship findings of [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] and a sign that the programme's next cycle may fold delegitimization discourse into its account of hyperpartisan networks.

### The arc as a whole

Across these eleven papers, MINE traces a coherent trajectory: from documenting *how* Italians engage with election news across hybrid TV/Twitter/Facebook environments (2013–2018), to *detecting* the coordinated and often deceptive networks that shape that engagement (2018–2022), to *interrogating* the platform-provided data and infrastructures that make any of this visible at all (2020–2026), while continuously upgrading the computational toolkit needed to do so at scale. The throughline is a recurring case — the Five Star Movement as digitally-native populist innovator — and a recurring epistemic anxiety: that engagement metrics, coordination signals, and even "fake news" itself are never simply given, but are artifacts of platform architectures, regulatory regimes, and rhetorical strategies that the researcher must render legible before they can be trusted as evidence of anything electoral at all.
