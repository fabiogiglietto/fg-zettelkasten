# LLMs & Computational Content Analysis

## A field moving faster than its own validation standards

The most visible throughline across this literature is reflexive anxiety about its own velocity. [[Balluff2026-if]] opens as a programmatic counterweight, cataloguing the epistemic, environmental, and infrastructural costs of treating LLMs as a default tool for text analysis, synthetic data, and experimentation, and arguing for a "least-resource-intensive-method-that-works" ethic. [[Tan2024-vl]] maps the same terrain more neutrally, synthesizing the fast-growing literature on LLM-based annotation and synthesis into generation, assessment, and utilization stages — a structure that the empirical papers below largely instantiate piece by piece. Between these two poles sits the bulk of the corpus: papers that neither reject nor naively embrace LLMs, but try to specify exactly what validation should look like for a tool that is simultaneously a classifier, an annotator, and a judge.

## From single prompts to validated, multi-agent pipelines

A cluster of papers treats "LLMs-in-the-loop" as a pipeline-engineering problem rather than a one-shot classification task. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] is foundational here: it articulates why fully LLM-integrated pipelines (fine-tuned classification → embedding-based clustering → LLM-generated cluster labels) demand bespoke, stage-specific validation protocols, and why crowdsourced human benchmarks are inadequate once the LLM itself out-performs low-skilled annotators. [[Farjam2026-zg]] formalizes this further into an explicit Instructor–Classifier–Judge–Human-Intermediary architecture, showing that adding a Judge agent raises Krippendorff's alpha above conventional reliability thresholds and that the resulting pipeline transfers across languages without translation. [[Meher2025-qb]] pushes adaptation downward into the model itself, showing that QLoRA fine-tuning of an open-weight Llama model yields dramatic gains for rare event categories in conflict classification, while remaining robust to prompt rewording — evidence that reliability gains need not depend on proprietary frontier models. At a meta level, [[Alizadeh2026-es]] interrogates whether these pipelines are even verifiable: using coding agents to reproduce published social-science findings, it finds high reproduction accuracy but also demonstrates that confirmatory prompting induces "sycophantic" specification search, a direct echo of the validation anxieties raised by Marino and Balluff.

A second strand within this cluster reframes the LLM not as an automated coder but as an *interlocutor*. [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] both resist a replacement logic, using LLMs to surface ambiguous or borderline cases in human codebooks (competency-based-education interviews and Semetko-and-Valkenburg frame codes, respectively) and treating divergences between LLM and human coding as occasions for theoretical refinement rather than error to be minimized. This "LLM-as-collaborator" move sits in productive tension with the more automation-oriented pipelines above: both traditions converge on the conclusion that human interpretive authority must be explicitly retained, but they locate that authority at different pipeline stages.

## Benchmarking the engines: embeddings, classifiers, and their failure modes

A parallel set of papers interrogates the components that feed these pipelines. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] directly compares OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against the Italian-specific UmBERTo for clustering political news, finding the proprietary embedding consistently superior — a finding that motivates the embedding choice later operationalized in [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]. [[Fan2025-ut]] complicates any simple embedding-quality story by showing that pretrained embeddings encode spurious source and language confounders, and that linear concept erasure (LEACE) can recover large gains in clustering/retrieval without degrading unrelated benchmarks — a technical corrective directly relevant to any cross-source or multilingual clustering pipeline in this set. [[Brown2025-jk]] turns the fairness question around: rather than asking whether LLMs match a "ground truth," it shows that demographic disagreement between LLM and human annotators is small and dataset-specific, while item difficulty (human label entropy) is by far the dominant predictor of LLM-human disagreement — reframing annotation bias debates around task ambiguity rather than model choice. Two papers locate harder ceilings on reliability: [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows that even reasoning- and search-augmented LLMs fail at political fact-checking without curated retrieval, with web search alone providing only moderate gains and search-enabled citation patterns skewing toward left-leaning reliable sources; [[Paci2025-ag]] shows that LLMs applied to Italian political speech struggle specifically with pragmatic phenomena (implicature, presupposition), falling well below expert human performance even with chain-of-thought prompting. Together these papers suggest that classification accuracy, fairness, and pragmatic competence are separable validity dimensions that a single F1 score cannot capture.

## LLMs as scaling instruments for measurement

A distinct use case treats LLMs not as classifiers of discrete categories but as general-purpose measurement instruments for latent constructs. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows that simply asking an instruction-tuned LLM to place a text on an ideological dimension, and averaging many such responses, reproduces established scaling benchmarks across languages and text types — a striking generalization of "prompt-and-average" as a substitute for Wordscores/Wordfish-style estimation. [[Lee2026-je]] extends inference to latent, undeclared traits: LLMs infer users' political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse, exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary, outperforming supervised baselines and raising the stakes around privacy and micro-targeting that [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] later addresses from the persuasion side. [[Waight2025-al]] and [[Elfes2026-jb]] push measurement toward narrative structure itself: Waight proposes "narrative similarity" as an estimand distinct from lexical, topical, or semantic similarity, building an LLM claim-distillation pipeline that dramatically outperforms text-reuse and topic-modeling baselines in detecting cross-outlet narrative diffusion; Elfes operationalizes Greimas's Actantial Model via an LLM to measure "narrative polarization," finding that video content is sharply polarized while comments surprisingly converge on surface narratives even as deeper motifs remain partisan. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] generalize this anti-reductionist impulse to framing analysis more broadly, arguing that fixed, predefined frame sets (the norm in automated content analysis) miss emergent and multimodal framing dynamics that unsupervised, LLM-augmented pipelines can surface instead.

## Multimodal extensions

Several papers carry the same validation logic into visual and multimodal content, arguing that platforms like Instagram render text-only pipelines structurally inadequate. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] benchmarks GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision pipelines (RetinaFace, FaceNet512, Google Cloud Vision) for face recognition and person counting in campaign imagery, finding the multimodal LLM substantially more accurate and flagging a possible gender bias in the specialized models that GPT-4o avoids. [[Arminio2025-tw]] shows that routing images through a VLLM's connotative textual description, rather than a CNN's denotative features, yields more socially meaningful (and more interpretable) image clusters, at a modest cost to denotative coherence — reproducing, in the visual domain, the same denotative/connotative distinction that motivates narrative-level over lexical-level text measures elsewhere in this set. [[Gardam2025-er]] makes the normative case explicit, arguing for a "visual-first" methodology for climate-denial discourse on image-centric platforms.

## Substantive payoff: pipelines put to work

A set of applied studies demonstrates what validated pipelines can deliver once built. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to classify collective-action frames in Proud Boys Telegram messages at scale, then uses Granger causality to link online framing cycles to offline violence — a case where supervised fine-tuning (rather than prompting) remains the tool of choice. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Marino2026-he]] both deploy GPT-4/GPT-4o zero-shot classification longitudinally — on a decade of Norwegian Progress Party Facebook sentiment and on three years of pro-Bolsonaro Brazilian Facebook networks, respectively — to show that hyperpartisan online engagement is far less stable than echo-chamber assumptions predict, and that negative or high-intensity content drives shares/comments differently than likes. Read alongside [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], these studies trace a visible arc from methods paper to validated pipeline to substantive finding within the same broader research program.

## Convergent strand: LLMs as persuasive and conversational agents

A neighboring cluster studies LLMs not as analysts of text but as generators of persuasive content, and is methodologically entangled with the content-analysis strand because it depends on similarly validated classifiers to code outcomes and detect accuracy trade-offs. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically decomposes what makes conversational AI persuasive (post-training and information density dominate over scale or personalization) while documenting a persuasion–accuracy trade-off that echoes [[DeVerna2025-dl]]'s finding that fact-checking quality depends on curated context rather than capability alone. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] both show that disclosure — of perceived bias or of persuasive intent — can sharply attenuate LLM persuasiveness, pointing toward transparency as a lever distinct from accuracy. [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] extend conversational LLMs into applied interventions (misinformation inoculation; belief change among skeptics on climate, vaccines, and inequality), while [[Brady2026-ln]] and [[Allen2024-av]] use LLM-based content classification as an instrumental step within larger causal designs — respectively, an algorithmic field experiment on Bluesky and a large-scale estimate of vaccine-misinformation impact on Facebook — again relying on validated classifiers (e.g., BERT-based hesitancy scoring) as the measurement backbone. [[Allen2025-ot]] situates this whole experimental turn within a broader methodological conversation about platform access, arguing that LLM-based, platform-independent tools are becoming necessary substitutes for shrinking API access.

## The sociotechnical surround: provenance, bias, and institutional risk

A final set of papers steps back to ask what gets baked into the models and pipelines themselves. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows that state-coordinated media is memorized by commercial LLMs and shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence in affected languages — a finding with direct implications for any content-analysis pipeline that treats LLM judgments as neutral. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] turns the analytic lens on the discourse about LLMs themselves, finding that mentalistic-agentic media framings of AI are present but contested rather than dominant. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a cautionary case study of AI-generated content at scale (Grokipedia), showing derivative, less-reliable sourcing concentrated in politically controversial articles — relevant to anyone using LLM-generated text as either corpus or ground truth. [[Minici2024-tf]] extends the foundation-model paradigm to coordinated inauthentic behavior detection via graph-LLM hybrids, while [[Miller2026-cp]] documents a live, large-scale deployment of AI-assisted content evaluation (X's Community Notes AI Note Writer) that preserves human-rater authority over AI output — a design philosophy that rhymes with the human-in-the-loop commitments of [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]]. [[Altoe2026-wu]] closes the loop between detection and triage, building an end-to-end streaming pipeline (LLM-based claim normalization, anomaly detection, virality prediction) explicitly designed around fact-checkers' actual workflow constraints. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] synthesizes much of this agenda at the level of democratic theory, arguing that LLM-enabled bridging, moderation, and deliberation systems could reshape digital public squares for better or worse — a fitting bookend to [[Balluff2026-if]]'s opening caution: the same tools that promise scalable, validated content analysis also carry unresolved risks of bias, concentration, and institutional capture that this literature is only beginning to measure.
