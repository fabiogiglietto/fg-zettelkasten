# LLMs in Computational Content Analysis

## From manual coding to LLM-in-the-loop pipelines

The founding methodological problem this literature confronts is straightforward but consequential: once large language models can plausibly perform tasks that used to require trained human coders — classification, clustering, labeling, scaling — what does rigorous validation even mean? [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] frames this most explicitly, describing a three-stage "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline (fine-tuned political classifier, embedding-based clustering, LLM-generated cluster labels) for Italian election Facebook data, and arguing that fully LLM-integrated pipelines need *novel*, task-specific validation protocols rather than a single overarching human-coder benchmark — partly because LLMs now outperform low-skilled crowdworkers, making services like MTurk or Fiverr inadequate as a validation baseline. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the same terrain at a more general level, organizing the field around generation, assessment, and downstream use of LLM annotations, and explicitly naming quality assurance and ethics as the open problems the rest of this corpus tries to solve empirically. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] supplies one empirical brick in that foundation, showing that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large consistently yields more semantically coherent clusters of Italian political news than the language-specific UmBERTo model — a finding [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] draws on directly for its own embedding stage — while also warning that clustering-quality metrics (especially HDBSCAN's) can be gamed by labeling large swaths of data as noise, a caution about metric validity that recurs throughout the topic.

## Task-specific validation across modalities

Several papers extend this validation logic to particular tasks and data types, each converging on the same moral: LLMs are powerful general-purpose tools, but their outputs must be checked against task-appropriate ground truth rather than assumed reliable by fiat. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] benchmarks GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person-counting in Instagram campaign imagery, finding the multimodal LLM outperforms bespoke models — evidence that prompting can lower technical barriers to visual political communication research while still requiring systematic accuracy checks (including checks for gendered bias in recognition rates). [[Meher2025-qb]]'s ConflLlama shows the same logic for structured event classification, fine-tuning Llama-3.1 via QLoRA on terrorism-event data and validating gains against a base zero-shot model, with attention to prompt robustness as an indicator that the model has learned real category structure rather than surface cues. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] validates a lighter-weight "ask-and-average" approach to positioning political texts on ideological scales, correlating LLM-derived estimates against established scaling benchmarks across languages and text types. [[Ober2026-vd]] moves into qualitative research, integrating topic modeling, LLM-assisted labeling, and human codebook refinement for interview transcripts, treating topic modeling's mathematical transparency as a validity anchor for the more opaque LLM labeling step. [[Waight2025-al]] pushes hardest on estimand clarity, arguing that "narrative similarity" is conflated in prior text-as-data work with lexical, topical, or semantic similarity, and building an LLM claim-distillation pipeline that is validated against hand-coded gold standards and shown to outperform text-reuse, topic-modeling, and Relatio baselines — with real substantive stakes, since naive text-reuse measures would have entirely missed mainstream U.S. outlets echoing Russian state narratives about Ukrainian "biolabs."

## Where the pipeline strains: disagreement, bias, and pragmatic limits

A second cluster interrogates where and why LLM annotation breaks down, directly addressing the topic's concern with reliability and disagreement. [[Brown2025-jk]] offers a systematic answer: across four contentious annotation datasets, demographic bias in LLM-human agreement is small and dataset-specific rather than model-specific, while item difficulty (human label entropy) is by far the strongest predictor of disagreement — suggesting fairness audits should center task difficulty, not just model or prompt choice. [[Paci2025-ag]] finds a harder ceiling: even strong models like GPT-4o-mini fall well short of expert human interpretation of implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political speech, with Chain-of-Thought prompting helping only modestly — a reminder that some politically consequential tasks (detecting manipulative implicit content) may exceed current pragmatic competence regardless of prompting strategy. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows an analogous ceiling for fact-checking: standard and even reasoning-enabled LLMs perform poorly on PolitiFact claims from internal knowledge alone, web search helps only moderately (and search-enabled models exhibit a left-leaning citation skew), and only curated retrieval-augmented generation closes the gap — reinforcing that "bigger/newer model" is not itself a validation strategy. [[Fan2025-ut]] approaches disagreement from the representation side, showing that raw text embeddings encode confounds like source or language that bias downstream similarity and clustering, and proposing linear concept erasure as a low-cost corrective — a methodological complement to the clustering validation work in [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]].

## Applications: pipelines put to work in political discourse research

A substantial share of the corpus demonstrates these validated (or validating) pipelines directly on political discourse, showing the payoff of the methodological groundwork. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to classify collective-action frames across half a million Proud Boys Telegram messages, linking online framing to offline violence via Granger causality — an example of supervised fine-tuning as the workhorse classifier underlying a substantive communication argument. [[Larsson2026-ro]] uses GPT-4 zero-shot classification with human validation to code a decade of Norwegian Progress Party Facebook posts for sentiment, explicitly framing the paper as *both* a substantive contribution on negative campaigning and a methodological demonstration of LLM-assisted content analysis in a non-English language — a direct echo of [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]'s and [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]]'s Italian-language validation concerns. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes Greimas' Actantial Model via an LLM to measure "narrative polarisation" in YouTube discourse on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, reporting LLM-human inter-coder agreement comparable to human-human agreement as its validation benchmark. [[Lee2026-je]] and [[Yang2026-tq]] extend the human-in-the-loop logic to new use-cases: the former validates LLM inference of political alignment from nonpolitical text against supervised baselines (with confidence scores enabling principled aggregation), while the latter uses an LLM annotation pipeline itself as infrastructure for a systematic review, estimating the prevalence of social-media-based social science research across two decades.

## LLMs as interlocutors, not just coders

A related but distinct set of papers treats LLMs not as annotators of political text but as *actors* within political communication — chatbots and dialogue partners — raising parallel validation and reliability questions about efficacy, trust, and manipulation. [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Szabo2026-rd]] both show LLM dialogue can durably shift beliefs (reducing conspiracy belief; building misinformation resistance), with [[Costello2024-bg]] validating claim accuracy via fact-checker review (99.2% true) as a safeguard against the very reliability concerns raised elsewhere in the topic. [[Dubey2026-bl]] and [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] complicate any simple optimism: balanced-news chatbots are trusted more by high-conspiracy-belief users than low-belief users (an unexpected reversal of assumed defensiveness), while systematic experiments across 19 LLMs show that the same levers (post-training, information density) that maximize persuasiveness simultaneously degrade factual accuracy — a persuasion-accuracy trade-off that mirrors, at the level of generation rather than annotation, the reliability-versus-capability tensions documented in [[DeVerna2025-dl]] and [[Paci2025-ag]].

## Structural bias, critique, and the limits of neutral tooling

Finally, several papers step back to question whether "validation" of individual pipelines is sufficient given deeper structural biases in the models themselves. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows that state media control shapes LLM training data and output valence across languages, meaning a model's apparent neutrality on political content can itself be an artifact of asymmetric media environments baked into pretraining corpora — a finding with direct implications for any pipeline (e.g., [[DeVerna2025-dl]]'s fact-checking, [[Elfes2026-jb]]'s narrative coding) that treats LLM judgments as an unbiased arbiter. [[Balluff2026-if]] draws the corpus's most explicitly normative conclusion, arguing that rapid, unreflective LLM adoption in communication research ignores reproducibility risks (opaque corporate updates and guardrails), environmental costs, and the frequent adequacy of smaller open models — a direct counterpoint to papers in this topic that reach for frontier LLMs by default, and a call to treat model choice itself as a methodological decision requiring the same trade-off reasoning that [[Tan2024-vl]], [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], and [[Brown2025-jk]] apply to validation design. Read together, the corpus traces an arc from enthusiastic adoption, through increasingly rigorous task-specific validation (embeddings, classification, clustering, scaling, pragmatics), to a mature reflexivity about what LLMs can reliably measure, whose political realities they encode, and when a lighter-weight or human-only method remains the more defensible choice.
