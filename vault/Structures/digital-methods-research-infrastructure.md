# Digital Methods & Research Infrastructure

## Coordination detection: from CooRnet to a general science of coordinated behavior

The methodological core of this topic is the lineage of tools built to detect coordinated activity on social media, beginning from the CooRnet/CooRTweet family of approaches. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] belongs to this lineage indirectly but crucially: by dissecting how Meta's 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in the URL Shares Dataset introduces a platform-wide algorithmic artifact that can masquerade as substantive cross-country similarity, it is a foundational methodological warning about the data on which coordination-detection tools are built — a reminder that infrastructure choices upstream of any detection algorithm can produce spurious signal. [[Yang2025-iv]] pushes the coordination-detection method itself forward, showing that timing-based signals (the backbone of CooRnet-style approaches) are trivially gameable, and proposing statistical regularities in sharing speed and frequency as a more evasion-resistant alternative — a direct methodological successor to the original network-based coordination logic. [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] and [[Luceri2025-tr]] extend the same detection paradigm into new empirical and modal territories: authoritarian, multilingual protest contexts on Twitter, and TikTok's video-first ecosystem, respectively, both showing that the network-similarity approach travels but needs adaptation to context and content type. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] steps back from any single implementation to synthesize the entire field, proposing a theory-grounded, threat-agnostic definition of coordinated online behavior (actors, synergic actions, intent; authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, time-variance) that reconciles the fragmented, often platform-contingent operational definitions (including CooRnet-style ones) that have proliferated since Facebook coined "coordinated inauthentic behavior" in 2018. Read together, these five papers trace an arc from a single tool's data-quality caveat, through incremental methodological hardening against evasion, to cross-platform and cross-context generalization, and finally to a field-level conceptual consolidation — the trajectory one would expect of a maturing research infrastructure rather than a single technique.

## The post-API age: documenting collapse and its consequences

A second cluster documents, quantifies, and periodizes the erosion of the open-API research infrastructure that tools like CooRnet originally depended on. [[Freelon2024-sc]] supplies the master historical framework — five periods from API prehistory to the current fragmented regime of laissez-faire, academic, walled-garden, and pay-to-play access — against which nearly every other paper in this set implicitly positions itself. Bastos2025-ya and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] each retell this history from a Twitter/X-specific angle: the former as an elegiac retrospective on "Twitter studies" as a subfield made possible by REST/Streaming/Academic APIs, the latter as a three-phase "Wild West → Golden Age → Walled Garden" narrative that explicitly links Twitter/X's trajectory to the parallel closures at Meta, Reddit, and TikTok, and to the EU's attempt to convert access from platform philanthropy into regulatory right. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and [[Yang2026-tq]] supply the bibliometric evidence for what this closure actually cost empirical social science: tens of thousands of Twitter-dependent studies with a measurable output decline after 2023 pricing changes, and, at the discipline-wide level, a plateau and decline in social-media-based publishing after 2022 despite the field's earlier explosive growth — both papers converging on the same diagnosis of infrastructural precarity, though from bibliometric rather than qualitative angles. Together this cluster establishes the historical and quantitative baseline that motivates everything downstream: tool-building, API auditing, and regulatory intervention are all responses to the same documented collapse.

## Auditing the replacements: what platform APIs and tools actually deliver

If the previous cluster shows that access collapsed, this cluster interrogates what has (or has not) replaced it, subjecting official replacement mechanisms to the same critical scrutiny that digital methods scholars once reserved for scraping. [[Rieder2025-ju]] and [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] independently audit the two most consequential Research APIs offered in response to DSA obligations — YouTube's and TikTok's — and reach structurally similar conclusions: nominal compliance masks severe completeness, consistency, and representativeness problems (YouTube's search endpoint is "forgetful by design," with retrievability decaying within weeks of publication; TikTok's expanded API remains inconsistent and under-documented), such that neither tool can reliably support the systemic-risk research the DSA is meant to enable. [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] extend this critique to Meta's successive access regimes — CrowdTangle and its "clean room" successor, the Meta Content Library — arguing via the "seeing like an API" framework and detailed workflow analysis that technical design and governance rules jointly constrain not just data completeness but the very kinds of questions, methods (qualitative, mixed, cross-platform), and researchers (outside well-resourced, code-fluent, Global North teams) that platform-mediated infrastructure can accommodate. [[Zheng2026-bi]] offers a constructive counterpoint: rather than only auditing official APIs, it builds independent, unpermissioned random-sampling infrastructure (TubeStats, TokStats) to solve the "denominator problem" that opportunistic, engagement-skewed samples create, explicitly framing such tools as ethically defensible public-interest infrastructure in the post-API age. [[Schulte2026-df]] synthesizes the practical fallout of all this fragmentation at the level of a concrete empirical case — multi-platform electoral discourse analysis around the 2025 German election — arguing that the deeper problem is not a shortage of studies but the absence of reusable, standardized, cross-platform observability infrastructure, and proposing exactly the kind of methodological standardization this topic's broader research program aims toward.

## Regulatory infrastructure: the DSA as a new access regime

A distinct but tightly linked cluster examines the EU Digital Services Act, and specifically Article 40, as an attempted institutional fix for the post-API collapse — asking whether regulation can manufacture the infrastructure that platforms voluntarily withdrew. [[Ohme2026-nv]] frames Article 40 as a substantive practical change to researcher access conditions, while [[Pierri2025-hm]] and its close counterpart [[Pierri2026-ib]] (drawing on direct ERC-supported pilot experience with DG-CONNECT) catalog the concrete implementation bottlenecks — misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, a "circular problem" whereby data access is needed to win grants but funding is needed to pursue access — and flag large language models as a regulatory blind spot the DSA has yet to address. [[Peters2026-mo]] adds an underappreciated dimension by showing, through discourse analysis of EU consultation feedback, that "data quality" itself is a politically contested concept: officially near-absent from DSA documents, raised almost exclusively by academic and NGO stakeholders, and strategically inverted by platforms like Meta to argue that access is futile precisely because platform data is poor. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] situates itself as a state-of-the-field survey of the resulting API and tool landscape under this new regulatory regime, functioning as connective tissue between the auditing literature above and the regulatory literature here. Collectively these papers show the DSA less as a settled solution than as a fragile, contested negotiation — echoing [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]]'s and [[Bruns2026-pn]]'s shared verdict that mandated access does not automatically translate into usable access.

## Structural risks: industry capture and legal exposure

Underpinning both the API-collapse and DSA-negotiation narratives is a more fundamental worry about who controls, and who is permitted to produce, knowledge about platforms. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document this at the level of the published record itself: roughly half of high-profile social media research carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small set of repeatedly-engaged authors and editors, correlated with topical bias toward user-focused (rather than platform-scale) questions, and amplified by disproportionate citation and media attention — a structural argument for why independent infrastructure (CooRnet-style tools, independent APIs, DSA-mandated access) matters as a check on industry-shaped evidence bases. [[Park2026-tr]] supplies the complementary risk register from the researcher's own vantage point, documenting via interviews the chilling effects of overbroad computer-crime and IP law on exactly the kind of unpermissioned, scraping-adjacent, coordination-detection research this topic is built around, and showing that legal exposure — not just technical or regulatory access — is itself an infrastructural bottleneck that shapes what gets studied and published.

## Beyond platform APIs: recruitment, trace data, and new computational instruments

A final set of papers rounds out the toolkit beyond API access proper, addressing survey recruitment, trace-data measurement validity, and emergent computational instruments for social-scientific analysis. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] is the direct methodological ancestor of the "Facebook ad-based survey recruitment" strand named in this topic, developing and validating a Pixel-based ad procedure for recruiting hard-to-reach populations — efficient and low-cost, though its effectiveness at reaching ideologically distinctive respondents remains inconclusive, a caution echoed by later work on data-quality contestation. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] and [[Fan2026-af]] extend the infrastructure-critique logic to trace-data collection itself: the former shows that ex-situ web scraping (the default method when APIs are unavailable) introduces systematic, non-random content distortions that dwarf simple time-delay effects, while the latter proposes a framework for exploiting the fine-grained temporal structure of donated digital trace data across platforms, again converging on the theme that how data is captured — not just whether it is accessible — determines what can validly be known. [[Arminio2025-tw]], [[Fan2025-ut]], and [[Bruns2025-fz]] each contribute new computational instruments to the broader methodological toolkit — VLLM-based connotative image clustering, linear concept erasure for deconfounding text embeddings, and vector-embedding "practice mapping" for multimodal network interactions — extending the standard-setting ambition of this topic from data access into data analysis. [[Ferrara2026-io]]'s scalable self-supervised community-detection architecture sits at the far technical edge of this cluster, offering infrastructure for the kind of large-scale network analysis that coordination detection and platform-mapping research increasingly require. Two outliers bookend the topic's temporal and thematic range: [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] represents an early, prescient call for socially-informed internet architecture that anticipates today's arguments about platform-embedded power asymmetries, while [[Stanusch2026-ec]] extends digital-methods controversy mapping to the AI industry itself, showing that the same infrastructural and discursive struggles over transparency and accountability that animate the DSA literature are now replaying around generative AI governance — suggesting that the research infrastructure built for studying social media platforms will need to generalize to this adjacent frontier.
