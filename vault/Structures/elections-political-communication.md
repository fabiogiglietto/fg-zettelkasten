# Elections & Political Communication

## The Shifting Terrain of Election Research

Across this collection, a shared meta-concern runs beneath the substantive findings: **what can we still know about elections in the current platform environment, and through which infrastructures?** [[Lukito2026-nb]] maps the field's "price, proficiency, or permission" problem across 72 data-access tools, showing how platform-permission regimes create ephemeral, WEIRD-centric research infrastructures. [[Philipp2026-tl]] examines how the EU Digital Services Act is (partially) reopening data access, while [[Schulte2026-df]] treats multi-platform observability itself as an unresolved research-infrastructure problem, using the 2025 German election to show how X's API caps and TikTok's algorithmic filtering shape what can be studied. [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this to content-moderation auditing, calling for "observability" over transparency. These papers together frame everything else in the collection: the very possibility of empirical election research now depends on regulatory negotiation with platforms.

## Coordinated Behavior, Inauthenticity, and Reach

A tightly linked cluster reconceptualizes disinformation research away from content-veracity toward **coordinated collective action**. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] and [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] establish, through Italian election data, that partisan insularity and coordinated link-sharing behavior are more diagnostic than the falsity of individual claims. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] then operationalizes this into a continuously-updating workflow applied to the 2022 Italian election, while [[Oprea2025-lf]] adapts an "Authenticity Matrix" to detect hyperactive user amplification in Romania's 2024 EP campaign. The two versions of the FIES-linked deceptive networks study [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[noauthor_undated-bm]] complicate this picture at scale: 49 Meta-identified deceptive networks reached 37 million US users in 2020, but reach was massively concentrated (three networks drove 70%+), largely mediated by resharing by ordinary users, and — critically — bivariate associations with political outcomes evaporated after controlling for pre-exposure characteristics. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] similarly finds misinformation's diffusion patterns distinctive but concentrated. Together these papers push toward a more cautious causal register even as they refine detection methods.

## Elite Strategies and Platform-Specific Adaptation

If deceptive networks account for less than 1% of political content views, the collection insists that **elite actors and party strategies deserve more attention than shadowy foreign operations**. [[Tornberg2025-ir]], across 26 countries, finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation-sharing by parliamentarians — only their *interaction*, radical-right populism, does. [[Tai2026-qk]] similarly locates institutional and ideological constraints on elite misinformation-sharing in the US, and [[Knupfer2025-vt]] theorizes a "logic of connective faction" whereby ideologically extreme Republican legislators bind themselves to hyper-partisan digital ecosystems around triggers like CRT. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians pursuing three distinct modes of epistemic authority during the Manaus crisis — derivative reporting, direct state authority claims, and local brokerage. Platform-specific strategy work appears prominently: [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] documents how the Sweden Democrats reserved memetic, affectively-charged securitizing content for TikTok while treating Facebook/X as sites for text-heavy legitimation; [[Larsson2026-ro]] finds Norway's Progress Party has drifted toward increasingly negative Facebook content under Listhaug's leadership; [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] shows German candidates and parties adopting complementary visual strategies on Instagram stories versus posts.

## The Media Environment: Advertising, Visibility, and Amplification

Several papers turn to the **structural bias of visibility itself**. [[Iris2026-pg]] finds systematic overrepresentation of Radical Right actors across five countries' online news coverage of the 2024 EP elections, independent of outlet orientation. [[Balluff2026-bv]] demonstrates via difference-in-differences that Austria's Kurz-OE24 "Inseratenaffäre" produced not obviously more favorable coverage of Kurz but more negative coverage of his competitors — a "pay positive to go negative" pattern. On advertising specifically, [[Kim2026-wg]] provides the collection's strongest causal claim about digital campaigning: 2016 Russian voter suppression ads showed geo-racial targeting and depressed turnout by up to 17% in targeted subpopulations, findings visible only through heterogeneous-effect designs. This contrasts pointedly with [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s null findings from a 2020 randomized ad-removal experiment on Facebook/Instagram (though underpowered for turnout), and connects to [[Votta2025-xz]] on how delivery algorithms shape reach independently of advertiser choice, [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] on volunteer-based ad auditing in Brazil 2018, and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] on Meta's undisclosed 2021-25 political content reduction that cut Italian MPs' reach by 72% while extremists compensated through posting volume. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] adds a fourth category of communicator — social bots as agenda-builders — to the classical organization/press/public trio.

## Audience-Side Dynamics: Emotion, Perception, and Persuasion

A further cluster examines **how citizens receive, feel, and are shaped by** electoral communication. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] finds across three Scandinavian elections that "Angry" Facebook Reactions predict shares while "Love" predicts likes, with populist parties disproportionately drawing anger; [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review generalizes this pattern across 64 studies. [[Rossini2026-jn]]'s three-wave panel in Brazil links messaging-app news use to political intolerance *via* misinformation beliefs, positing misinformation as an amplifier rather than origin of intolerance. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] documents a "hostile misinformation effect" across Germany, Netherlands, and Poland: citizens systematically believe their in-group is targeted more by falsehoods, especially on the right, with downstream damage to media trust and electoral integrity beliefs. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] situates such perceptions in the electoral context of far-right party performance. On the sensemaking side, [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] show how election-integrity rumors emerge less from false facts than from evidence-frame interactions and collaborative "deep story" performance, with 2022 audiences interpreting ambiguous events through 2020's fraud narrative. Finally, [[Lin2025-xp]] delivers a striking experimental result: brief LLM dialogues produce persuasion effects exceeding traditional ads across four elections, driven by facts and evidence rather than manipulation — though right-leaning AI advocacy was systematically less accurate.

## Cross-Cutting Threads

Three arcs traverse the collection. First, **a shift from actor-focused to behavior-focused detection** ([[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], [[Oprea2025-lf]]) paired with a shift from content-focused to context-focused analysis of misinformation ([[Starbird2025-jj]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]]). Second, **a growing skepticism of average treatment effects and simple correlations** as ways of characterizing platform influence, evident in the exposure-vs-effect gap in [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[noauthor_undated-bm]], the heterogeneous-effect turn in [[Kim2026-wg]], and the null findings of [[Allcott2025-jb]] — all suggesting that platform effects are real but narrow, concentrated, and often invisible to aggregate designs. Third, **the radical-right populism nexus** as an integrative object: whether via misinformation-sharing ([[Tornberg2025-ir]]), factional radicalization ([[Knupfer2025-vt]]), visual securitization ([[Bouchafra2026-ts]]), disproportionate media visibility ([[Iris2026-pg]]), hostile misinformation perception ([[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]), engagement asymmetries ([[Kalsnes2025-zb]], [[Larsson2026-ro]]), or algorithmic resilience through posting volume ([[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]), the radical right emerges as the political family most tightly co-evolved with contemporary platform dynamics — a finding whose robustness across contexts is arguably the collection's central substantive contribution.
