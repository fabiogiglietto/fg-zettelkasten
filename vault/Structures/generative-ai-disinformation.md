# Generative AI & Disinformation

## An emerging research infrastructure

The papers gathered here trace the contours of a fast-consolidating subfield — one that the new 'IA Generativa e Media' course now formalizes into a teaching arc. Read together, they move from micro-level questions ("does this fake image feel more credible than fake text?") to macro-level ones ("can a swarm of AI agents corrode the shared reality on which democratic deliberation depends?"), and from empirical measurement to epistemological reckoning with what a 'fact' even means once generative systems are in the loop.

## The mechanics of AI persuasion

A cluster of large-N experimental papers establishes, with unusual rigor, *how* conversational AI persuades and how powerful that persuasion is. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] dissects the levers — post-training, prompting, information density — showing that persuasiveness and factual accuracy trade off against each other, a finding sharpened by [[Hackenburg2026-ud]], which demonstrates that frontier systems now out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the same "information throughput" mechanism doing the causal work. [[Lin2025-xp]] extends this into live electoral contexts across four countries, confirming that AI persuades chiefly through facts and evidence rather than manipulation — while also documenting a troubling partisan asymmetry, with AIs advocating right-leaning candidates producing systematically less accurate claims. Together these three papers converge on a counter-intuitive policy implication that [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] then operationalizes: since persuasion rides on volume of claims rather than on deception per se, disclosing that a system *is* AI does almost nothing to blunt its effect, but disclosing *what it is trying to do* — its persuasive intent — roughly halves it. This quartet effectively reframes AI-driven persuasion less as a hallucination problem and more as a throughput and transparency-design problem, a claim that reverberates through the more disinformation-specific papers below.

## Visual and multimodal deception

A second strand asks whether these persuasive dynamics change when the medium shifts from text to image or video. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] delivers a bracing corrective to assumptions of visual supremacy: AI-generated stills and decontextualized video only outperform textual disinformation when an issue is highly salient and ample authentic footage exists to lend plausibility (the Ukraine war, but not MH370) — suggesting that low-tech recontextualization, not novel synthetic generation, may remain the more potent threat in many contexts. [[Choi2026-bz]] complicates the picture from a cognitive angle, showing that difficulty authenticating one deepfake carries over to shape confidence and vulnerability toward *later* fake content of the *same modality*, implying that disinformation exposure has cumulative, modality-specific psychological residue rather than being a series of independent events. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] then supplies an industrial-scale empirical counterpoint: in coordinated Facebook gambling networks, generative AI did not introduce new persuasive logics so much as intensify and recombine existing ones (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO), producing a documented 13,000%+ post-volume explosion after ChatGPT's launch — evidence that the "unprecedented AI threat" framing found wanting by Hameleers at the level of individual message effects can nonetheless be true at the level of aggregate platform-scale exploitation of regulatory grey zones.

## Scaling deception: coordination, swarms, and industrialized content

A third cluster shifts from message-level effects to the organizational logics of AI-enabled campaigns. [[Orlando2025-ul]] offers a striking mechanistic finding: LLM agents in a simulated platform spontaneously reproduce the coordination signatures of real influence operations — network density, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification — and mere *awareness* of teammates is nearly as effective at generating this coordination as explicit collective deliberation. [[Schroeder2026-im]] takes this simulated possibility and projects it as policy urgency, arguing that agentic "malicious AI swarms" constitute a qualitative leap beyond botnets (persistent memory, real-time adaptive tone, cross-platform presence) and proposing layered governance responses. [[Tang2026-gu]] grounds these abstractions in an on-the-ground labour study: Chinese "AI slop" creators who treat virality itself as an engineerable, benchmarkable production process via matrix-account systems — a vivid, non-Western case of exactly the kind of systematized, throughput-driven content generation that Hackenburg's persuasion research identifies as mechanistically potent. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extends the coordination lens to financial harm, mapping the "scam ecosystem" of platforms, telecoms, and money-laundering networks that deepfake fraud exploits, and arguing — in a way that dovetails with Rauchfleisch's transparency findings — that gatekeeper-level, not individual-level, interventions are the only viable regulatory lever.

## Structural and geopolitical embedding

Two papers push the frame outward from campaigns to political-economic structure. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows that propaganda need not be actively deployed by an AI system to shape its outputs: state-coordinated media, memorized in training corpora, already "launders" pro-regime valence into commercial LLM responses, a subtler and more diffuse mechanism of institutional influence than any of the deliberate campaigns discussed above. [[Beacken2026-zb]] complements this with a comparative corrective, arguing against deterministic narratives of GenAI's uniform transformative power: across six democratically weakened states, adoption and use of generative propaganda are shaped by local political, economic, and social structure rather than following one global trajectory — a useful check on the more universalizing claims of the persuasion and swarm literatures.

## Audience reception and sensemaking

A parallel set of studies relocates agency to the audience, asking how people actually process AI-mediated content rather than how effective it is in the lab. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s large Kenyan field experiment is the pivot point here: it shows deepfake impact is not fixed at production but "socially negotiated" through surrounding comments, with partisanship moderating whether viewers extend or withhold skepticism — a finding that meaningfully complicates the individual-level persuasion models of Hackenburg and Lin by embedding deception effects in a social, collective-sensemaking process. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds a parallel dynamic on TikTok, where generative AI appears as a tool for "speculative worldbuilding" within a broader immigration discourse that is, contrary to expectation, more pro- than anti-immigration — underscoring that platform vernacular and affordances mediate whatever persuasive power AI content might carry. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Suk2026-ai]] turn to reception of AI-generated news specifically: readers rate AI-written and human-written journalism as equivalent in quality, and disclosure produces only a curiosity-driven, short-lived engagement bump rather than durable trust — a micro-level echo of Rauchfleisch's transparency findings — while Suk's theoretical framework argues that trust in generative AI must be studied multilevel (individual, institutional, societal), building on but distinct from legacy media-trust scholarship.

## Epistemological stakes

The two most theoretical papers step back to ask what all of this does to the concept of truth itself. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues that generative outputs fit none of fact-checking's inherited categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) and proposes "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category — plausible, context-dependent, opaque — requiring relational rather than binary evaluation. [[Emilio2026-ik]] radicalizes this concern into the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic, interactive "synthetic realities" proliferate across content, identity, interaction, and institutional layers, societies may rationally begin discounting *all* digital evidence, which paradoxically empowers bad-faith denial more than it protects against deception. Read as the capstone of this topic, these two papers suggest that the persuasion mechanics, visual tactics, coordinated swarms, and structural propaganda documented elsewhere are not merely a set of discrete harms to be mitigated one by one, but symptoms of a deeper erosion of the shared evidentiary ground that fact-checking, journalism, and democratic deliberation all depend on — precisely the "epistemic security" agenda that closes the loop back to the governance proposals of Schroeder and the gatekeeper-liability arguments of Schiffrin.
