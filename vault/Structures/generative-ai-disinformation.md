# Generative AI & Synthetic Disinformation

## The shifting locus of AI-enabled deception

The papers filed here trace an arc from *mechanism* to *manifestation* to *meaning*: how generative AI produces persuasive and deceptive content, how that content circulates and scales in real platform ecosystems, and what it does to the shared epistemic ground disinformation research has long presupposed. Read together, they suggest that the "generative AI and disinformation" problem is less about any single fake artifact than about an emerging infrastructure — persuasive, coordinative, and epistemic — that operates across chatbots, coordinated networks, and the platforms that host them.

## How persuasive is AI, and why

A cluster of large-scale experiments interrogates AI persuasion directly. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] decomposes the "levers" of conversational AI persuasion across 19 LLMs and tens of thousands of UK participants, finding that post-training and prompting strategy matter more than scale, and that *information density* — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims — is the central mechanism, alongside a troubling accuracy trade-off: the same levers that persuade also degrade truthfulness. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, pitting AI against professional canvassers and elite competitive debaters and finding AI reliably out-persuades even coached human experts, with the advantage collapsing only when AI is throttled to human writing speed — again implicating throughput of claims, not rapport or personalization, as the driver. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates this in live electoral contexts (US, Canada, Poland, a Massachusetts ballot measure), showing brief AI dialogues shift votes more than traditional political ads, again via facts and evidence rather than manipulative tactics — though with a partisan asymmetry in claim accuracy that echoes concerns raised elsewhere about biased information environments. Together these three studies substantially discipline the "microtargeting panic" discourse: personalization contributes little; what matters is conversational density of arguable claims. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] then tests the natural governance response — disclosure — and finds that labeling content as AI-generated (the EU AI Act's approach) does almost nothing, whereas disclosing *persuasive intent* roughly halves the effect, reframing transparency policy around what a system is trying to do rather than what it is.

## Coordinated visual and industrial-scale production

A second cluster shifts from dyadic persuasion to networked, visual, and industrialized production. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] documents an exponential post-ChatGPT surge in AI-generated gambling promotion across coordinated Facebook groups, showing generative AI doesn't invent new persuasion drivers so much as intensify and recombine existing ones (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, culturally localized appeals) at industrial scale, exploiting the regulatory gap between paid and organic content. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] generalizes this into a typology of "Deceptive Information Operations" spanning a pro-Putin propaganda network, the same gambling ecosystem, and poorly moderated adult-content groups, arguing that what unites them structurally is not falsity but the convergence of deceptive intent, platform-affordance exploitation, and coordination — a "Zombie Internet" that current CIB-style enforcement frameworks cannot see. [[Tang2026-gu]] documents an adjacent phenomenon in China, where "AI slop" creators formalize virality itself into an engineered, benchmarked production process via matrix account systems, destabilizing conventional notions of authorship. [[Bosch2024-hj]] adds a earlier, lower-tech precedent — TikTok's sonic propaganda around the invasion of Ukraine — underscoring that platform-specific affordances (not just synthetic media per se) have long enabled scaled disinformation, a point [[Hameleers2026-mc]] reinforces experimentally by showing that decontextualized *real* video can be as or more credible than AI-generated images, cautioning against overstating GenAI's novelty relative to cheaper deception techniques.

## Emergent coordination: swarms and agentic operations

[[Orlando2025-ul]] and [[Schroeder2026-im]] push the coordination question into agentic territory. Orlando's generative agent-based modeling shows that LLM agents can spontaneously reproduce real-world information-operation dynamics — network densification, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification — with mere mutual awareness among agents nearly matching explicit collective deliberation. Schroeder's policy forum piece extrapolates this into a governance warning: "malicious AI swarms" fusing LLM reasoning with multi-agent architectures could manufacture consensus, poison training data ("LLM grooming"), and erode institutional trust at a scale and adaptivity beyond prior botnets, arguing that voluntary platform compliance and symmetric "prosocial swarms" are insufficient responses.

## Deception in the wild: fraud, elections, and audience sensemaking

Several papers ground these dynamics in concrete harms. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] surveys the global regulatory patchwork around deepfake financial fraud, arguing that liability must shift from victims to the platform/telecom/financial gatekeepers that structurally enable scam ecosystems. [[Wack2026-bt]], set in Kenya's electoral context, complicates any purely artifact-centric account of deepfake harm: audience impact is not fixed at production but is "collectively sensemade" through surrounding social commentary, moderated by partisanship — a finding that resonates with [[Choi2026-bz]]'s experimental evidence that difficulty authenticating one piece of (deep)fake content carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to shape confidence and vulnerability toward subsequent fakes. [[Beacken2026-zb]] provides comparative ballast against technological determinism, showing that GenAI propaganda adoption across six democratically weakened states is highly uneven, shaped by local political-economic structure rather than following a uniform trajectory — a useful corrective to the more totalizing claims elsewhere in this set.

## Epistemic stakes: what happens to "truth" and trust

A more theoretical cluster asks what generative AI does to factuality and trust as such. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues that conventional fact-checking epistemologies (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot adequately categorize AI outputs and proposes "emergent facts" — plausible, context-dependent, opaque — as a new epistemic category requiring relational rather than binary evaluation. [[Emilio2026-ik]] extends this into a systems-level diagnosis: the real risk is not isolated deepfakes but coherent "synthetic realities" across a content–identity–interaction–institutions stack, producing a paradox whereby ubiquitous synthetic media licenses rational discounting of *all* digital evidence, taxing truth itself. [[Marwick2025-vx]] offers a complementary, ethnographic counterpoint from Far-Right online communities, showing that disinformation's radicalizing power operates less through single dramatic exposures than through processual socialization in which participants marshal "evidence" (including AI-adjacent pseudoscience and memetic content) to construct a self-image of rational skepticism — a reminder that synthetic content's persuasive work is often assimilated into pre-existing communal epistemic practices rather than imposed on passive audiences.

## Structural and institutional backdrop

Finally, several papers situate all of this within broader shifts in the media and political economy. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues that the entire "social media" paradigm is dissolving into post-social formations — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private enclaves, and AI-mediated communication — of which chatbot-mediated disinformation and synthetic content circulation are symptomatic, eroding the shared epistemic infrastructure earlier disinformation scholarship assumed. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits a decade of critical platform scholarship to show that generative AI has intensified, rather than created, pre-existing harms of concentrated power, opaque algorithms, and data extraction, and calls for research to move beyond diagnosis toward intervention. [[Waight2026-ts]] closes the loop between state power and model outputs, demonstrating that state-controlled media is memorized into LLM training data and measurably skews model outputs toward pro-regime framings — an institutional-influence pathway distinct from, but complementary to, the platform- and content-level manipulations documented elsewhere in this collection.

Taken as a whole, the note suggests a field converging on a shared diagnosis: generative AI's disinformation threat lies less in any single deceptive artifact than in the compounding of scalable persuasion (Hackenburg, Lin, Rauchfleisch), industrialized coordination (Giglietto, Giada, Orlando, Schroeder), and a slower erosion of the shared standards by which claims are verified and trusted (Dierickx, Ferrara, Törnberg) — with structural conditions (platform design, state power, local political economy) determining where and how these dynamics take hold.
