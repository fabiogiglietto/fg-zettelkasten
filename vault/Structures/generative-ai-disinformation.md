# Generative AI & Synthetic Media Manipulation

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster of papers traces a shift in how scholars think about generative AI and manipulation: from a narrow concern with *fake artifacts* (deepfaked images, cloned voices) toward a broader concern with *synthetic persuasion as a systemic, infrastructural condition*. Read together, they move from measuring whether AI persuades, to documenting how it coordinates at scale, to asking what happens to public epistemology once synthetic content becomes ambient and cheap.

## How persuasive is conversational AI, and why?

A tight empirical core establishes the mechanics of AI persuasion before worrying about its political effects. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] runs the foundational diagnostic: across 19 LLMs and tens of thousands of participants, persuasiveness is driven far more by post-training (reward modeling) and prompting strategy than by model scale or personalization — and the same levers that boost persuasion (chiefly "information density," the packing-in of fact-checkable claims) degrade factual accuracy, establishing a persuasion–accuracy trade-off that recurs throughout the topic. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, pitting frontier AI against elite human persuaders — professional canvassers, championship debaters — and finding AI reliably wins, again traced to information throughput rather than rapport; tellingly, forcing AI to human typing speed erases its advantage, pinning the mechanism down causally. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates this in real electoral contexts (US, Canada, Poland) and a ballot measure, showing brief AI dialogues outperform traditional political ads via facts-and-evidence rather than psychological manipulation — while also surfacing a partisan asymmetry: AI advocating right-leaning candidates was systematically less accurate, echoing the broader information environment it was trained on.

[[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates the assumption, embedded in much of this literature, that *visual* synthetic content is inherently more dangerous than text: experimentally, AI-generated images are not uniformly more credible or engaging than text, and low-tech decontextualized video can outperform both — suggesting that alarm about generative AI specifically may be somewhat overstated relative to older manipulation techniques, a useful corrective to the swarm- and slop-focused papers that follow.

## From messages to coordinated, agentic campaigns

A second strand scales the question from single persuasive messages to coordinated operations. [[Schroeder2026-im]] offers the conceptual leap: fusing LLM reasoning with multi-agent architectures creates "malicious AI swarms" capable of persistent, adaptive, cross-platform infiltration — manufacturing consensus, poisoning training data ("LLM grooming"), and eroding institutional trust in ways that qualitatively exceed earlier botnets. [[Orlando2025-ul]] operationalizes this empirically via generative agent-based modeling, showing that LLM agents *spontaneously* reproduce real-world coordination signatures (network densification, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification) once given mere awareness of teammates — no central orchestration required, a finding that validates Schroeder's swarm concern from the bottom up. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] grounds this in an observed, non-simulated case: coordinated Facebook gambling networks show an exponential, structurally-broken rise in posting volume dated to ChatGPT's release, with generative AI not replacing but intensifying existing persuasive visual drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO) at industrial scale — exploiting Meta's governance gap between paid and organic content.

## Political deployment: ideology, geography, and virality as craft

A third cluster asks how this capacity is actually taken up across political and cultural contexts, resisting technological determinism. [[Beacken2026-zb]] sets the comparative baseline: across six democratically weakened states, GenAI propaganda use is shaped by local structural conditions rather than following one inevitable trajectory, pushing back against universalizing claims found elsewhere in the topic. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Topinka2026-hb]] both examine far-right deployment but from different angles — the former documents the empirical convergence of populist communication style and AI imagery on Instagram/X in the 2024 European elections, while Topinka theorizes the underlying mechanism: "slopaganda" doesn't deceive so much as emotionally *lure*, functioning as an "ideological attunement machine" optimized by alignment/RLHF to resonate with ambient feed affect rather than persuade via argument — a direct complication of the fact-density persuasion model from Hackenburg and Lin. [[Crichton2026-kn]] extends this structurally: commercial image models, when used to "regenerate" extremist imagery, sanitize explicit markers while preserving ideological content as ambient in-group cues — evidence that far-right affinity with generative AI is architectural, not incidental. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] offers a counterpoint from immigration discourse on Canadian TikTok, where generative AI appears only as a minor "speculative worldbuilding" tool within an otherwise pro-immigration-skewed platform vernacular — a reminder that AI-driven manipulation competes with, rather than simply overwhelms, organic platform dynamics. [[Tang2026-gu]] reframes the production side entirely: in China, "AI slop" creators treat virality itself as an engineerable output via benchmarking and matrix-account systems, recasting creative labor and authorship rather than political persuasion per se, and thereby bridging this topic to platform labor studies.

## Reception: audiences as co-producers of deepfake meaning

[[Wack2026-bt]] shifts the lens from producers to audiences, showing in a large Kenyan survey experiment that deepfake impact is not fixed at the moment of production but socially negotiated through surrounding comments — credulous framing amplifies harm, incredulous framing restores support, moderated by partisanship. This "collective sensemaking" finding complicates the swarm and slopaganda papers' assumption of a passive audience, suggesting manipulation campaigns succeed or fail partly through downstream peer interpretation rather than content alone.

## Infrastructural and epistemic consequences

The final cluster pulls back to ask what synthetic persuasion does to media ecosystems and truth itself. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues the "social media" paradigm is dissolving under three pressures — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private retreat, and AI-mediated communication — with generative AI severing platforms from human content production entirely ("platforms without users"), a frame that contextualizes why swarms, slop, and coordinated campaigns proliferate so easily in engagement-optimized systems with no incentive to distinguish synthetic from human content. [[Emilio2026-ik]] names the resulting stakes directly: GenAI risk is not isolated deepfakes but "synthetic realities" — fabricated content, identity, interaction, and institutions jointly — producing a paradox in which societies rationally discount all digital evidence, raising the cost of truth. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] responds at the epistemological level, arguing traditional fact-checking categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot capture AI outputs and proposing "emergent facts" as a new category requiring relational, rather than binary, verification. [[Waight2026-ts]] closes the loop by showing that institutional power already shapes this epistemic terrain upstream, before any user ever queries a model: state-coordinated media is memorized into training data and "laundered" into ostensibly neutral LLM outputs, demonstrating that the manipulation this topic traces is not only a downstream capability problem but a training-data-level one.

## Where the money is

Finally, [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] translates these dynamics into policy stakes, mapping the "scam ecosystem" of AI-driven financial fraud across platforms, telecoms, and financial institutions, and arguing — consonant with Törnberg's and Schroeder's calls for incentive-based governance — that liability must shift from individual victims to gatekeeper infrastructures, since labeling and detection alone (as Emilio and Crichton also show) cannot keep pace with generative capability.

Together, these papers chart a field moving from "can AI persuade?" through "how does it coordinate and circulate?" to "what does its ambient presence do to collective knowledge and trust" — with recurring tensions between fact-based and affect-based persuasion, between deception and resonance, and between technological capability and the structural, political, and economic conditions that determine whether and how it is actually weaponized.
