# Generative AI and Synthetic Media Disinformation

## The reconfiguration of the information ecosystem

A first cluster of papers argues that generative AI is not simply adding a new deception tool to an otherwise stable media environment but is helping to dissolve the "social media" paradigm itself. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] makes the strongest version of this claim, arguing that algorithmic recommendation, user retreat into semi-private spaces, and AI-mediated communication are together producing "post-social" formations — including "platforms without users" — that require new analytic vocabulary. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits an older diagnosis of social media's structural pathologies (wealth concentration, opaque algorithms, precarious labor, data extraction) and shows how generative AI has intensified each of them, particularly through non-consensual scraping of user content for training and a resulting "crisis of authenticity." [[Nguyen2026-vm]] complements this macro view from the supply side of discourse, showing that news media's own framing of LLMs — often mentalistic and agentic — helps construct the public imaginary within which disinformation debates unfold, while [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] documents an adjacent discursive struggle over Google's AI Overviews, where platform, journalist, and marketer framings compete to define generative AI's epistemic authority over search itself. Together these papers set the stage: before asking how GenAI is weaponized for deception, they show how it is reshaping the very infrastructure and vocabulary of public communication.

## Epistemology: what counts as a fact when content is synthetic

A second strand tackles the conceptual problem head-on: if content is generated rather than retrieved, what does "truth" even mean? [[Dierickx2026-tw]] proposes "emergent facts" as a fourth epistemic category beyond evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based facts, arguing that fact-checking itself needs new criteria (accuracy, verifiability, contextual relevance, consistency) for GenAI outputs. [[Emilio2026-ik]] extends this into a systems-level argument, formalizing "synthetic realities" as a four-layer stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and naming the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic content proliferates, rational actors may discount all digital evidence, producing an epistemic tax that benefits strategic deniers. [[Choi2026-bz]] grounds these abstract claims empirically at the level of audience psychology, showing that difficulty authenticating one deepfake carries over — in a modality-congruent way — to reduce discernment and confidence when facing subsequent fake content. Read together, these three papers trace a throughline from philosophical reframing, to systemic diagnosis, to measurable psychological consequence.

## Persuasion and propaganda: mechanisms and actors

The largest cluster examines how GenAI is actually deployed to persuade or manipulate, and by whom. At the level of mechanism, [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Lin2025-xp]] provide large-scale experimental evidence that conversational AI's persuasive power stems less from personalization or scale than from information density and post-training/prompting choices — with Hackenburg documenting a troubling persuasion–accuracy trade-off, and Lin showing this translates into real shifts in voter preference across four elections, with a partisan asymmetry in factual accuracy favoring left-leaning framings. These findings complicate alarmist assumptions elsewhere in the literature by showing AI persuasion working largely through legitimate argumentation rather than exotic manipulation.

At the level of institutional and state actors, [[Waight2026-ts]] shows that authoritarian media control is already "laundered" into LLM outputs via training data, producing systematically more pro-regime responses in lower-media-freedom languages — a subtler, infrastructural form of the digital authoritarianism that [[Beacken2026-zb]] examines comparatively across six democratically weakened countries, arguing against deterministic narratives of uniform AI impact in favor of context-sensitive analysis of how elites actually adopt GenAI propaganda. At the level of coordinated inauthentic behavior, [[Orlando2025-ul]] models how networked LLM agents spontaneously reproduce information-operation coordination patterns (network density, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification) even without explicit deliberation, while [[Schroeder2026-im]] extrapolates this into a policy-forum warning about "malicious AI swarms" capable of manufacturing consensus and poisoning training data via "LLM grooming," proposing layered governance responses. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] offers a striking applied case of these dynamics: coordinated Facebook gambling networks whose post volume exploded after ChatGPT's launch, using generative AI to intensify (not replace) established persuasion drivers and exploit regulatory gaps between paid and organic content.

## Platform case studies and audience reception

A set of papers grounds these dynamics in specific platforms and content forms. [[Triedman2025-uy]] dissects Grokipedia as a test case of AI-generated knowledge production, finding it heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet skewed toward unreliable and blacklisted sources on controversial topics — a concrete instance of the "emergent facts" problem theorized elsewhere. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] examines TikTok's immigration discourse, finding pro-immigration content dominant but noting generative AI's emerging use for racist "speculative worldbuilding," while [[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates assumptions about visual disinformation's inherent power, showing AI-generated images do not universally outperform text or decontextualized video — effects are issue-bound and dependent on the availability of plausible authentic footage. On the reception side, [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that audiences rate AI-generated and human-written news as equivalent in quality pre-disclosure, and that disclosure boosts short-term engagement (likely curiosity) without building durable trust in AI journalism — a finding that qualifies both techno-optimist and alarmist expectations about AI-generated content's persuasive reach.

## Financial harms and countermeasures

Beyond political disinformation, [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extends the topic into deepfake-enabled financial fraud, mapping a cross-border "scam ecosystem" of platforms, telecoms, and financial institutions, and arguing for gatekeeper-focused liability regimes rather than victim-blaming approaches — a governance logic that echoes Schroeder's and Ferrara's calls for systemic, infrastructural interventions rather than artifact-level detection. On the detection and mitigation side, [[Unknown2025-qj]] examines red-teaming as a public-interest practice for surfacing generative AI harms, reframing a cybersecurity-derived method as a sociotechnical, participatory mechanism of accountability — pointing toward the practical countermeasures that the more diagnostic papers in this collection (Ferrara, Schroeder) gesture toward but do not themselves operationalize.

## The arc of inquiry

Read as a whole, this set of papers moves from diagnosing a structural shift in the media environment (Törnberg, Baym, Nguyen), through conceptualizing the epistemological rupture GenAI introduces (Dierickx, Ferrara, Choi), into empirical dissection of persuasion mechanisms and actors — individual, institutional, and swarm-like (Hackenburg, Lin, Waight, Beacken, Orlando, Schroeder, Giglietto) — grounded in concrete platform cases (Triedman, Hollingshead, Hameleers, Gilardi, Weinbrand) and extending into adjacent financial harms and governance responses (Schiffrin, Unknown2025-qj). The throughline connects the *IA Generativa e Media* course's core concern: not whether AI can produce convincing fakes, but how its integration into existing communicative, economic, and institutional infrastructures is reshaping who can manufacture belief, at what scale, and with what possibility of detection or repair.
