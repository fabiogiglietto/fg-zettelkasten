# Italian Elections & News Ecosystem (MINE)

## Foundations: hybrid media and the polarized-pluralist baseline

The programme's intellectual scaffolding is laid by [[Hallin2004-kg]], whose three-models framework — and especially the "Polarized Pluralist" category into which Italy is conventionally slotted — supplies the comparative vocabulary that recurs across almost every subsequent paper: political parallelism, instrumentalization, weak professionalization. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] applies this lens empirically to the 2013 general election, showing that Twitter's "second screen" during talk shows reproduces rather than disrupts television's agenda-setting logic — participation is narrow, dominated by opinion and interaction requests rather than genuine agenda-building. This early finding — that networked media extend, rather than replace, legacy political-media relationships — anticipates a theme that persists through the corpus: new platforms do not dissolve parallelism, they relocate it.

## From Likes to coordination: tracing manipulation across Italian campaigns

[[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]] marks the programme's starting point in the "big social data" forecasting literature, testing whether Facebook Likes predict the 2011 administrative election outcomes. Its null result on vote-share prediction, paired with a suggestive signal for *candidate viability*, sets up a methodological pivot that defines the next decade of work: rather than treating social media metrics as a direct proxy for public opinion, the MINE line shifts toward *behavioural* diagnostics of media-political manipulation. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] formalizes this by introducing "insularity" as a measure of partisan news ecologies during the 2018 campaign, showing that the Five Star Movement's supporters uniquely weaponize the shares/comments ratio to amplify favorable and contest unfavorable coverage — evidence that populist "cyber parties" can engineer platform engagement strategically. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] then operationalizes "coordinated link sharing behavior" (CLSB) as a replicable, action-based alternative to content- or actor-based disinformation detection, validating it against the 2018 and 2019 Italian campaigns and showing coordinated networks disproportionately spread problematic domains. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this infrastructure to the Covid-19 infodemic, tracing how "Alternative Influence Networks" and an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster recycle legacy-media appearances through remediation, underscoring that even ostensibly anti-system actors remain parasitic on mainstream media attention. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] closes this arc by turning the static CLSB list into a dynamic monitoring workflow, applied to the 2022 snap election to surface an M5S hyperpartisan network, a religious clickbait operation, and a proselytizing bot network — demonstrating that coordinated-behavior detection must itself evolve continuously to keep pace with adapting actors.

## Scaling up: LLMs, embeddings, and the methodological turn

A second cluster treats the 2018/2022 Italian election corpora as a testbed for computational method development. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks OpenAI embeddings against the Italian-specific UmBERTo model for clustering political news, finding the general-purpose LLM embedding consistently superior — a finding operationalized in [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]'s "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline, which chains fine-tuned classification, embedding-based clustering, and LLM-generated cluster labels, while confronting the harder problem of validating outputs that no longer map cleanly onto fixed-category content analysis. [[Paci2025-ag]] stress-tests the limits of this LLM turn directly, showing that even strong models fail to recover the implicatures and presuppositions embedded in Italian political speech — a sobering counterweight suggesting that while LLMs are powerful for retrieval, clustering, and labeling tasks, they remain unreliable interpreters of the pragmatic subtlety that is precisely what makes political discourse manipulative. Read together, these three papers trace a methodological arc from enthusiastic adoption to calibrated skepticism about what generative AI can responsibly automate in this research line, a tension echoed in [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]]'s more optimistic finding (in a German rather than Italian context) that GPT-4o substantially outperforms specialized computer-vision pipelines for visual political communication — suggesting multimodal competence may outpace purely textual pragmatic competence.

## Platform governance, visibility, and the DSA data-access regime

A third strand turns from content analysis to the platforms themselves as objects of study, driven by the collapse of CrowdTangle and the emergence of DSA-mandated data infrastructures. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s US newsfeed-quality study to Germany, France, and Italy, finding untrustworthy-URL shares spike in Italian and German election years even as exposure (Views) stays comparatively stable — a first hint that Meta's internal curation dampens the translation of partisan supply into reach. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] makes this dampening mechanism explicit at scale in the US context, showing partisan content faces a systematic "reach penalty" and quality journalism a "reach reward," both of which intensify around the 2020 "break the glass" interventions — evidence that Facebook is an active curator, not a neutral conduit. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] brings this argument home to Italy, documenting that Meta's political-content-reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' Facebook reach by 72% roughly ten months before its announced global rollout, while extremist accounts compensated through posting volume — a case study in both platform opacity and the transparency gains (and gaps) of the new Meta Content Library. [[McNally2025-dn]] complements this with a longitudinal, Guardian-focused audit showing hard news (but not soft content) bears the lagged imprint of algorithmic ranking changes, arguing the "black box" metaphor obscures a detectable sociotechnical system. These empirical interventions are framed by a cluster of infrastructure-and-policy papers: [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys the new DSA-mandated APIs and portals and their uneven eligibility rules; [[Lukito2026-nb]] catalogues 72 data-access tools around a "price, proficiency, or permission" typology, diagnosing the systemic underrepresentation of consumption-side data; and [[Schulte2026-df]] uses the 2025 German election to show that platform-observability bottlenecks are themselves the chief obstacle to multi-platform electoral research, arguing for treating access as reusable research infrastructure rather than a one-off scraping exercise.

## Beyond Italy: comparative parallelism and cross-national diffusion

Running alongside the Italian core is a comparative arm testing whether parallelism- and visibility-related dynamics generalize. [[Balluff2026-bv]] uncovers a rare documentable instance of media capture through government advertising in Austria's "Inseratenaffäre," showing a politician's visibility — not favorability — surges in a captured outlet, with the capture mechanism instead displacing negativity onto competitors. [[Iris2026-pg]] scales the visibility question to five countries' 2024 EP election coverage, finding structural overrepresentation of the Radical Right regardless of outlet orientation, intensifying in the campaign's final weeks. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] examines Scandinavian Facebook reactions, finding populist parties uniquely skilled at eliciting "Angry" reactions that, unlike "Love," reliably drive sharing. [[Oprea2025-lf]] documents inauthentic hyperactive-user amplification across all major Romanian parties' pages during the same EP cycle, while [[Lukito2026-il]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] push the comparative lens toward the US and Germany respectively — the former showing right-leaning outlets curate distinct content for Twitter versus Truth Social, the latter showing German alternative and legacy media share largely the same named-entity universe but organize it into more cohesive, self-referential network structures. Together these papers test, largely outside Italy, whether the mechanisms of visibility bias, affective amplification, and coordinated inauthenticity first isolated in the Italian corpus are general features of contemporary electoral media ecosystems rather than national idiosyncrasies.

## Open threads: rhetoric, trust, and the limits of visibility research

Two more exploratory contributions point toward where the line of inquiry is heading next. [[Mosca2026-yh]] reframes "fake news" accusations themselves as a rhetorical weapon of negative campaigning in the Italian press, proposing a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model that complements the coordinated-behavior and media-capture strands by treating delegitimization discourse as a form of parallelism in its own right. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] shifts attention from production to reception, finding Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-involved news — a finding that, read against the programme's earlier skepticism about LLM pragmatic competence ([[Paci2025-ag]]), raises the question of whether audience trust is tracking or diverging from actual model reliability. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]], on health-news gatekeeping by Google News Italia, stands as an adjacent case showing the same production/engagement mismatch (scientific topics dominate supply, emotional topics dominate engagement) that recurs throughout the political corpus, suggesting the MINE programme's core finding — that visibility, engagement, and trust diverge from editorial intention and electoral reality in structurally predictable ways — extends well beyond the strictly political news it was built to explain.
