# Italian Political Communication & Elections

## From hybrid media to platform-native coordination

The Italian strand of this programme begins before the current era of API-driven computational methods, with [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s study of the 2013 general election as a "hybrid" contest between television talk shows and Twitter. Its finding — that only a narrow slice of the public engaged in the transactions between broadcast and social media, and that politicians and broadcasters largely failed to activate connected audiences — establishes a baseline claim that recurs throughout the programme: visibility and engagement on social platforms are structurally uneven, concentrated among a small set of actors and audiences rather than evenly distributed across the political field. The leap from this broadcast-centred hybridity to a platform-native understanding of Italian elections comes with [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], which uses the 2018 campaign to show that populist parties' news ecosystems are more "insular" on Twitter and that insularity predicts a Facebook engagement signature (more sharing, less commenting) that amplifies favourable coverage of the Five Star Movement specifically. This paper effectively relocates the Hallin-and-Mancini "polarized pluralist" diagnosis of the Italian media system into the attention economy of social platforms, setting the empirical and theoretical terms — insularity, partisan amplification, populist cyber-infrastructure — for everything that follows.

## Coordination as the unit of analysis: hyperpartisan networks across two elections

The programme's methodological signature is the shift from content- or actor-based detection of disinformation to *behavioural* detection of coordination, articulated first in [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]]'s study of coordinated link sharing around the 2018 general election and 2019 European election. Rather than asking whether a given domain is "fake," the paper asks which networks of pages, groups, and profiles share the same URLs anomalously fast and often, finding that such coordinated entities are dramatically more likely to spread problematic content and that the balance of openly political versus deceptively non-political actors in a network shapes its sharing strategy. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends this logic to the 2022 snap election with a workflow for *continuously updating* lists of coordinated accounts rather than relying on static blacklists, and in doing so surfaces three qualitatively different hyperpartisan operations — a Five Star Movement-aligned echo chamber fabricating favourable polls, a click-economy network exploiting religious pages for political clickbait, and a transnational religious-proselytism network using Messenger bots. Read together, these two papers chart an evolution from "finding problematic content" to "finding problematic infrastructure," and they establish that Italy's two post-2018 elections are not a single snapshot but a comparable pair of observations in a longitudinal design — a design made explicit when the programme later turns its unsupervised NLP pipelines on both election corpora simultaneously. Outside the Italian corpus proper, [[Nizzoli2020-cf]]'s treatment of the 2019 UK election offers a methodological counterpoint: it argues that coordination exists on a continuum rather than a binary, and that the *most* coordinated communities are often small activist fringes rather than mainstream party machines — a nuance that complicates, without contradicting, the Italian finding that coordination correlates with problematic amplification.

## From CrowdTangle to LLMs-in-the-loop: retooling the pipeline

A second arc concerns method itself. As CrowdTangle-based detection matured, the programme confronted the need to classify, cluster, and label hundreds of thousands of Italian-language political URLs shared across both the 2018 and 2022 campaigns — a task for which [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks OpenAI's `text-embedding-3-large` against the Italian-specific UmBERTo model, finding the LLM-based embeddings consistently superior for producing semantically coherent clusters in both election years. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] builds this result into a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline — fine-tuned classification, LLM-generated embeddings, LLM-generated cluster labels — and confronts the harder problem of *validating* such a pipeline, arguing that crowdsourced annotation is inadequate once models outperform low-skilled human coders and that validation protocols must be tailored to each stage and to the variable granularity of LLM-generated narratives. This methodological self-scrutiny resonates with [[Paci2025-ag]]'s more skeptical finding, using the IMPAQTS corpus of Italian political speech, that even strong LLMs fall well short of expert human interpretation when asked to unpack implicatures and presuppositions — a caution against over-trusting LLM outputs for the subtler pragmatic dimensions of political discourse that the clustering and labelling pipelines mostly bracket. The broader field is moving in the same direction: [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows that simply asking an LLM to place a text on an ideological dimension and averaging many such judgments can replicate established scaling benchmarks across languages, while [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] demonstrates an analogous leap for *visual* political communication, where GPT-4o substantially outperforms specialized computer-vision pipelines for recognizing candidates and counting people in Instagram imagery. Together these papers suggest the Italian programme's pivot to LLM-based clustering is part of a wider reconfiguration of computational political communication methods, one in which generative models increasingly substitute for both bespoke classifiers and human coders — with all the validation anxieties [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] and [[Paci2025-ag]] make explicit.

## Platform governance, transparency, and parliamentary visibility

The most directly consequential development for this topic is the move from *describing* coordinated or partisan dynamics to *auditing platform governance itself*. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses over 2.5 million posts from the Meta Content Library to show that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach by 72% at its trough, that this suppression began roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout, and — most strikingly — that extremist accounts evaded the per-post penalty by multiplying their posting volume, ending up with *greater* aggregate reach than before the policy. This finding of policy-induced, asymmetric redistribution of visibility across the Italian political spectrum is a natural extension of the coordination studies: where [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] and [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] showed how partisan networks manufacture visibility from below, [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] shows how platform policy reshapes visibility from above, and exposes a transparency gap in Meta's own DSA compliance reporting. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], working on US data but methodologically continuous with this agenda, generalizes the claim that Facebook is an active curator rather than a neutral conduit, showing that partisan audiences incur a "reach penalty" while journalistically high-quality sources receive a reach premium, and that both effects fluctuate with known governance interventions such as the 2020 "break the glass" measures — evidence that algorithmic curation is a dynamic, crisis-responsive process rather than a stable background condition. [[McNally2025-dn]] arrives at a structurally similar conclusion for a different outlet and timeframe, finding that Facebook's News Feed algorithm changes produced detectable, lagged effects specifically on hard-news engagement, and using this as an argument for systemic algorithmic auditing under the DSA. [[Philipp2026-tl]] frames the regulatory context these audits depend on, surveying how DSA-mandated APIs and data portals have partially reopened — unevenly and incompletely — the platform data access that had been curtailed since the Cambridge Analytica era, a framing that situates [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]'s explicit use of the Meta Content Library as a case study in what DSA-enabled research can and cannot yet achieve. The programme's own theoretical throat-clearing for this governance turn appears early, in [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]], which anticipates many of these later empirical findings by arguing that blurred organizational boundaries, opaque micro-targeting, and strategic amplification jointly undermine traditional exposure-measurement methods and demand exactly the kind of platform-level, longitudinal auditing that the later papers deliver.

## Rhetoric, trust, and the adjacent information ecosystem

A smaller but connected cluster examines how falsity and trust are rhetorically and perceptually constructed around Italian political communication. [[Mosca2026-yh]] reframes "fake news" accusations themselves as a rhetorical weapon of negative campaigning, proposing a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model in which political actors wield fake-news charges opportunistically and newspapers amplify them unevenly — a framing that complements the coordination papers' concern with manufactured visibility by showing how *delegitimization* can be equally strategic and equally amplifiable. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] turns to the audience side of the ecosystem, finding that Italian adults trust AI-generated search results more than AI-involved journalism, a result that bears on how the "gatekeeping" function studied in [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] — where Google News Italia's aggregation choices on health topics shape downstream Facebook engagement, with celebrity and scandal content outperforming technical science news — may be evolving as generative AI intermediaries enter the chain between source, aggregator, and audience. Both papers extend the programme's long-standing interest in gatekeeping and amplification (first seen in the insularity/amplification dynamics of [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]) into adjacent domains — health communication and AI-mediated search — that sit at the edges of electoral communication proper but share its core analytic vocabulary.

## Italy in comparative perspective

Although the MINE programme's empirical center of gravity is Italian, it is explicitly in dialogue with a wider European and transatlantic literature that tests whether its findings travel. [[Darius2026-xl]]'s cross-platform analysis of all 27 EU member states during the 2024 European Parliament election finds that populist radical-right parties enjoy outsized audience *support* (if not activity) relative to mainstream competitors, especially on TikTok — a finding structurally analogous to the Five Star Movement's amplification advantage documented in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], now generalized across platforms and countries. [[Iris2026-pg]] complements this with evidence that radical-right actors also receive disproportionate *media* (not just audience) visibility in the run-up to the same election, suggesting that the amplification dynamics the Italian studies locate on Facebook and Twitter have analogues in conventional news salience. Elsewhere, Scandinavian and other national case studies — [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] on Love/Angry reactions and sharing in Norway, Sweden, and Denmark, [[Larsson2026-ro]] on a decade of negative-sentiment drift in the Norwegian Progress Party's Facebook communication, [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review of Facebook reactions research, and [[Oprea2025-lf]]'s documentation of hyperactive inauthentic accounts amplifying Romanian party pages during the 2024 EP campaign — collectively corroborate the Italian programme's central intuitions about negativity, anger, and coordinated inauthenticity as drivers of platform visibility, while extending them to multiparty and Eastern European contexts the Italian case alone cannot address. Methodologically adjacent infrastructure papers, such as [[Schulte2026-df]]'s audit of the bottlenecks in multi-platform German election research and [[Balluff2026-bv]]'s quasi-experimental detection of covert media capture in Austria, extend the governance-and-transparency concerns of [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] to other national settings, reinforcing the view that the data-access and methodological problems first worked through on Italian Facebook data are now a shared, continent-wide research agenda. Finally, studies further afield — [[Lukito2026-il]] on cross-platform curation by US right-leaning outlets, [[Much2026-gu]] on gendered podcast ecosystems and the 2024 US youth gender gap — mark the outer boundary of the topic, illustrating how the coordination-and-amplification toolkit pioneered on the Italian 2018/2022 elections is being adapted to entirely new platforms (TikTok, Truth Social, podcasts) and entirely new axes of political sorting, even as the core Italian trajectory — from TV/Twitter hybridity, through coordinated hyperpartisan networks, to LLM-assisted pipelines and platform-policy audits — remains the programme's most sustained and longitudinally coherent line of argument.
